Mycoremediation of Textile Dye Effluent in India: A Scoping Review of the Gap Between Laboratory Evidence and Field Deployment

Fungal enzymes can break down the azo dyes that make Indian textile effluent so persistent. Almost none of that research has left the lab bench for the dyeing clusters producing the pollution.

Abstract

India's textile dyeing industry, concentrated in a small number of large clusters, is one of the country's most significant sources of industrial water pollution, releasing synthetic azo dyes that conventional physico-chemical treatment struggles to fully remove. A substantial body of laboratory research, much of it conducted at Indian institutions, has demonstrated that filamentous fungi and their extracellular enzymes, chiefly laccases and peroxidases, can decolorize and partially mineralize these same dyes at high efficiency. This scoping review characterizes that research literature by stage of development (fungal isolation and screening, lab-scale reactor trials, and field or industrial deployment) and sets it against the treatment infrastructure actually operating in India's major dyeing clusters.

The review finds a consistent pattern: dozens of studies demonstrate fungal decolorization efficiencies exceeding 90 percent at flask or small-reactor scale, including work using real, uncontrolled textile effluent rather than synthetic dye solutions. No published evidence was found of fungal or enzymatic dye-degradation technology operating at field or industrial scale within an Indian textile cluster. Instead, the treatment infrastructure built into government-funded Zero Liquid Discharge systems, representing hundreds of crores of public investment, relies entirely on physico-chemical and reverse-osmosis-based technology with generic bacterial activated-sludge treatment, not fungal or enzymatic systems purpose-built for dye degradation.

The discussion traces this gap to a combination of technical barriers (enzyme production cost, instability under real industrial conditions, and the toxicity of synthetic mediators used to boost laccase activity), regulatory barriers (the absence of an approval pathway for biological dye-treatment technology, and court-mandated deadlines that were met using conventional technology before biological alternatives had matured), and an institutional one: capital already sunk into reverse-osmosis infrastructure creates inertia against adopting an unproven alternative, however promising the underlying science. To move past a qualitative account of that gap, this review also builds an original scale-up feasibility model, extrapolating the most field-relevant published dataset (an IIT Delhi engineered laccase system, tested in continuous flow on real effluent) to a defined target of 530 cubic metres per day, one percent of Tirupur's combined CETP capacity. The model finds that the required reactor volume, while roughly 440,000 times larger than what has been tested, is not the binding constraint. Enzyme cost is: at current laboratory-demonstrated dosages and commercial enzyme prices, the modelled treatment cost of approximately ₹28,500 per cubic metre runs roughly 140 to 1,900 times higher than the ₹15 to ₹200 per cubic metre Indian CETPs already pay using conventional technology, reframing the lab-to-field gap as primarily an enzyme-economics problem rather than a scale-up engineering one.

1. Introduction

Textile dyeing and finishing account for an estimated 17 to 20 percent of industrial water pollution worldwide, and azo dyes, the class most commonly used in the industry, make up roughly 80 percent of all commercial dyes produced globally.13 Their molecular structure, built around a stable nitrogen-nitrogen double bond, is precisely what makes them commercially useful (colourfast, resistant to fading) and environmentally persistent (resistant to breakdown). India hosts several of the world's largest textile dyeing clusters, including Tirupur in Tamil Nadu, which alone contributes more than half of India's knitwear exports and has, at various points, run 760 registered dyeing units.14 The clusters at Jodhpur in Rajasthan and Panipat in Haryana carry a similar concentration of dyeing and printing capacity in water-scarce regions.17

The ecological cost of this concentration is well documented. Untreated or under-treated effluent from Tirupur's dyeing units led to a 2011 Madras High Court order closing all units found in violation of pollution norms, following documented degradation of the Noyyal River and surrounding farmland.14 A similar pattern has played out along Rajasthan's Jojari River near Balotra, where textile effluent has been linked to farmland turning barren and displacement of agricultural communities.17

Fungal bioremediation of dye effluent, mycoremediation, has been studied as an alternative or complement to conventional treatment for several decades. Certain fungi, most notably white-rot species, secrete extracellular oxidative enzymes, laccases, lignin peroxidases, and manganese peroxidases, evolved originally to break down lignin, the structurally similar aromatic polymer that makes wood rigid. These same enzymes act on the aromatic azo bonds in synthetic dyes, and a substantial published literature, including work from Indian institutions, demonstrates decolorization efficiencies often exceeding 90 percent under laboratory conditions.910 This review asks a narrower and more practical question than most of that literature does: how much of this laboratory-demonstrated capability has actually reached the dyeing clusters generating India's textile effluent, and what is standing in the way.

2. Methods

This is a scoping review rather than a formal systematic review conducted under a PRISMA protocol. The distinction matters and is stated plainly: the aim here is not to exhaustively enumerate every study on fungal dye degradation, but to characterize the literature by stage of technological maturity and compare it against documented, deployed treatment infrastructure in India's major textile clusters. This approach follows the same evidentiary logic used elsewhere on this site: characterizing a body of existing material against a shared framework rather than generating new primary data.

2.1 Literature Search and Classification

Studies were identified through iterative searches of PubMed, Nature/Scientific Reports, ScienceDirect, Springer Link, and indexed institutional repositories (Bioresources and Bioprocessing, AMB Express, Frontiers in Microbiology, Environmental Sciences Europe, Chemosphere), using combinations of terms covering fungal species names, decolorization, mycoremediation, laccase and peroxidase enzymes, azo dye degradation, and textile effluent. Search terms were run in successive rounds, each round narrowed by the results of the previous one: general terms first ("mycoremediation textile dye effluent India"), then organism-specific terms once candidate species appeared in early results (for example, "Aspergillus bombycis textile dye" or "Geotrichum candidum textile effluent"), then infrastructure-specific terms to characterize deployed treatment technology ("Tirupur CETP Zero Liquid Discharge technology").

A study was treated as India-relevant if it met at least one of three criteria: the fungal strain was isolated from an Indian contamination site (with the site named), the authors' institutional affiliation was Indian, or the study explicitly addressed an Indian regulatory or industrial context. Nine primary studies met this bar and are discussed below; a further set of review articles and non-Indian primary studies were used only for corroborating context, not counted toward the three-tier classification. Studies were classified into three stages: isolation and screening (fungal strains identified and tested against dye solutions at plate or flask scale, typically under 100 mL), lab-scale reactor (testing in a designed bioreactor system, whether batch or continuous, typically under 1 litre), and field or industrial deployment (any application at or connected to an actual operating textile dyeing facility or common effluent treatment plant). Within the reactor tier, each study was further flagged for whether it tested real, uncontrolled textile effluent or a synthetic dye solution prepared at a fixed concentration, since this distinction matters directly for field relevance and is not always foregrounded in the studies' own abstracts.

2.2 Infrastructure Comparison

To characterize what has actually been deployed, publicly available government and industry documentation was reviewed for three major clusters (Tirupur, Jodhpur, and Panipat), covering funded Common Effluent Treatment Plant (CETP) projects, the technology components specified in those projects, and any reference to biological or enzymatic dye-specific treatment as opposed to generic activated-sludge biological oxidation used for broader organic load reduction. Central Pollution Control Board assessment reports, Tamil Nadu Department of Textiles project documentation, and Tirupur Water Infrastructure Company (TWIC) technical summaries were the primary sources for this comparison.

3. Results

3.1 Isolation and Screening: A Deep and Active Literature, Traceable to Named Indian Contamination Sites

The isolation and screening tier is the largest and most active, and unlike much of the global mycoremediation literature, several Indian studies trace their fungal strains to specifically named, documented contamination sites rather than generic "textile effluent" sourcing. Aspergillus bombycis was isolated from dye-contaminated soil at the Gujarat Industrial Development Corporation estate in Vatva, Ahmedabad, a documented dye-industry pollution site.4 Four further isolates, Trichoderma virens, Phlebiopsis cf. ravenelii, Talaromyces stipitatus, and Aspergillus niger, were recovered from textile dye-contaminated soil near Meerut, Uttar Pradesh, screened from a pool of 74 isolates down to four candidates on the basis of decolorization performance against five synthetic azo dyes.6 Pleurotus sajor caju, tested by the Applied Microbiology Lab at IIT Delhi's Centre for Rural Development and Technology, showed laccase-driven decolorization of both Reactive Blue 13 and Reactive Black 5, two dyes common in Indian textile effluent, with the study going further than most by comparing whole-organism (growing biomass) decolorization against purified laccase-mediated decolorization directly, and confirming the degradation pathway of Reactive Blue 13 using FTIR, HPLC, and GC-MS.5

This tier of research also extends to heavy metal contamination, a different pollutant class but evidence of the same underlying capacity: fungal isolates collected from contaminated soil sites in Hisar and Panipat, Haryana, demonstrated meaningful chromium and arsenic removal capacity.78 Taken together, these studies indicate that the specific expertise needed for this work, isolating functional fungal strains from real Indian contamination sites and characterizing their degradative capacity, already exists across at least four Indian states (Gujarat, Uttar Pradesh, Haryana, and Delhi), even where it hasn't yet been directed at an operating field problem.

3.2 Lab-Scale Reactor Trials: Fewer, and a Real-Effluent / Synthetic-Solution Split

A smaller number of studies progressed to a designed reactor system, and applying the real-effluent-versus-synthetic-solution distinction described in Methods narrows the field further. Two Indian studies used real, uncontrolled textile effluent rather than a prepared dye solution, and both are notable for testing multiple treatment-quality indicators beyond visible colour removal, not just decolorization percentage.

The first is a 2023 study from IIT Delhi's Department of Biochemical Engineering and Biotechnology, which evaluated an engineered laccase variant derived from the fungus Cyathus bulleri, expressed in recombinant Pichia pastoris yeast, against real combined effluent collected from local dyeing units. The enzyme achieved 60 to 100 percent decolorization at a concentration of 500 units per litre, tested not only at 50 mL flask scale but in a membrane reactor system at a 0.3 litre volumetric level, run in both batch and continuous modes.1

The second is a 2021 study from Siksha 'O' Anusandhan University in Bhubaneswar, Odisha, using the yeast-like fungus Geotrichum candidum against real textile effluent. Within 18 hours, the treatment achieved a 98.5 percent reduction in chemical oxygen demand, 56.3 percent reduction in biological oxygen demand, 73.2 percent reduction in total suspended solids, 64 percent reduction in salinity, 89 percent colour removal, and 87 percent reduction in dye concentration, with the degradation pathway for the dye acid orange 10 confirmed through FTIR and LC-MS analysis identifying specific breakdown products.2 A related study from the same group directly compared suspended versus matrix-immobilized G. candidum cultures, finding immobilization raised six-hour decolorization from 85.5 to 98.5 percent, a meaningful engineering step, since immobilized biomass is generally easier to retain and reuse in a continuous-flow system than free-floating suspended culture, and is closer in form to how a fungal treatment stage would need to operate if integrated into a real effluent-flow system.3

Separately, fungal pellets of Aspergillus bombycis achieved 99.02 percent decolorization of a 20 mg/L synthetic solution of Reactive Red 31 within 12 hours in a purpose-built aerobic lab reactor, alongside a 94.19 percent reduction in chemical oxygen demand and an 83.97 percent reduction in total organic carbon.4 This result is methodologically strong on treatment-quality metrics but sits in the synthetic-solution category rather than the real-effluent one, illustrating exactly the kind of distinction this review's classification scheme is designed to surface.

3.3 Field and Industrial Deployment: An Empty Category

No published study or industry report was identified describing fungal or enzymatic dye-specific treatment technology operating at an actual Indian textile dyeing facility or common effluent treatment plant. This is the central finding of this review, and it is a null result rather than a data point, which makes it worth stating precisely: the search covered the three largest dyeing clusters in the country and found the deployment tier of the literature to be, as far as public documentation shows, empty.

What has been deployed instead is well documented. Following the 2011 Tirupur closure order, government and industry funding built out Common Effluent Treatment Plants engineered around Zero Liquid Discharge: pre-treatment via conventional activated-sludge biological oxidation (a generic bacterial process for organic load reduction, not a dye-specific fungal or enzymatic system), followed by reverse osmosis and mechanical evaporation to recover water and salts.15 The Indian government and Tamil Nadu state government jointly sanctioned ₹703.29 crore to build and upgrade 18 such CETPs in Tirupur alone, with a further ₹300 crore in direct subsidy.15 Tirupur's nine largest CETPs, developed with the assistance of TWIC (Tirupur Water Infrastructure Company), together treat approximately 53,000 cubic metres of effluent per day, entirely through this physico-chemical and reverse-osmosis pathway.16

4. Discussion

4.1 Why the Laboratory Evidence Hasn't Reached the Clusters

Three overlapping barriers explain the gap between what the lab literature shows and what is actually running at Tirupur, Jodhpur, and Panipat.

The first is technical. Enzymes that perform well in a controlled 50 mL flask or a 0.3 litre reactor face a fundamentally different challenge in real effluent flowing at industrial volumes: fluctuating pH, temperature, salt concentration, and dye mixture, all of which can destabilize enzyme activity that was optimized under stable lab conditions. Laccase-mediator systems, which use small helper molecules to extend the enzyme's reach to a broader range of dye structures, introduce a further complication: several of the synthetic mediators used are themselves toxic, meaning a treatment intended to detoxify effluent can, if not carefully managed, add a new toxicant to it.13 Enzyme production itself is also expensive at scale; downstream purification, a routine cost of working with any biological catalyst, is frequently cited across the wider bioremediation literature as one of the largest single barriers to commercial viability.12

The second barrier is regulatory. There is no established approval pathway in India for biological or enzymatic dye-treatment technology to be certified for use in place of, or alongside, conventional CETP infrastructure. Court-mandated Zero Liquid Discharge deadlines, the same 2011 order that shut down Tirupur's dyeing units, were met using reverse osmosis and evaporation, technology with a known regulatory and engineering track record, well before any fungal or enzymatic alternative had reached a comparable stage of development.15 Once a compliance deadline is met with proven technology, the regulatory pressure that might have pushed toward a cheaper or more targeted biological alternative largely disappears.

The third barrier is institutional, and it compounds the other two. Once ₹700 crore of public money and matching private investment has been sunk into reverse-osmosis and evaporator infrastructure built to a specific court deadline, the case for retrofitting or replacing that infrastructure with an unproven biological system has to clear a very high bar, even if the biological system might eventually prove cheaper to run. This dynamic is not unique to fungal treatment. It mirrors a broader pattern described in bio-based industry analysis as two sequential "valleys of death": the first between laboratory discovery and a working prototype, the second, often harder to cross, between a working prototype and something an industry with existing sunk costs is willing to adopt.12

4.2 A Geographic Mismatch Between Where the Research Happens and Where the Pollution Is

Mapping the studies in Table 1 against India's major dyeing clusters surfaces a second, less obvious gap alongside the lab-to-field one. The research is concentrated in Gujarat, Odisha, Uttar Pradesh, and Delhi, institutions and, in several cases, contamination sites tied to dye manufacturing or general industrial estates rather than to the specific textile dyeing and finishing clusters where the bulk of India's effluent volume is actually generated: Tirupur in Tamil Nadu, Jodhpur and Bhilwara in Rajasthan, Panipat in Haryana, and Surat in Gujarat. Gujarat is the one state appearing on both lists, home to both the Vatva contamination site that yielded the Aspergillus bombycis isolate and to Surat's large-scale textile processing cluster, yet no published study identified in this review connects the two. A research pipeline oriented toward the specific effluent chemistry of an actual dyeing cluster, rather than toward whichever contamination site happens to be locally accessible to a given research group, would be a meaningful step in itself, independent of the scale-up question addressed below.

4.3 What Would Need to Change

The IIT Delhi laccase study is the clearest indication of what a next step might look like: alongside the Siksha 'O' Anusandhan Geotrichum candidum work, it is one of only two identified cases that combined real, uncontrolled Indian textile effluent with reactor-scale testing rather than flask-scale synthetic dye solutions. Closing the remaining gap to field relevance would mean scaling that kind of trial by roughly two orders of magnitude, from 0.3 litres toward a pilot plant handling a meaningful fraction of a single CETP's daily volume, ideally integrated alongside existing infrastructure rather than proposed as a wholesale replacement for it. A pilot retrofit, positioned as a supplementary polishing step after existing activated-sludge and reverse-osmosis treatment rather than a competitor to the capital already invested, would face a lower institutional bar than a from-scratch biological CETP. Notably, the IIT Delhi Pleurotus sajor caju study was itself funded under the Department of Biotechnology's Accelerated Translational Grant for Commercialization, a scheme explicitly intended to move laboratory research toward market-ready technology.5 That translation-focused funding existed and was awarded to exactly this line of research, and the technology has still not reached a field trial, is a useful data point in its own right: it suggests the barrier sits somewhere past the initial funding gap, likely at the scale-up and industry-adoption stage described below, rather than at the earliest stage of research prioritization. This broader pattern, promising bench-scale biological treatment stalling before reaching real industrial wastewater streams, is not unique to India: a 2024 review of fungal-based textile treatment methods and a more recent review of hybrid fungal-wetland systems both describe the field, globally, as still oriented toward laboratory optimization rather than deployed application,911 which suggests the barriers identified in this review are structural to the technology's stage of development rather than specific to Indian institutions or policy.

4.4 Scope and Limitations

This review does not claim to have identified every fungal dye-degradation study relevant to India, nor every pilot project that may exist in unpublished industry reports or trade literature outside standard academic databases. It is possible that limited-scale pilot efforts exist that have not been published or indexed in the sources searched. Nine primary Indian studies were identified and classified for this review; a systematic search under a formal PRISMA protocol, screening a larger initial pool of records against explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria, would likely surface additional isolation-tier studies, though it is unlikely to change the reactor-tier finding materially, since real-effluent reactor testing is uncommon enough in this literature that both identified instances (IIT Delhi and Siksha 'O' Anusandhan) turned up early and consistently across multiple search rounds. What the review does establish with reasonable confidence is a pattern: an active and geographically distributed body of laboratory research, only two identified cases of reactor-scale testing using real rather than synthetic effluent, and no public evidence of field or industrial deployment at any of the three clusters examined.

5. Scale-Up Feasibility Model

Sections 3 and 4 characterize what has been published. This section produces an original estimate: taking the most field-relevant published dataset identified in this review and extrapolating it, under explicitly stated assumptions, to a defined deployment target. The output is a reactor sizing, an enzyme-cost estimate, and a comparison against what Indian CETPs already pay to treat the same volume of effluent by conventional means. This is a first-order engineering estimate, not a validated pilot result, and its limitations are stated in §5.4.

5.1 Model Basis and Target

The model is built on the continuous-mode operating parameters reported for the IIT Delhi engineered Cyathus bulleri laccase system, the only identified Indian study reporting continuous-flow operation on real textile effluent with a defined hydraulic retention time (HRT). In continuous operation, the 0.3 litre membrane reactor was run at an HRT of 6 hours with a steady-state laccase dosage of approximately 120 U per litre of effluent, achieving roughly 95 percent decolorization and 96 percent detoxification.1 The target deployment volume chosen for this model is 1 percent of Tirupur's combined CETP capacity, 530 cubic metres per day, a deliberately modest benchmark representing a supplementary polishing stage rather than a wholesale CETP replacement.

5.2 Reactor Sizing and Enzyme Demand

Holding the tested HRT and enzyme dosage constant and scaling only the throughput volume, treating 530 m³/day requires a continuous flow rate of approximately 22,083 litres per hour. At a fixed 6-hour HRT, the reactor's working volume must scale proportionally to flow rate, giving a required reactor volume of approximately 132,500 litres, or roughly 132.5 cubic metres, a scale-up factor of approximately 440,000 times the volume actually tested. At the same 120 U/L steady-state dosage, daily enzyme demand works out to approximately 63.6 million enzyme units (63,600 kU) per day. This figure is a lower bound: the source study noted that continuous-mode laccase activity declined during operation and required periodic replenishment in two dosing regimes to maintain the 120 U/L level, meaning actual cumulative enzyme consumption per litre treated is likely higher than this steady-state estimate captures.

5.3 Cost Estimate and Comparison to Deployed Treatment

A 2024 techno-economic analysis of fungal laccase production for wastewater treatment, using submerged fermentation of Trametes hirsuta, estimated the cost of crude laccase at 3.90 Canadian dollars per thousand units (kU).18 Applying that figure to the 63,600 kU/day demand calculated above gives an enzyme cost of approximately 248,000 CAD per day, or roughly 470 CAD per cubic metre treated. At representative 2026 exchange rates, that converts to approximately $340 USD, or roughly ₹28,500, per cubic metre, enzyme cost alone, before reactor capital, membrane replacement, aeration, or labour.

Indian industry sources place the running cost of existing CETP and ZLD treatment trains, the physico-chemical, reverse-osmosis, and evaporation systems actually operating at Tirupur, in the range of ₹15 to ₹200 per kilolitre (one kilolitre equals one cubic metre) depending on effluent strength and technology.19 The modelled enzyme cost of ₹28,500 per kilolitre is therefore between roughly 140 and 1,900 times higher than what Indian textile clusters already pay, using existing technology, to treat the same volume of effluent.

5.4 Interpretation and Model Limitations

This comparison, generated by combining two published figures that were never presented together in either source, gives the discussion in §4.1 a number rather than a general claim: enzyme production cost is not a minor line item standing in the way of an otherwise-ready technology, it is, at current laboratory-demonstrated dosages and commercial enzyme prices, the dominant cost by a wide margin. That reframes what "closing the gap" would actually require. It is not primarily a scale-up engineering problem, the 440,000-fold reactor scale-up is large but not qualitatively different from ordinary bioprocess scale-up. It is an enzyme economics problem: either the required dosage needs to fall substantially through improved enzyme engineering or immobilization and reuse (the kind of step demonstrated at small scale in the immobilized Geotrichum candidum study3), or production costs need to fall through cheaper substrates and fermentation routes, before a fungal treatment stage becomes cost-competitive with what CETPs already pay.

The model rests on several assumptions that should be stated plainly rather than absorbed into the headline numbers. It assumes linear scalability of both HRT and dosage from a 0.3 litre reactor to a 132.5 cubic metre one, an assumption that does not generally hold in bioreactor engineering, where mixing, oxygen transfer, and fouling dynamics change non-linearly with scale, meaning real-world reactor and enzyme requirements could differ substantially in either direction. It uses a single laccase cost benchmark from a different fungal species (Trametes hirsuta rather than the engineered Cyathus bulleri variant actually tested), produced in a different national market, as a stand-in for Indian production costs, which may not transfer directly given differing labour, energy, and regulatory costs between Canada and India. And it treats the 120 U/L steady-state dosage as the full cost driver while acknowledging, without being able to quantify, that periodic re-dosing likely raises true consumption further. Despite these caveats, the gap between the modelled cost and the deployed benchmark is large enough, well over two orders of magnitude at the low end, that it is unlikely to close entirely even after correcting for the assumptions above. The most direct implication for future research is that publishing the cost of achieving a given decolorization efficiency, not only the efficiency itself, would let subsequent studies and this kind of model track progress toward economic viability directly, rather than only toward technical performance.

6. Conclusion

India's textile dyeing clusters produce some of the country's most ecologically damaging industrial effluent, and a genuine, methodologically sound body of research, much of it produced at Indian institutions, has repeatedly shown that fungal enzymes can decolorize and partially mineralize the azo dyes responsible. That evidence has not translated into field deployment. The treatment infrastructure actually operating at Tirupur, built at a cost of hundreds of crores of public funding, relies entirely on physico-chemical and reverse-osmosis technology, with no dye-specific biological component beyond generic activated sludge.

The gap is not a failure of the science. It is the predictable result of a court-mandated compliance deadline being met by mature, bankable technology before biological alternatives had reached comparable scale, compounded by the ordinary economics of enzyme production and the institutional inertia that follows a large capital investment. The scale-up model in Section 5 puts a number on that economics: at current laboratory-demonstrated dosages and commercial enzyme prices, a fungal polishing stage would cost roughly two to three orders of magnitude more per kilolitre than Indian CETPs already pay using conventional technology. Closing that gap is primarily an enzyme-cost problem before it is a scale-up problem, and it would not require replacing what has already been built. It would require a scaled pilot, integrated as a supplementary step within existing CETP infrastructure, that reports cost per kilolitre alongside decolorization efficiency, treating real cluster effluent at a volume closer to industrial relevance than the 0.3 litres the most advanced published Indian study has so far reached.
