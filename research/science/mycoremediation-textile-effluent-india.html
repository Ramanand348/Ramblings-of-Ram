Mycoremediation of Textile Dye Effluent in India: A Scoping Review of the Gap Between Laboratory Evidence and Field Deployment

Fungal enzymes can break down the azo dyes that make Indian textile effluent so persistent. Almost none of that research has left the lab bench for the dyeing clusters producing the pollution.

Abstract

India's textile dyeing industry, concentrated in a small number of large clusters, is one of the country's most significant sources of industrial water pollution, releasing synthetic azo dyes that conventional physico-chemical treatment struggles to fully remove. A substantial body of laboratory research, much of it conducted at Indian institutions, has demonstrated that filamentous fungi and their extracellular enzymes, chiefly laccases and peroxidases, can decolorize and partially mineralize these same dyes at high efficiency. This scoping review characterizes that research literature by stage of development (fungal isolation and screening, lab-scale reactor trials, and field or industrial deployment) and sets it against the treatment infrastructure actually operating in India's major dyeing clusters.

The review finds a consistent pattern: dozens of studies demonstrate fungal decolorization efficiencies exceeding 90 percent at flask or small-reactor scale, including work using real, uncontrolled textile effluent rather than synthetic dye solutions. No published evidence was found of fungal or enzymatic dye-degradation technology operating at field or industrial scale within an Indian textile cluster. Instead, the treatment infrastructure built into government-funded Zero Liquid Discharge systems, representing hundreds of crores of public investment, relies entirely on physico-chemical and reverse-osmosis-based technology with generic bacterial activated-sludge treatment, not fungal or enzymatic systems purpose-built for dye degradation.

The discussion traces this gap to a combination of technical barriers (enzyme production cost, instability under real industrial conditions, and the toxicity of synthetic mediators used to boost laccase activity), regulatory barriers (the absence of an approval pathway for biological dye-treatment technology, and court-mandated deadlines that were met using conventional technology before biological alternatives had matured), and an institutional one: capital already sunk into reverse-osmosis infrastructure creates inertia against adopting an unproven alternative, however promising the underlying science.

1. Introduction

Textile dyeing and finishing account for an estimated 17 to 20 percent of industrial water pollution worldwide, and azo dyes, the class most commonly used in the industry, make up roughly 80 percent of all commercial dyes produced globally. Their molecular structure, built around a stable nitrogen-nitrogen double bond, is precisely what makes them commercially useful (colourfast, resistant to fading) and environmentally persistent (resistant to breakdown). India hosts several of the world's largest textile dyeing clusters, including Tirupur in Tamil Nadu, which alone contributes more than half of India's knitwear exports and has, at various points, run 760 registered dyeing units. The clusters at Jodhpur in Rajasthan and Panipat in Haryana carry a similar concentration of dyeing and printing capacity in water-scarce regions.

The ecological cost of this concentration is well documented. Untreated or under-treated effluent from Tirupur's dyeing units led to a 2011 Madras High Court order closing all units found in violation of pollution norms, following documented degradation of the Noyyal River and surrounding farmland. A similar pattern has played out along Rajasthan's Jojari River near Balotra, where textile effluent has been linked to farmland turning barren and displacement of agricultural communities.

Fungal bioremediation of dye effluent, mycoremediation, has been studied as an alternative or complement to conventional treatment for several decades. Certain fungi, most notably white-rot species, secrete extracellular oxidative enzymes, laccases, lignin peroxidases, and manganese peroxidases, evolved originally to break down lignin, the structurally similar aromatic polymer that makes wood rigid. These same enzymes act on the aromatic azo bonds in synthetic dyes, and a substantial published literature, including work from Indian institutions, demonstrates decolorization efficiencies often exceeding 90 percent under laboratory conditions. This review asks a narrower and more practical question than most of that literature does: how much of this laboratory-demonstrated capability has actually reached the dyeing clusters generating India's textile effluent, and what is standing in the way.

2. Methods

This is a scoping review rather than a formal systematic review conducted under a PRISMA protocol. The distinction matters and is stated plainly: the aim here is not to exhaustively enumerate every study on fungal dye degradation, but to characterize the literature by stage of technological maturity and compare it against documented, deployed treatment infrastructure in India's major textile clusters. This approach follows the same evidentiary logic used elsewhere on this site: characterizing a body of existing material against a shared framework rather than generating new primary data.

2.1 Literature Search and Classification

Published studies were identified through searches of peer-reviewed journal databases and institutional repositories, using combinations of terms covering fungal species, decolorization, mycoremediation, laccase and peroxidase enzymes, azo dye degradation, and textile effluent, filtered for relevance to Indian institutions, effluent sources, or contamination sites where identifiable. Studies were classified into three stages: isolation and screening (fungal strains identified and tested against dye solutions at plate or flask scale, typically under 100 mL), lab-scale reactor (testing in a designed bioreactor system, whether batch or continuous, typically under 1 litre, sometimes using real rather than synthetic effluent), and field or industrial deployment (any application at or connected to an actual operating textile dyeing facility or common effluent treatment plant).

2.2 Infrastructure Comparison

To characterize what has actually been deployed, publicly available government and industry documentation was reviewed for three major clusters (Tirupur, Jodhpur, and Panipat), covering funded Common Effluent Treatment Plant (CETP) projects, the technology components specified in those projects, and any reference to biological or enzymatic dye-specific treatment as opposed to generic activated-sludge biological oxidation used for broader organic load reduction.

3. Results

3.1 Isolation and Screening: A Deep and Active Literature

The isolation and screening tier is by far the largest and most active. Fungal strains including Aspergillus flavus, Aspergillus niger, Aspergillus bombycis, and Pleurotus sajor caju have all been isolated or tested against azo, reactive, and anthraquinone dyes, with reported decolorization efficiencies frequently above 90 percent within days. Pleurotus sajor caju, for example, showed strong laccase-driven decolorization of Reactive Black 5, a dye common in Indian textile effluent, with higher laccase activity correlating directly with faster decolorization. This tier of research also extends to heavy metal contamination: fungal isolates collected from contaminated soil sites in Hisar and Panipat, Haryana, demonstrated meaningful chromium and arsenic removal capacity, indicating that the bioprospecting and isolation expertise needed for this work already exists within Indian research institutions, even where it hasn't yet been directed at dye-specific field problems.

3.2 Lab-Scale Reactor Trials: Fewer, But Working With Real Effluent

A smaller number of studies have progressed to designed reactor systems, and a subset of these use real textile effluent rather than synthetic dye solutions, a meaningful step toward field relevance. The most advanced Indian example identified is a 2023 study from IIT Delhi's Department of Biochemical Engineering and Biotechnology, which evaluated an engineered laccase variant derived from Cyathus bulleri, expressed in recombinant yeast, against real combined effluent collected from local dyeing units. The enzyme achieved 60 to 100 percent decolorization at a concentration of 500 units per litre, and was tested not only at 50 mL flask scale but in a membrane reactor system at a 0.3 litre volumetric level, run in both batch and continuous modes.

Separately, fungal pellets of Aspergillus bombycis achieved 99.02 percent decolorization of the sulfonated dye Reactive Red 31 within 12 hours in a purpose-built aerobic lab reactor, alongside substantial reductions in chemical oxygen demand and total organic carbon, both standard indicators of overall effluent treatment quality, not just visible colour removal.

3.3 Field and Industrial Deployment: An Empty Category

No published study or industry report was identified describing fungal or enzymatic dye-specific treatment technology operating at an actual Indian textile dyeing facility or common effluent treatment plant. This is the central finding of this review, and it is a null result rather than a data point, which makes it worth stating precisely: the search covered the three largest dyeing clusters in the country and found the deployment tier of the literature to be, as far as public documentation shows, empty.

What has been deployed instead is well documented. Following the 2011 Tirupur closure order, government and industry funding built out Common Effluent Treatment Plants engineered around Zero Liquid Discharge: pre-treatment via conventional activated-sludge biological oxidation (a generic bacterial process for organic load reduction, not a dye-specific fungal or enzymatic system), followed by reverse osmosis and mechanical evaporation to recover water and salts. The Indian government and Tamil Nadu state government jointly sanctioned ₹703.29 crore to build and upgrade 18 such CETPs in Tirupur alone, with a further ₹300 crore in direct subsidy. Tirupur's nine largest CETPs, developed with the assistance of TWIC (Tirupur Water Infrastructure Company), together treat approximately 53,000 cubic metres of effluent per day, entirely through this physico-chemical and reverse-osmosis pathway.

4. Discussion

4.1 Why the Laboratory Evidence Hasn't Reached the Clusters

Three overlapping barriers explain the gap between what the lab literature shows and what is actually running at Tirupur, Jodhpur, and Panipat.

The first is technical. Enzymes that perform well in a controlled 50 mL flask or a 0.3 litre reactor face a fundamentally different challenge in real effluent flowing at industrial volumes: fluctuating pH, temperature, salt concentration, and dye mixture, all of which can destabilize enzyme activity that was optimized under stable lab conditions. Laccase-mediator systems, which use small helper molecules to extend the enzyme's reach to a broader range of dye structures, introduce a further complication: several of the synthetic mediators used are themselves toxic, meaning a treatment intended to detoxify effluent can, if not carefully managed, add a new toxicant to it. Enzyme production itself is also expensive at scale; downstream purification, a routine cost of working with any biological catalyst, is frequently cited across the wider bioremediation literature as one of the largest single barriers to commercial viability.

The second barrier is regulatory. There is no established approval pathway in India for biological or enzymatic dye-treatment technology to be certified for use in place of, or alongside, conventional CETP infrastructure. Court-mandated Zero Liquid Discharge deadlines, the same 2011 order that shut down Tirupur's dyeing units, were met using reverse osmosis and evaporation, technology with a known regulatory and engineering track record, well before any fungal or enzymatic alternative had reached a comparable stage of development. Once a compliance deadline is met with proven technology, the regulatory pressure that might have pushed toward a cheaper or more targeted biological alternative largely disappears.

The third barrier is institutional, and it compounds the other two. Once ₹700 crore of public money and matching private investment has been sunk into reverse-osmosis and evaporator infrastructure built to a specific court deadline, the case for retrofitting or replacing that infrastructure with an unproven biological system has to clear a very high bar, even if the biological system might eventually prove cheaper to run. This dynamic is not unique to fungal treatment. It mirrors a broader pattern described in bio-based industry analysis as two sequential "valleys of death": the first between laboratory discovery and a working prototype, the second, often harder to cross, between a working prototype and something an industry with existing sunk costs is willing to adopt.

4.2 What Would Need to Change

The IIT Delhi laccase study is the clearest indication of what a next step might look like: it is the only identified case that combined real, uncontrolled Indian textile effluent with reactor-scale testing rather than flask-scale synthetic dye solutions. Closing the remaining gap to field relevance would mean scaling that kind of trial by roughly two orders of magnitude, from 0.3 litres toward a pilot plant handling a meaningful fraction of a single CETP's daily volume, ideally integrated alongside existing infrastructure rather than proposed as a wholesale replacement for it. A pilot retrofit, positioned as a supplementary polishing step after existing activated-sludge and reverse-osmosis treatment rather than a competitor to the capital already invested, would face a lower institutional bar than a from-scratch biological CETP.

4.3 Scope and Limitations

This review does not claim to have identified every fungal dye-degradation study relevant to India, nor every pilot project that may exist in unpublished industry reports or trade literature outside standard academic databases. It is possible that limited-scale pilot efforts exist that have not been published or indexed in the sources searched. What the review does establish with reasonable confidence is a pattern: an active and successful body of laboratory research, a near-complete absence of published reactor-scale work using real effluent beyond the single IIT Delhi study identified, and no public evidence of field deployment at any of the three clusters examined.

5. Conclusion

India's textile dyeing clusters produce some of the country's most ecologically damaging industrial effluent, and a genuine, methodologically sound body of research, much of it produced at Indian institutions, has repeatedly shown that fungal enzymes can decolorize and partially mineralize the azo dyes responsible. That evidence has not translated into field deployment. The treatment infrastructure actually operating at Tirupur, built at a cost of hundreds of crores of public funding, relies entirely on physico-chemical and reverse-osmosis technology, with no dye-specific biological component beyond generic activated sludge.

The gap is not a failure of the science. It is the predictable result of a court-mandated compliance deadline being met by mature, bankable technology before biological alternatives had reached comparable scale, compounded by the ordinary economics of enzyme production and the institutional inertia that follows a large capital investment. Closing it would not require replacing what has already been built. It would require a scaled pilot, integrated as a supplementary step within existing CETP infrastructure, treating real cluster effluent at a volume closer to industrial relevance than the 0.3 litres the most advanced published Indian study has so far reached.
