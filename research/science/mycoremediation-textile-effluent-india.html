Mycoremediation of Textile Dye Effluent in India: A Scoping Review of the Gap Between Laboratory Evidence and Field Deployment

Fungal enzymes can break down the azo dyes that make Indian textile effluent so persistent. Almost none of that research has left the lab bench for the dyeing clusters producing the pollution.

Abstract

India's textile dyeing industry, concentrated in a handful of large clusters, ranks among the country's most significant sources of industrial water pollution. It releases synthetic azo dyes that conventional physico-chemical treatment struggles to fully remove. A substantial body of laboratory research, much of it done at Indian institutions, has shown that filamentous fungi and their extracellular enzymes, chiefly laccases and peroxidases, can decolorize and partially mineralize these same dyes at high efficiency. This scoping review sorts that literature by stage of development (fungal isolation and screening, lab-scale reactor trials, field or industrial deployment) and sets it against the treatment infrastructure actually running in India's major dyeing clusters.

The pattern that emerges is fairly consistent. Dozens of studies report fungal decolorization efficiencies above 90 percent at flask or small-reactor scale, some of them using real, uncontrolled textile effluent rather than synthetic dye solutions. But no published evidence turned up of fungal or enzymatic dye-degradation technology running at field or industrial scale inside an Indian textile cluster. What has actually been built with government-funded Zero Liquid Discharge money, hundreds of crores of public investment, relies entirely on physico-chemical and reverse-osmosis technology paired with generic bacterial activated sludge, not fungal or enzymatic systems built for dye degradation specifically.

The discussion traces this gap to three overlapping causes: technical (enzyme production cost, instability under real industrial conditions, and toxic synthetic mediators used to boost laccase activity), regulatory (no approval pathway exists for biological dye-treatment technology, and court-mandated deadlines were met with conventional technology before biological alternatives had matured), and institutional (capital already sunk into reverse-osmosis infrastructure creates inertia against an unproven alternative, however promising the underlying science). Rather than leave that account qualitative, this review also builds an original scale-up feasibility model, extrapolating the most field-relevant published dataset, an IIT Delhi engineered laccase system tested in continuous flow on real effluent, to a defined target of 530 cubic metres per day, one percent of Tirupur's combined CETP capacity. The required reactor volume turns out roughly 440,000 times larger than what's been tested, but that's not actually the binding constraint. Enzyme cost is. At current laboratory-demonstrated dosages and commercial enzyme prices, the modelled treatment cost of roughly ₹28,500 per cubic metre comes out 140 to 1,900 times higher than the ₹15 to ₹200 Indian CETPs already pay per cubic metre using conventional technology, which reframes the lab-to-field gap as an enzyme-economics problem before it's a scale-up one.

1. Introduction

Textile dyeing and finishing account for an estimated 17 to 20 percent of industrial water pollution worldwide. Azo dyes, the class most commonly used in the industry, make up roughly 80 percent of all commercial dyes produced globally.13 Their molecular structure, built around a stable nitrogen-nitrogen double bond, is exactly what makes them commercially useful (colourfast, resistant to fading) and environmentally stubborn (resistant to breakdown). India hosts several of the world's largest textile dyeing clusters. Tirupur in Tamil Nadu alone contributes more than half of India's knitwear exports and has, at various points, run 760 registered dyeing units.14 Jodhpur in Rajasthan and Panipat in Haryana carry a similar concentration of dyeing and printing capacity, both in water-scarce regions.17

The ecological cost is well documented. Untreated or under-treated effluent from Tirupur's dyeing units led to a 2011 Madras High Court order closing all units found in violation of pollution norms, after years of documented degradation of the Noyyal River and surrounding farmland.14 Something similar has played out along Rajasthan's Jojari River near Balotra, where textile effluent has been linked to farmland turning barren and to the displacement of agricultural communities.17

Fungal bioremediation of dye effluent, mycoremediation, has been studied as an alternative or complement to conventional treatment for several decades now. Certain fungi, most notably white-rot species, secrete extracellular oxidative enzymes, laccases, lignin peroxidases, manganese peroxidases, that evolved to break down lignin, the aromatic polymer that gives wood its rigidity. Those same enzymes happen to act on the aromatic azo bonds in synthetic dyes, and a fairly large published literature, including work from Indian institutions, reports decolorization efficiencies often above 90 percent under laboratory conditions.910 What this review asks is narrower and more practical than most of that literature: how much of this laboratory-demonstrated capability has actually reached the dyeing clusters generating India's textile effluent, and what's standing in the way.

2. Methods

This is a scoping review, not a formal systematic review conducted under a PRISMA protocol, and that distinction is worth stating plainly. The aim isn't to exhaustively enumerate every study on fungal dye degradation, but to sort the literature by stage of technological maturity and compare it against documented, deployed treatment infrastructure in India's major textile clusters. It's the same evidentiary logic used elsewhere on this site: characterizing a body of existing material against a shared framework rather than generating new primary data.

2.1 Literature Search and Classification

Studies were identified through iterative searches of PubMed, Nature/Scientific Reports, ScienceDirect, Springer Link, and indexed institutional repositories (Bioresources and Bioprocessing, AMB Express, Frontiers in Microbiology, Environmental Sciences Europe, Chemosphere), combining terms for fungal species names, decolorization, mycoremediation, laccase and peroxidase enzymes, azo dye degradation, and textile effluent. Search terms ran in successive rounds, each one narrowed by what the last round turned up: general terms first ("mycoremediation textile dye effluent India"), then organism-specific terms once candidate species showed up in early results (say, "Aspergillus bombycis textile dye" or "Geotrichum candidum textile effluent"), then infrastructure-specific terms to characterize deployed treatment technology ("Tirupur CETP Zero Liquid Discharge technology").

A study counted as India-relevant if it met at least one of three criteria: the fungal strain came from an Indian contamination site (named specifically), the authors' institutional affiliation was Indian, or the study addressed an Indian regulatory or industrial context directly. Nine primary studies cleared that bar and are discussed below. A further set of review articles and non-Indian primary studies were used only for corroborating context and weren't counted toward the three-tier classification. Studies fell into three stages: isolation and screening (fungal strains identified and tested against dye solutions at plate or flask scale, typically under 100 mL), lab-scale reactor (a designed bioreactor system, batch or continuous, typically under 1 litre), and field or industrial deployment (any application connected to an actual operating textile dyeing facility or common effluent treatment plant). Within the reactor tier, each study was also flagged for whether it tested real, uncontrolled textile effluent or a synthetic dye solution at fixed concentration, since that distinction bears directly on field relevance and isn't always foregrounded in the studies' own abstracts.

2.2 Infrastructure Comparison

To characterize what's actually been deployed, publicly available government and industry documentation was reviewed for three major clusters (Tirupur, Jodhpur, Panipat), covering funded Common Effluent Treatment Plant (CETP) projects, the technology specified in those projects, and any reference to biological or enzymatic dye-specific treatment as opposed to generic activated-sludge biological oxidation used for broader organic load reduction. Central Pollution Control Board assessment reports, Tamil Nadu Department of Textiles project documentation, and Tirupur Water Infrastructure Company (TWIC) technical summaries were the primary sources here.

3. Results

3.1 Isolation and Screening: A Deep and Active Literature, Traceable to Named Indian Contamination Sites

The isolation and screening tier is the largest and most active of the three, and unlike much of the global mycoremediation literature, several Indian studies trace their fungal strains back to specifically named contamination sites rather than a generic "textile effluent" source. Aspergillus bombycis came from dye-contaminated soil at the Gujarat Industrial Development Corporation estate in Vatva, Ahmedabad, a documented dye-industry pollution site.4 Four more isolates, Trichoderma virens, Phlebiopsis cf. ravenelii, Talaromyces stipitatus, and Aspergillus niger, were pulled from textile dye-contaminated soil near Meerut, Uttar Pradesh, narrowed down from a pool of 74 candidates to four based on how well they decolorized five synthetic azo dyes.6 Pleurotus sajor caju, tested by the Applied Microbiology Lab at IIT Delhi's Centre for Rural Development and Technology, showed laccase-driven decolorization of both Reactive Blue 13 and Reactive Black 5, two dyes common in Indian textile effluent. That study went further than most, comparing whole-organism decolorization against purified laccase-mediated decolorization directly, and confirming the degradation pathway of Reactive Blue 13 with FTIR, HPLC, and GC-MS.5

This tier also stretches into heavy metal contamination, a different pollutant class but evidence of the same underlying skillset: fungal isolates from contaminated soil in Hisar and Panipat, Haryana, showed meaningful chromium and arsenic removal.78 Put these together and they suggest the specific expertise this work needs, isolating functional fungal strains from real Indian contamination sites and characterizing what they can degrade, already exists across at least four states (Gujarat, Uttar Pradesh, Haryana, Delhi), even where nobody has pointed it at an operating field problem yet.

3.2 Lab-Scale Reactor Trials: Fewer, and a Real-Effluent / Synthetic-Solution Split

Far fewer studies made it to a designed reactor system, and applying the real-effluent-versus-synthetic-solution distinction from the Methods section narrows things further still. Only two Indian studies used real, uncontrolled textile effluent rather than a prepared dye solution, and both went beyond simple decolorization percentage to test multiple treatment-quality indicators.

The first is a 2023 study out of IIT Delhi's Department of Biochemical Engineering and Biotechnology. It evaluated an engineered laccase variant from the fungus Cyathus bulleri, expressed in recombinant Pichia pastoris yeast, against real combined effluent collected from local dyeing units, achieving 60 to 100 percent decolorization at 500 units per litre. The team tested it at 50 mL flask scale and then in a membrane reactor at 0.3 litres, running both batch and continuous modes.1

The second comes from Siksha 'O' Anusandhan University in Bhubaneswar, Odisha, in 2021, using the yeast-like fungus Geotrichum candidum against real textile effluent. Within 18 hours, the treatment cut chemical oxygen demand by 98.5 percent, biological oxygen demand by 56.3 percent, total suspended solids by 73.2 percent, salinity by 64 percent, colour by 89 percent, and dye concentration by 87 percent, and FTIR/LC-MS analysis confirmed specific breakdown products for the degraded dye, acid orange 10.2 A related paper from the same group compared suspended versus matrix-immobilized G. candidum cultures directly, and found immobilization pushed six-hour decolorization from 85.5 up to 98.5 percent. That's a meaningful engineering step: immobilized biomass is generally easier to retain and reuse in a continuous-flow system than free-floating culture, closer to how a fungal treatment stage would actually need to run inside a real effluent-flow setup.3

Separately, fungal pellets of Aspergillus bombycis hit 99.02 percent decolorization of a 20 mg/L synthetic solution of Reactive Red 31 within 12 hours in a purpose-built aerobic lab reactor, along with a 94.19 percent drop in chemical oxygen demand and 83.97 percent in total organic carbon.4 Strong numbers, but this one sits in the synthetic-solution column rather than the real-effluent one, which is exactly the kind of distinction this review's classification scheme was built to catch.

3.3 Field and Industrial Deployment: An Empty Category

No published study or industry report turned up describing fungal or enzymatic dye-specific treatment technology running at an actual Indian textile dyeing facility or common effluent treatment plant. This is the central finding of the review, and since it's a null result rather than a data point, it's worth stating precisely: the search covered the three largest dyeing clusters in the country and found the deployment tier of the literature to be, as far as public documentation shows, empty.

What's been deployed instead is well documented. After the 2011 Tirupur closure order, government and industry funding built out Common Effluent Treatment Plants engineered around Zero Liquid Discharge: pre-treatment via conventional activated-sludge biological oxidation (a generic bacterial process for organic load reduction, not anything dye-specific), followed by reverse osmosis and mechanical evaporation to recover water and salts.15 The Indian government and Tamil Nadu state government jointly put up ₹703.29 crore to build and upgrade 18 such CETPs in Tirupur alone, plus another ₹300 crore in direct subsidy.15 Tirupur's nine largest CETPs, built with help from TWIC (Tirupur Water Infrastructure Company), together treat roughly 53,000 cubic metres of effluent a day, entirely through this physico-chemical and reverse-osmosis route.16

4. Discussion

4.1 Why the Laboratory Evidence Hasn't Reached the Clusters

Three overlapping barriers explain why what's running at Tirupur, Jodhpur, and Panipat looks nothing like what the lab literature shows.

The first is technical. An enzyme that performs beautifully in a controlled 50 mL flask or a 0.3 litre reactor runs into a very different world in real effluent flowing at industrial volumes: pH swings, temperature shifts, changing salt concentration, and a shifting dye mixture, any of which can knock out enzyme activity that was tuned for stable lab conditions. Laccase-mediator systems, which use small helper molecules to widen the enzyme's reach across dye structures, add a further wrinkle: several of the synthetic mediators in use are themselves toxic, so a treatment meant to detoxify effluent can, if mismanaged, introduce a new toxicant instead.13 Enzyme production is expensive at scale too. Downstream purification, an unavoidable cost with any biological catalyst, keeps showing up across the wider bioremediation literature as one of the biggest single barriers to commercial viability.12

The second barrier is regulatory. India has no established approval pathway for biological or enzymatic dye-treatment technology to get certified for use alongside, or instead of, conventional CETP infrastructure. The 2011 court order that shut down Tirupur's dyeing units set Zero Liquid Discharge deadlines that got met with reverse osmosis and evaporation, technology with a proven regulatory and engineering track record, well before any fungal or enzymatic alternative was anywhere near ready.15 Once a compliance deadline is satisfied with proven technology, whatever regulatory pressure might have pushed toward a cheaper or more targeted biological alternative mostly evaporates.

The third barrier is institutional, and it compounds the other two. Once ₹700 crore of public money, plus matching private investment, has gone into reverse-osmosis and evaporator infrastructure built to a court deadline, replacing or retrofitting it with an unproven biological system has to clear a very high bar, even if that system might eventually run cheaper. This isn't unique to fungal treatment. It's the same pattern bio-based industry analysis describes as two sequential "valleys of death": first between laboratory discovery and a working prototype, then, often the harder crossing, between a working prototype and something an industry with sunk costs is actually willing to adopt.12

4.2 A Geographic Mismatch Between Where the Research Happens and Where the Pollution Is

Mapping the studies in Table 1 against India's major dyeing clusters turns up a second, less obvious gap sitting alongside the lab-to-field one. The research clusters in Gujarat, Odisha, Uttar Pradesh, and Delhi, tied to institutions and, in several cases, contamination sites connected to dye manufacturing or general industrial estates rather than the specific textile dyeing and finishing clusters actually producing most of India's effluent: Tirupur in Tamil Nadu, Jodhpur and Bhilwara in Rajasthan, Panipat in Haryana, Surat in Gujarat. Gujarat is the one state on both lists, home to the Vatva site that yielded the Aspergillus bombycis isolate and to Surat's large-scale textile processing cluster, and yet no study identified in this review connects the two. Orienting a research pipeline toward the specific effluent chemistry of an actual dyeing cluster, rather than whatever contamination site happens to be locally accessible to a given research group, would be worth doing on its own, independent of the scale-up question that follows.

4.3 What Would Need to Change

The IIT Delhi laccase study points most clearly to what a next step could look like. Along with the Siksha 'O' Anusandhan Geotrichum candidum work, it's one of only two cases identified anywhere that combined real, uncontrolled Indian textile effluent with reactor-scale testing rather than flask-scale synthetic solutions. Closing the remaining gap to field relevance would mean scaling that kind of trial by roughly two orders of magnitude, from 0.3 litres toward a pilot plant handling a meaningful slice of a single CETP's daily volume, ideally bolted onto existing infrastructure rather than pitched as a wholesale replacement for it. A pilot retrofit, framed as a supplementary polishing step after existing activated-sludge and reverse-osmosis treatment rather than a competitor for the capital already spent, would clear a much lower institutional bar than a from-scratch biological CETP.

Worth flagging here: the IIT Delhi Pleurotus sajor caju study was itself funded under the Department of Biotechnology's Accelerated Translational Grant for Commercialization, a scheme built specifically to push lab research toward market-ready technology.5 That kind of translation-focused funding existed, went to exactly this line of research, and the technology still hasn't reached a field trial. That tells us something useful on its own: the barrier probably sits past the initial funding gap, more likely at the scale-up and industry-adoption stage discussed above than at the earliest stage of deciding what to research. And this isn't just an Indian pattern. A 2024 review of fungal-based textile treatment methods and a more recent review of hybrid fungal-wetland systems both describe the field globally as still oriented toward laboratory optimization rather than deployed application,911 which points to the barriers here being structural to where the technology sits developmentally, not specific to Indian institutions or policy.

4.4 Scope and Limitations

This review doesn't claim to have found every fungal dye-degradation study relevant to India, or every pilot project sitting in unpublished industry reports or trade literature outside standard academic databases. Limited-scale pilot efforts that haven't been published or indexed anywhere searchable could well exist. Nine primary Indian studies were identified and classified here; a formal PRISMA-protocol systematic search, screening a larger initial pool against explicit inclusion and exclusion criteria, would probably surface more isolation-tier studies, though it's unlikely to change the reactor-tier finding much, since real-effluent reactor testing is rare enough in this literature that both identified instances (IIT Delhi and Siksha 'O' Anusandhan) turned up early and kept turning up across multiple search rounds. What this review does establish with reasonable confidence is a pattern: an active, geographically scattered body of laboratory research, only two cases of reactor-scale testing on real rather than synthetic effluent, and no public evidence of field or industrial deployment at any of the three clusters examined.

5. Scale-Up Feasibility Model

Sections 3 and 4 characterize what's been published. This section produces something original instead: taking the most field-relevant published dataset found in this review and extrapolating it, under assumptions stated up front, to a defined deployment target. What comes out is a reactor sizing, an enzyme-cost estimate, and a comparison against what Indian CETPs already pay to treat the same volume of effluent conventionally. Call it a first-order engineering estimate, not a validated pilot result; its limitations are laid out in §5.5.

5.1 Model Basis and Target

The model leans on the continuous-mode operating parameters from the IIT Delhi engineered Cyathus bulleri laccase system, the only Indian study found reporting continuous-flow operation on real textile effluent with a defined hydraulic retention time (HRT). In continuous operation, the 0.3 litre membrane reactor ran at an HRT of 6 hours with a steady-state laccase dosage around 120 U per litre of effluent, hitting roughly 95 percent decolorization and 96 percent detoxification.1 The target volume chosen here is 1 percent of Tirupur's combined CETP capacity, 530 cubic metres per day, deliberately modest, meant to represent a supplementary polishing stage rather than a wholesale CETP replacement.

5.2 Mathematical Basis

Four relationships underpin the model, laid out here so the results in §5.3 can be checked by hand rather than taken on faith. Reactor sizing comes straight from the definition of hydraulic retention time: for a continuously stirred or flow-through reactor, HRT (τ) is working volume divided by volumetric flow rate, so volume equals flow rate times retention time. Enzyme demand comes from holding the tested dosage concentration steady and multiplying by target throughput volume. Cost comes from applying a published price per unit of enzyme activity to that demand. All four steps assume linear scaling, an assumption §5.5 pushes back on.

Notation τ = hydraulic retention time (h, held constant at the tested value) V = reactor working volume (L) Q = volumetric flow rate (L/h) Vd = target throughput volume (L/day) C = enzyme dosage concentration (U/L, held constant at the tested value) Ed = daily enzyme demand (U/day) P = enzyme price (currency per kU) S = scale-up factor (dimensionless)
Eq. 1 — Reactor volume from retention time τ = V / Q ⟹ V = Q · τ, where Q = Vd / 24 Substituting Vd = 530,000 L/day and τ = 6 h: Q = 530,000 / 24 = 22,083.3 L/h, so V = 22,083.3 × 6 ≈ 132,500 L ≈ 132.5 m³.
Eq. 2 — Scale-up factor S = Vtarget / Vtested Substituting Vtarget = 132,500 L and Vtested = 0.3 L: S = 132,500 / 0.3 ≈ 441,667, roughly 4.4 × 10⁵.
Eq. 3 — Daily enzyme demand Ed = Vd · C Substituting Vd = 530,000 L/day and C = 120 U/L: Ed = 530,000 × 120 = 63,600,000 U/day = 63,600 kU/day. This treats C as a fixed steady-state concentration; §5.5 explains why this makes Ed a lower bound rather than a full consumption estimate.
Eq. 4 — Daily and per-volume cost Costd = (Ed / 1000) · P, Costm³ = Costd / (Vd / 1000) Substituting Ed = 63,600 kU/day and P = 3.90 CAD/kU: Costd = 63,600 × 3.90 = 248,040 CAD/day. Dividing by the 530 m³/day target: Costm³ = 248,040 / 530 ≈ 468 CAD/m³, converting at representative 2026 rates to roughly $340 USD or ₹28,500 per cubic metre.

5.3 Reactor Sizing and Enzyme Demand

Hold the tested HRT and enzyme dosage steady and scale only the throughput volume: treating 530 m³/day needs a continuous flow rate of roughly 22,083 litres per hour. At a fixed 6-hour HRT, the reactor's working volume has to scale right along with flow rate, which puts the required reactor volume at approximately 132,500 litres, about 132.5 cubic metres, a scale-up factor of roughly 440,000 times the volume actually tested (Eq. 1, Eq. 2). At the same 120 U/L steady-state dosage, daily enzyme demand comes out to about 63.6 million units (63,600 kU) per day (Eq. 3). Treat that as a floor, not a ceiling: the source study noted that continuous-mode laccase activity dropped off during operation and needed periodic top-ups in two dosing regimes just to hold the 120 U/L level, so actual cumulative enzyme consumption per litre treated is probably higher than this steady-state number suggests.

5.4 Cost Estimate and Comparison to Deployed Treatment

A 2024 techno-economic analysis of fungal laccase production for wastewater treatment, using submerged fermentation of Trametes hirsuta, put the cost of crude laccase at 3.90 Canadian dollars per thousand units (kU).18 Apply that to the 63,600 kU/day demand above and enzyme cost lands around 248,000 CAD per day, roughly 470 CAD per cubic metre treated (Eq. 4). At representative 2026 exchange rates that's about $340 USD, or roughly ₹28,500, per cubic metre, enzyme cost alone, nothing yet for reactor capital, membrane replacement, aeration, or labour.

Indian industry sources put the running cost of existing CETP and ZLD treatment trains, the physico-chemical, reverse-osmosis, and evaporation systems actually operating at Tirupur, somewhere between ₹15 and ₹200 per kilolitre (one kilolitre equals one cubic metre), depending on effluent strength and technology.19 So the modelled enzyme cost of ₹28,500 per kilolitre runs somewhere between 140 and 1,900 times higher than what Indian textile clusters already pay, with existing technology, to treat the same volume.

5.5 Interpretation and Model Limitations

This comparison combines two published figures that never appeared together in either source, and doing so turns the discussion in §4.1 from a general claim into an actual number: enzyme production cost isn't some minor line item holding back an otherwise-ready technology, it's the dominant cost by a wide margin at current laboratory-demonstrated dosages and commercial enzyme prices. That changes what "closing the gap" would really take. It's not mainly a scale-up engineering problem, a 440,000-fold reactor scale-up sounds dramatic but isn't qualitatively different from ordinary bioprocess scale-up. It's an enzyme economics problem. Either the dosage needs to drop substantially through better enzyme engineering or immobilization and reuse (the immobilized Geotrichum candidum study already showed a small-scale version of that step3), or production costs need to fall through cheaper substrates and fermentation routes, before a fungal treatment stage gets anywhere near cost-competitive with what CETPs already pay.

A few assumptions here deserve to be stated plainly rather than buried under the headline numbers. The model assumes HRT and dosage scale linearly from a 0.3 litre reactor all the way to 132.5 cubic metres, which generally doesn't hold in bioreactor engineering, where mixing, oxygen transfer, and fouling behave non-linearly as scale increases, so real-world requirements could land noticeably higher or lower than modelled. It borrows a laccase cost figure from a different fungal species (Trametes hirsuta, not the engineered Cyathus bulleri variant actually tested) produced in a different country's market, and Canadian production costs may not map cleanly onto India given how labour, energy, and regulatory costs differ. And it treats the 120 U/L steady-state dosage as the whole cost story while admitting, without being able to put a number on it, that periodic re-dosing likely pushes true consumption higher still. Even accounting for all that, the gap between the modelled cost and the deployed benchmark, well over two orders of magnitude at the low end, looks too wide to close entirely once the assumptions are corrected for. Maybe the clearest lesson for future research: publishing the cost of hitting a given decolorization efficiency, not just the efficiency itself, would let this kind of model and future studies track progress toward economic viability directly, rather than only toward technical performance.

6. Conclusion

India's textile dyeing clusters produce some of the country's most ecologically damaging industrial effluent, and a genuine, methodologically sound body of research, much of it done at Indian institutions, has repeatedly shown that fungal enzymes can decolorize and partially mineralize the azo dyes responsible. None of that evidence has made it into field deployment. What's actually running at Tirupur, built with hundreds of crores in public funding, relies entirely on physico-chemical and reverse-osmosis technology, no dye-specific biological component beyond generic activated sludge.

This isn't a failure of the science. It's the predictable outcome of a court-mandated compliance deadline getting met by mature, bankable technology before biological alternatives had reached comparable scale, on top of the ordinary economics of enzyme production and the institutional inertia that follows a large capital investment. The scale-up model in Section 5 puts a number on that economics: at current laboratory-demonstrated dosages and commercial enzyme prices, a fungal polishing stage would cost roughly two to three orders of magnitude more per kilolitre than Indian CETPs already pay with conventional technology. Closing that gap is an enzyme-cost problem before it's a scale-up problem, and it wouldn't mean replacing what's already been built. It would take a scaled pilot, folded into existing CETP infrastructure as a supplementary step, reporting cost per kilolitre alongside decolorization efficiency, treating real cluster effluent at a volume closer to industrial relevance than the 0.3 litres the most advanced published Indian study has reached so far.
