In Silico Identification of Horizontal Gene Transfer: Tracking blaNDM-1 Across Multi-Species Gut Reservoirs

A comparative genomics study using NCBI Pathogen Detection and MicroBIGG-E data to trace the carbapenem-resistance gene blaNDM-1 as it moves between E. coli and Shigella inside the human gut resistome.

Abstract

Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) have gone from a localized clinical concern to a genuine global health security problem. At the center of that shift sits the New Delhi metallo-beta-lactamase-1 (blaNDM-1) gene, a highly mobile genetic determinant that renders nearly all β-lactam antibiotics, carbapenems included, clinically useless. Traditional surveillance tends to focus on clonal outbreaks within a single species. This study instead uses a high-resolution in silico comparative genomic approach to look at inter-species spread of blaNDM-1 within the human gut resistome over 2024–2026, spread that doesn't show up if you're only watching one species at a time.

Using the NCBI Pathogen Detection and MicroBIGG-E platforms, a multi-species genomic cluster (PDS000002968.262) turned up containing both Escherichia coli and Shigella isolates collected between 2024 and 2026. These taxonomically distinct organisms shared a high-resolution SNP cluster (SNP distance <20), which is solid evidence of recent Horizontal Gene Transfer (HGT). The gene itself sat on a highly conserved genetic platform, contig ACBQRM010001011.1, with 100% sequence identity and coverage across the divergent taxa. That level of conservation points to the cassette moving via a stable, efficient plasmid shuttle rather than accumulating through ordinary background mutation.

Finding identical resistance environments in both a commensal-like organism (E. coli) and a more clinically dangerous one (Shigella) says something about how bacterial evolution works here: the gut isn't a static backdrop, it's a busy junction where resistance genes move between species to dodge antibiotic pressure. That argues for surveillance built around genes rather than species, tracking the mobile element itself rather than any one host organism. Catching these transfer hubs early gives public health responders a better shot at getting ahead of pan-resistant enteric pathogens before they become the norm.

1. Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is escalating fast enough that it now counts as one of the more formidable challenges facing global public health. Clinical attention has historically gone to resistant pathogens themselves, but there's growing evidence that the human gut microbiome works as a "resistome," a large standing reservoir of resistance genes.1 The blaNDM-1 gene is a particularly worrying member of that reservoir, since it confers resistance to nearly all beta-lactam antibiotics, carbapenems included, drugs that are often the last option available.2 It spreads mainly through Horizontal Gene Transfer (HGT), where mobile genetic elements carry resistance markers from one bacterial species to another. In the crowded, mixed-species environment of the gastrointestinal tract, a commensal organism can act as a stable host for a while and then pass the gene on to something more dangerous.

2. Methods

Genomic sequences came from the NCBI Pathogen Detection Browser, targeting isolates carrying the blaNDM-1 genotype. The search was limited to human-host isolates collected between January 2022 and early 2026. To catch recent HGT events specifically, isolates were grouped by Pathogen Detection SNP Cluster (PDS) ID, and clusters containing at least two distinct bacterial species (say, Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae) at low SNP distance were flagged as likely evidence of recent inter-species transfer. The regions flanking the gene were analyzed further to pin down which mobile genetic elements (MGEs) were actually involved.

2.1 Dataset Selection and Retrieval

Genomic data came from the NCBI Pathogen Detection Project, a centralized hub pulling in sequence data from public health agencies worldwide.4 The MicroBIGG-E (Microbial Browser for Identification of Genetic and Genomic Elements) database was the specific tool used.5 Search parameters targeted isolates with the genotype across a 2022-to-2026 window, and the host filter was restricted to Homo sapiens to keep the focus clinical.

2.2 Multi-species SNP Cluster Analysis

Active HGT was identified using the Pathogen Detection SNP Cluster (PDS) hierarchy.4 Isolates were grouped at a high-resolution threshold (SNP distance <20), and clusters were screened specifically for cases spanning two or more distinct genera within a single PDS ID (Escherichia and Shigella, for instance). That's how hopping events got pinpointed: wherever core genome similarity was high enough to suggest either a recent common ancestor or an actual, immediate gene transfer.

2.3 Characterization of the Genetic Environment

For each cluster identified, the genetic architecture around the gene was examined using Allele Detection and HMM (Hidden Markov Model) methods within MicroBIGG-E, to confirm gene identity and coverage.35 The Contig ID mattered most here, since it determines whether the resistance marker sits on a mobile genetic element (MGE) rather than the chromosome. Percentage identity and coverage were both calculated to gauge how conserved the resistance cassette was across species.

3.2 Identification of Inter-species Resistance Reservoirs

Genomic surveillance through the NCBI Pathogen Detection Browser turned up a high-priority multi-species cluster, PDS000002968.262, holding isolates of both Escherichia coli and Shigella.4 These isolates sat within a high-resolution SNP cluster (SNP distance <20), which points to shared evolutionary history and recent divergence rather than coincidence. Both species carried the New Delhi metallo-beta-lactamase-1 genotype, confirming active transmission of carbapenem resistance across different enteric genera between 2022 and 2026.

3.3 Genomic Environment and Mobile Genetic Elements

Deeper analysis through MicroBIGG-E showed the gene in these isolates (isolate PDT003013055.1, specifically) sitting on a highly conserved genetic platform, contig ACBQRM010001011.1.5 Bioinformatic characterization of that contig showed:

  • Sequence conservation: the gene showed 100% coverage and 100% identity against the reference allele (Class: Beta-lactam; Subclass: Carbapenem).
  • Genetic stability: perfect conservation of the NDM-1 variant across different species in the same cluster points to the resistance marker moving as part of a stable, mobile unit rather than accumulating through rapid point mutation.
  • Co-resistance profiling: the NDM-1 gene sat close to other resistance determinants (gyrA and parC mutations among them), consistent with a multidrug-resistant profile that closes off nearly every clinical treatment option.

Beyond carbapenem resistance, the blaNDM-1-carrying isolates carried a fairly complex mix of virulence and fitness factors too. The iutA (aerobactin receptor) and iucB (N(6)-hydroxylysine O-acetyltransferase) genes turned up on the same or associated contigs, which suggests the resistance plasmid is doing double duty as a fitness platform as well. Those extra genes help the bacteria survive in iron-starved environments like the human gut, which gives them a real competitive edge, and that edge is probably part of why the blaNDM-1 gene has held on and spread across the Escherichia–Shigella complex.

4. Discussion

4.1 The Role of the Enteric "Resistome" Hub

Finding blaNDM-1 inside an identical PDS cluster spanning both E. coli and Shigella is solid evidence of Horizontal Gene Transfer (HGT) within the human gut resistome. E. coli and Shigella are genetically close but clinically very different, so their shared occupancy of a low-SNP cluster shows how quiet, commensal reservoirs can directly feed the spread of more dangerous, resistant pathogens.

4.2 Implications of the ACBQRM010001011.1 Platform

Where the resistance marker sits, on contig ACBQRM010001011.1, matters. Across the 2024–2026 timeline covered here, this contig functions as a kind of shuttle, moving carbapenemase genes between hosts efficiently. The 100% identity seen in this analysis suggests the plasmid-mediated transfer is outpacing the bacteria's own background mutation rate, letting resistance jump between species without losing potency along the way.

4.3 Conclusion for Surveillance

All of this points to a real weakness in single-species monitoring. Gene movement between E. coli and Shigella means the diagnostic lines we usually draw between species are getting blurred by mobile genetic elements. Surveillance going forward needs to prioritize multi-species cluster analysis if it's going to catch the next wave of pan-resistant enteric infections before they spread widely.

5. Conclusion

This study's findings show that blaNDM-1 spread within the human gut resistome reached a genuinely concerning point between 2024 and 2026. High-resolution in silico genomic surveillance turned up clear evidence of active Horizontal Gene Transfer within the PDS000002968.262 multi-species cluster, and the near-perfect conservation of the resistance cassette on contig ACBQRM010001011.1 across both Escherichia coli and Shigella shows that species boundaries matter less and less once mobile genetic elements are involved.

The resistance gene also sits alongside fitness-boosting siderophore systems (iutA, for one), which points to a shift toward plasmids that do double duty, carrying both resistance and a survival edge even without antibiotic pressure in the picture. That suggests this threat is settling into the commensal gut flora more permanently, which makes it harder to root out with standard clinical approaches.

The bigger takeaway is that AMR monitoring needs to move past species-specific diagnostics. A gene-centric surveillance model, one built around tracking these mobile elements across species rather than watching any single organism, looks like the better way to catch high-traffic resistance hubs early. Identifying the specific genetic shuttles behind these transfers gives public health agencies a real shot at staying ahead of pan-resistant enteric pathogens, and keeping carbapenems useful for longer.
