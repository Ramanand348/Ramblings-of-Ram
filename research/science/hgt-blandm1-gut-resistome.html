In Silico Identification of Horizontal Gene Transfer: Tracking blaNDM-1 Across Multi-Species Gut Reservoirs

A comparative genomics study using NCBI Pathogen Detection and MicroBIGG-E data to trace the carbapenem-resistance gene blaNDM-1 as it moves between E. coli and Shigella inside the human gut resistome.

Abstract

The relentless expansion of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) has transitioned from a localized clinical concern to a pervasive global health security threat. Central to this crisis is the New Delhi metallo-beta-lactamase-1 (blaNDM-1) gene, a highly mobile genetic determinant that renders nearly all β-lactam antibiotics, including carbapenems, clinically ineffective. While traditional surveillance often focuses on clonal outbreaks within a single species, this study uses a high-resolution in silico comparative genomic framework to investigate the "silent" inter-species dissemination of blaNDM-1 within the human gut resistome during the 2024–2026 period.

By leveraging the NCBI Pathogen Detection and MicroBIGG-E platforms, a critical multi-species genomic cluster (PDS000002968.262) was identified containing both Escherichia coli and Shigella isolates collected between 2024 and 2026. Analysis revealed that these taxonomically distinct organisms shared a high-resolution SNP cluster (SNP distance <20), providing robust evidence of recent Horizontal Gene Transfer (HGT). Molecular characterization localized the gene on a highly conserved genetic platform, specifically contig ACBQRM010001011.1, exhibiting 100% sequence identity and coverage across divergent taxa. This "perfect conservation" suggests the cassette is moving via a stable, high-efficiency plasmid shuttle that bypasses the background mutation rates of the host bacteria.

The presence of identical resistance environments in both commensal-like E. coli and virulent Shigella underscores a paradigm shift in bacterial evolution: the human gut is no longer a static environment but a high-traffic hub where resistance genes "hop" between species to evade antibiotic pressure. These findings argue for a transition from species-centric diagnostic models toward a gene-centric surveillance strategy. By intercepting these mobile resistance hubs in real time, public health interventions can better predict and mitigate the rise of pan-resistant enteric pathogens before they achieve widespread clinical dominance.

1. Introduction

The rapid escalation of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) represents one of the most formidable challenges to global public health. While clinical attention has historically focused on the expansion of resistant pathogens, recent evidence suggests that the human gut microbiome acts as a critical "resistome," a vast reservoir of antibiotic resistance genes.1 Among these, the blaNDM-1 gene is particularly alarming due to its ability to confer resistance to nearly all beta-lactam antibiotics, including carbapenems, which are often considered drugs of last resort.2 The dissemination of this gene is primarily driven by Horizontal Gene Transfer (HGT), where mobile genetic elements facilitate the "hopping" of resistance markers between different bacterial species. In the polymicrobial environment of the gastrointestinal tract, commensal organisms can serve as stable hosts, subsequently transferring these genes to opportunistic pathogens.

2. Methods

Genomic sequences were retrieved from the NCBI Pathogen Detection Browser, specifically targeting isolates containing the blaNDM-1 genotype. The search was restricted to human-host isolates collected between January 2022 and early 2026. A "multi-species cluster" approach was used to identify recent HGT events. Isolates were grouped based on Pathogen Detection SNP Cluster (PDS) IDs. Clusters containing at least two distinct bacterial species (e.g., Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae) with a low SNP distance were prioritized as evidence of recent inter-species gene transfer. The flanking regions of the gene were further analyzed to identify the specific mobile genetic elements (MGEs) involved.

2.1 Dataset Selection and Retrieval

Genomic data was sourced from the NCBI Pathogen Detection Project, a centralized hub that integrates sequence data from global public health agencies.4 The MicroBIGG-E (Microbial Browser for Identification of Genetic and Genomic Elements) database was specifically queried.5 Search parameters were set to identify isolates containing the genotype within a temporal window of 2022 to 2026. To focus on clinical relevance, the host filter was restricted to Homo sapiens.

2.2 Multi-species SNP Cluster Analysis

To identify active Horizontal Gene Transfer (HGT), the Pathogen Detection SNP Cluster (PDS) hierarchy was used.4 Isolates were grouped using a high-resolution threshold (SNP distance <20). Clusters were specifically screened for "multi-species clusters" — defined as a single PDS ID containing two or more distinct taxonomic genera (e.g., Escherichia and Shigella). This approach allowed "hopping" events to be pinpointed, where core genome similarity was high enough to suggest a recent common ancestor or immediate gene transfer.

2.3 Characterization of the Genetic Environment

For identified clusters, the genetic architecture of the gene was analyzed. The Allele Detection and HMM (Hidden Markov Model) methods within MicroBIGG-E were used to verify gene identity and coverage.35 Analysis focused on the Contig ID to determine whether the resistance marker was localized on a mobile genetic element (MGE) rather than the chromosome. Percentage identity and percentage coverage were calculated to assess the conservation of the resistance cassette across different species.

3.2 Identification of Inter-species Resistance Reservoirs

Large-scale genomic surveillance using the NCBI Pathogen Detection Browser identified a high-priority multi-species cluster, PDS000002968.262, containing isolates of both Escherichia coli and Shigella species.4 Notably, these isolates remained within a high-resolution SNP cluster (SNP distance <20), indicating a shared evolutionary history and recent divergence. Both species within this cluster were found to carry the New Delhi metallo-beta-lactamase-1 genotype, confirming active transmission of carbapenem resistance across different enteric genera between 2022 and 2026.

3.3 Genomic Environment and Mobile Genetic Elements

In-depth analysis via the MicroBIGG-E platform revealed that the gene in these isolates (specifically isolate PDT003013055.1) was localized on a highly conserved genetic platform, identified as contig ACBQRM010001011.1.5 Bioinformatic characterization of this contig showed:

  • Sequence conservation: the gene demonstrated 100% coverage and 100% identity against the reference allele (Class: Beta-lactam; Subclass: Carbapenem).
  • Genetic stability: the perfect conservation of the NDM-1 variant across different species within the same cluster suggests the resistance marker is moving as part of a stable, mobile genetic unit rather than undergoing rapid point mutations.
  • Co-resistance profiling: the NDM-1 gene was found in proximity to other resistance determinants (such as gyrA and parC mutations), suggesting a multidrug-resistant (MDR) "superbug" profile that limits nearly all clinical treatment options.

Beyond carbapenem resistance, the blaNDM-1-carrying isolates displayed a complex mosaic of virulence and fitness factors. Specifically, the presence of the iutA (aerobactin receptor) and iucB (N(6)-hydroxylysine O-acetyltransferase) genes on the same or associated contigs suggests that the resistance plasmid is a "hybrid" platform. These "plus" genes enhance the bacteria's ability to survive in iron-limited environments, such as the human gut, thereby providing a significant competitive advantage that facilitates the maintenance and spread of the blaNDM-1 gene across the Escherichia–Shigella complex.

4. Discussion

4.1 The Role of the Enteric "Resistome" Hub

The discovery of the blaNDM-1 gene within an identical PDS cluster containing both E. coli and Shigella provides definitive evidence for Horizontal Gene Transfer (HGT) within the human gut "resistome." Because E. coli and Shigella are genetically congruent but clinically distinct, their shared occupancy of a low-SNP cluster highlights how "silent" commensal reservoirs can directly fuel the expansion of virulent, resistant pathogens.

4.2 Implications of the ACBQRM010001011.1 Platform

The localization of the resistance marker on contig ACBQRM010001011.1 is significant. In the context of the 2024–2026 outbreak timeline, this specific contig acts as a "molecular shuttle," facilitating the rapid movement of carbapenemase genes. The 100% identity observed in this in silico analysis suggests that this plasmid-mediated transfer is occurring faster than the background mutation rate of the bacteria, allowing the "superbug" phenotype to leap between species without losing fitness or resistance efficacy.

4.3 Conclusion for Surveillance

These findings underscore the inadequacy of single-species monitoring. The movement of the gene between E. coli and Shigella suggests that traditional diagnostic boundaries are being blurred by mobile genetic elements. Future surveillance must prioritize multi-species cluster analysis to intercept the next wave of pan-resistant enteric infections before they achieve widespread clinical dissemination.

5. Conclusion

The findings of this study demonstrate that the dissemination of the New Delhi metallo-beta-lactamase-1 (blaNDM-1) gene within the human gut "resistome" has reached a critical stage of evolution between 2024 and 2026. Using high-resolution in silico genomic surveillance, this study provides definitive evidence of active Horizontal Gene Transfer (HGT) within the PDS000002968.262 multi-species cluster. The high-fidelity conservation of the resistance cassette on contig ACBQRM010001011.1 across both Escherichia coli and Shigella species confirms that taxonomical boundaries are becoming increasingly irrelevant in the face of mobile genetic elements.

Furthermore, the co-localization of carbapenem resistance with fitness-enhancing siderophore systems (e.g., iutA) reveals a shift toward "hybrid" plasmids that provide a competitive survival advantage even in the absence of antibiotic pressure. This suggests that the current "superbug" threat is becoming deeply integrated into the commensal gut flora, making it harder to eradicate through standard clinical protocols.

Ultimately, this research highlights that the next generation of AMR monitoring must move beyond species-specific diagnostics. A gene-centric, multi-species surveillance model is essential to intercepting these high-traffic resistance hubs. By identifying the specific genetic "shuttles" that facilitate these hops, public health agencies can better anticipate and mitigate the rise of pan-resistant enteric pathogens, ensuring that carbapenems remain effective as a life-saving clinical resource.
