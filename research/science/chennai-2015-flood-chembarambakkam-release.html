Twenty-One Hours at 29,000 Cusecs: A Mass-Balance Reading of the Chembarambakkam Release, December 2015

The audit says a gentler release was available. A storage balance built from its own numbers shows how much that would have bought, and where the decision that mattered actually sat.

Abstract

On 1 and 2 December 2015, the Chembarambakkam reservoir passed 29,000 cusecs into the Adyar for 21 hours, and that release has since been described both as the cause of Chennai's flood and as a small part of it. A Comptroller and Auditor General audit holds that a gentler schedule was available: at 2 pm on 1 December the tank had 0.268 TMC of room, and 12,000 cusecs could have been held for six hours. Both readings rest on a handful of published numbers, and those numbers can be put through a simple storage balance. Using only figures reported in the audit, the lake received about 2,610 mcft over the 25 hours from 2 pm on 1 December and retained about 104 mcft of it, so it acted as a pass-through. The audit's alternative holds only if inflow stayed below about 24,400 cusecs during those hours; at the implied average of 29,000 cusecs the tank fills in about 4.4 hours. With the room that existed, the lowest release that keeps the tank below full is about 26,000 cusecs, a reduction of roughly 10 percent at best. Published models put the Adyar's peak at 2,850 to 3,800 m³/s, between 1.4 and 3.4 times every published capacity figure, with the release supplying at most about a fifth of the peak at the city. The decision with real leverage came earlier: the lake stood above 21 ft from 16 November, and capping the release at 12,000 cusecs would have required about 1.26 TMC more empty space on 1 December.

1. The Record, and Where It Disagrees

Chembarambakkam is one of Chennai's drinking-water reservoirs. Its full capacity is 3.645 TMC (3,645 mcft) at a full depth of 24 ft, and its surplus spills into the Adyar.1 On 1 December 2015, after rain that IIT Madras's rapid assessment puts at 47.5 cm at the reservoir, it passed 29,000 cusecs into the river, and the Adyar flooded.6 Two claims have followed ever since. One is that the release caused the flood. The other is that it was a minor part of a flood driven by rain that fell below the dam. The 2017 CAG audit sides firmly with the first3, and hydrological models lean toward the second. Both can be true at once only if the question is asked more precisely than it usually is.

The audit's record is specific. The depth reached 21 ft at noon on 16 November, the level at which, on the audit's reading of the rules for the neighbouring Poondi reservoir, an extraordinary emergency should have been declared, and it stayed above that level until 2 December.14 The Collector and police were informed on 1 December, when the depth was 22.5 ft.4 At 2 pm that day storage was 3.377 TMC, which is 0.268 TMC below full, and the release was raised from 12,000 to 20,960 cusecs. It went to 25,000 cusecs at 5 pm and to 29,000 cusecs from 6 pm.2 The 29,000 cusecs ran for 21 hours, and peak storage was 3.481 TMC, leaving 0.164 TMC unused.14 The audit adds that the tank was never held at its full level, which it attributes to a wish to keep illegally tolerated private land in the foreshore from submerging, that the lake's operating rules had not been revised in 23 years, and that two upstream reservoirs recommended for flood mitigation were never built.14

The published figures do not all agree, and the disagreements matter for what can be concluded from them.

The storage figures differ by 19 mcft, which is immaterial. The duration does not: 29,000 cusecs for 12 hours is about 1,250 mcft, and for 21 hours it is about 2,190 mcft. The reconstruction below adopts the audit's 21 hours because they come from the primary document, and treats the shorter figure as a sensitivity. The capacity figures span a factor of almost two, which is why the results are shown against all four instead of a chosen one.

2. Reconstructing the Reservoir's Mass Balance

2.1 Data and Assumptions

The balance uses only the figures in section 1. Units are converted at 1 cusec = 1 ft³/s = 0.0283168 m³/s, and 1 TMC = 1,000 mcft. The window runs for 25 hours, from 2 pm on 1 December to 3 pm on 2 December, which is the 21 hours at 29,000 cusecs plus the four hours of step-up before it. Four assumptions carry the result.

  • The release steps were held as reported: 20,960 cusecs for three hours, 25,000 for one hour, and 29,000 for 21 hours.
  • Storage was 3,377 mcft at the start of the window and reached the reported maximum of 3,481 mcft at its end. This is the assumption that matters most. If the maximum was reached earlier, the inflow estimate falls.
  • Losses and abstractions are negligible. The treatment plant's rated capacity of 530 MLD is about 217 cusecs, under 1 percent of the flows involved.13
  • Inflow is represented by its mean over the window, because no hourly inflow record appears in the published sources.
Storage balance dS/dt = I(t) - O(t) Mean inflow over the window: I = (V_out + ΔS) / T V_out = released volume; ΔS = change in storage; T = 25 h = 90,000 s Room needed to cap the release at Q: V(Q) = ∫ max(I(t) - Q, 0) dt ≥ (I - Q) T

The last line explains why the later tables are lower bounds. If inflow varied around its mean, the true room needed to cap the release at any rate is at least what the constant-inflow figure gives.

3. What the Reconstruction Shows

3.1 The Lake as a Pass-Through

The release steps add up to about 2,509 mcft over the window, roughly 71 million m³. Storage rose by 104 mcft, from 3,377 to 3,481. The implied inflow volume is therefore about 2,613 mcft, which is a mean of about 29,030 cusecs over 25 hours and equals 72 percent of the lake's whole capacity. The lake retained 104 mcft of it, about 4 percent.

Over the whole window, then, the numbers say the opposite of outflow exceeding inflow: net storage rose. The audit's statement that outflow exceeded inflow can still hold for the opening hours, when the release was stepped up while the inflow may have been lower than the new release. The published figures cannot test that, because the hourly inflow record is not among them.

3.2 The Audit's Alternative Under Test

The audit argues that the 0.268 TMC of room allowed 12,000 cusecs to be held for six hours instead of stepping up, and that the tank would still not have brimmed.2 Over those six hours the actual releases total about 525 mcft, against 259 mcft at a steady 12,000 cusecs, a difference of 266 mcft. That is almost exactly the room the audit cites. The claim that the tank stays below full therefore depends on how much water was arriving.

Holding 12,000 cusecs for six hours stays within the room only if the mean inflow over those hours was below about 24,400 cusecs. The table shows what happens across plausible inflows.

If inflow was lower in the first six hours and higher later, the alternative works for longer, and it simply postpones the large release. In neither case does the 12,000 cusec hold remove the large release: once the tank is full the outflow has to match the inflow.

3.3 The Release Against the Rest of the Adyar

The release of 29,000 cusecs is about 821 m³/s. IIT Madras's model puts the flow from catchments below the reservoir, through Manimangalam, Perungalathur and Tambaram, at as much as 3,000 m³/s, and the combined peak entering the city at about 3,800 m³/s.6 On that estimate the release is about 22 percent of the peak, and the uncontrolled flow alone exceeds the channel capacity of 2,038 m³/s by about 960 m³/s.

Under every pairing the modelled peak exceeds the capacity, by 1.4 to 3.4 times, while the release alone, passed through a clear channel, stays inside every capacity figure. That is the quantitative content of the two claims at once. The release was not enough on its own to overtop the river, and the river had no margin left to absorb anything.

Three cautions apply. The models are unvalidated: the NRSC study states that its discharges could not be checked against field gauges, since none exist at the sites.7 The IIT Madras sum assumes the peaks coincide, and the travel time of the release against the timing of the local peak is not published. And close to the reservoir the balance reverses: the assessment attributes the flooding at Mudichur, West Tambaram and Manapakkam directly to the release.6 A related HEC-HMS study found that enlarging all tanks in the Adyar basin by 20 percent cut the modelled peak by only 3.34 percent.9

4. The Price of Freeboard

4.1 The Lowest Release the Room Allowed

Turning the question around gives the lowest steady release the room could have supported. With 268 mcft of room and a mean inflow of 29,030 cusecs over 25 hours, the lowest cap that keeps the tank below full across the whole window is about 26,000 cusecs, against the 29,000 actually used. Allowing for inflow that varied around its mean, the true figure would be higher still. The gain from perfect last-day management, on the audit's own numbers, is a reduction of at most about 10 percent in the largest release.

4.2 What a Lower Cap Would Have Required

Capping the release at 12,000 cusecs would have needed about 1,530 mcft of room, which is about 1,260 mcft more than existed. The table sets the room needed against the starting storage it implies, and against the water supply that room represents.

This is the real trade-off in operating a lake that is both a drinking-water source and a flood buffer, and it is why a release rule that looks careless after a flood can look prudent before one.

4.3 Where the Decision Window Sat

The 1,260 mcft gap could not have been closed on 1 December. It could have been closed over the preceding fortnight. The depth was above 21 ft from 16 November,4 so a release of about 1,040 cusecs sustained for 14 days, roughly 29 m³/s, would have created it. That is about 1.5 percent of the 2,000 m³/s that the NRSC simulation puts at the Adyar's first peak, on 23 November, an order of magnitude only.7 The cost is the one in section 4.2: had the northeast monsoon failed after that release, Chennai would have been short of the water it gave away. The audit's central complaint, that the lake's rules had not been revised in 23 years, is a complaint about the absence of any considered answer to that trade-off.

Later practice has moved in this direction. The Public Works Department's stated protocol releases water once the level reaches 22 ft against a full level of 24 ft, in graded steps that follow the inflow.5 The reconstruction suggests the limit of that rule. The roughly 250 mcft per foot implied near the full level means 2 ft of room is about 500 mcft, and on the constant-inflow estimate that would support a cap near 23,500 cusecs for an event the size of 2015's. A level-based trigger helps, but for an event of that size the protection has to come from elsewhere: the upstream storage the audit says was never built, and a channel with more capacity than the Adyar had.

5. What the Numbers Do and Don't Settle

Four results hold under the published figures. The lake passed on about 96 percent of the event's inflow volume over the 25 hours that mattered. The audit's gentler schedule would have trimmed the peak release by about a tenth at best, and works only under assumptions about inflow that the average does not support. The release was a modest share of the peak at the city, and the river had no margin left against any published capacity figure. The lever with real size was storage held back weeks earlier, at a price in water supply that an operating rule has to weigh in advance.

Several things stay open. The hourly inflow record would replace the constant-mean assumption and turn the lower bounds into estimates. The timing of the release's arrival against the local peak would show how much the two added. The true capacity of the Adyar in 2015, given encroachment and silt, is unsettled across a factor of almost two. The duration of the 29,000 cusec release appears as both 21 hours and 12 hours in the record. And the audit was read through its own quoted text as reproduced in search excerpts and press coverage, because the full PDF could not be retrieved automatically, so the figures taken from it are worth checking against the report itself.

What survives every reading is narrower than either side's usual claim. The release was neither the whole flood nor irrelevant to it, and the biggest decision was made, or not made, in the fortnight before the storm.
