Domesticated Capsid Genes: Designing a Genome-Wide Census Beyond Arc

A computational design for finding viral-capsid-derived genes in vertebrate proteomes, starting from Arc and the roughly 8 percent of the human genome that is retroviral in origin.

Key takeaways

  • About 8 percent of the human genome is endogenous retrovirus sequence, but nearly all of it is inert. The interesting cases are the few viral genes that were kept and put to work.
  • Arc, a gene needed for memory consolidation, encodes a protein that assembles into virus-like capsids and moves RNA between neurons.
  • A census of capsid-derived genes across vertebrate proteomes needs only public sequence databases and free software, so no wet lab is involved.
  • No results are reported here. The piece sets out the design, the expected outputs, and the ways it could fail.

Abstract

Roughly 8 percent of the human genome descends from retroviruses that infected germline cells and were then inherited as ordinary DNA.1 Almost all of that material has decayed past function. A small set of viral genes escaped decay and were domesticated, including the placental fusion protein syncytin2 and Arc, a neuronal gene whose product forms capsid-like particles.3 This piece proposes a computational census that searches vertebrate proteomes for capsid-derived genes using profile hidden Markov models, confirms candidates with predicted structures, and places them on a phylogeny. The study requires public databases and a laptop or free cloud notebook.

1. Two Kinds of Viral Inheritance

The figure of 8 percent and the story of Arc are often merged, and they should be kept apart. The 8 percent comes from the draft human genome analysis, which attributed about 8 percent of the sequence to long terminal repeat elements, most of them endogenous retroviruses.1 These are fossils. Many survive only as a lone repeat sequence with the viral genes long gone, and the youngest family, HERV-K, still carries intact stretches but is not known to produce infectious virus.

Arc belongs to a different category. Its ancestor was a Ty3/gypsy retrotransposon, a relative of retroviruses, and the gene is a single domesticated copy that natural selection has maintained for hundreds of millions of years.4 The two stories meet at one question: how many of the viral genes in vertebrate genomes were kept because the host found a use for them, and how many such genes remain unlisted?

2. Arc as the Worked Example

2.1 What Arc Does

Arc (activity-regulated cytoskeleton-associated protein) is switched on in neurons during learning, and mice lacking it show impaired long-term memory consolidation.5 Structural work showed that the Arc protein carries domains resembling the retroviral Gag capsid protein.4 The protein self-assembles into capsids that enclose Arc mRNA and are released in extracellular vesicles, and recipient neurons take up the cargo.3

2.2 A Repeated Event

Drosophila has its own Arc gene, and its protein also forms capsids and transfers RNA between cells.6 The fly and tetrapod genes come from separate retrotransposon captures, so the same trick was domesticated at least twice. Mammals add other cases. PEG10 is also Gag-derived, is required for placental development in mice,7 and packages its own mRNA into particles.8 These examples show that capsid domestication happens repeatedly, which is the argument for a systematic search.

3. Design of the Census

The pipeline moves from sequence to structure to evolutionary history. Each stage uses a tool with a published benchmark and a free implementation.

1. Seed profileKnown Gag-like proteins (Arc, PEG10, PNMA family, RTL genes)Multiple alignment and a profile HMM, alongside the Pfam Gag family PF03732Search model

Two outputs would be new. The first is a ranked list of unannotated Gag-like genes, each with a structural confirmation. The second is an estimate of how many independent capture events the vertebrate lineage contains, which the current literature has only for individual genes.

4. Feasibility and Failure Modes

Compute is modest. HMM searches over a few hundred proteomes finish in hours on a laptop, and AlphaFold models are downloadable, so nothing needs to be folded from scratch. The harder risks are scientific.

  • Divergence. Domesticated genes drift fast, and a profile built from known examples may miss distant ones. Structure-based search in stage 4 is the safeguard, since folds outlast sequence similarity.
  • Annotation gaps. A gene absent from a proteome may only be unannotated. Searching translated genome assemblies for a subset of species would test this.
  • False positives from retained viral remnants. A Gag-like protein is not proof of domestication. Candidates would need evidence of selection, such as a dN/dS ratio below one, and orthology across species.
  • Functional claims. Computation can nominate genes and describe their histories. Whether a candidate forms capsids or carries RNA needs an experiment, and the study should say so in its conclusions.

A natural extension is to check coding variation in the confirmed human genes using population genomes from India, where data access runs through the Indian Biological Data Centre application process.
