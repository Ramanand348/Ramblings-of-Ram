The Saint Who Stayed: Ramanuja, the Koyil and the Years of Fire

In the fifth enclosure of the Srirangam temple a seated figure has been anointed with camphor and saffron for some nine centuries, and the tradition of the Koyil says it is Ramanuja himself, still there. Twice the armies of the Delhi Sultanate came for the temple. The story is what it took for him to still be sitting there afterwards.

Key takeaways

  • The attackers were the armies of the Delhi Sultanate, in 1311 under Malik Kafur and in 1323 under Ulugh Khan, not the Mughals.
  • The temple's tradition holds that Ramanuja's own form still sits in the shrine; some Sri Vaishnava scholars read it as an image made over his relics, and no examination has settled the point.
  • Pillai Lokacharya carried the festival image Namperumal south in 1323, and it returned only in 1371, forty-eight years later, under Gopana of Vijayanagara.
  • Vedanta Desika saved the only manuscript of the Srutaprakasika, Sudarsana Suri's commentary on Ramanuja's Sri Bhashya, by lying among the dead.

1. Twice a year

Deep inside the fifth enclosure, counting out from the sanctum, past the great gopurams and the crowds queuing for Ranganatha, there is a small shrine in what was once the Vasantha Mandapam. Inside it a man sits cross-legged in the posture of a teacher, hands folded in the gesture of knowledge. His skin has the colour of old turmeric. His eyes are open.

Twice a year, once in Aippasi and once in Chithirai, the priests of the shrine prepare a paste of green camphor and saffron and apply it to the figure, and they have done so for as long as anyone in the Koyil can remember, which the Koyil counts in centuries. No other substance goes on. The orange tint of the body, the devotees will tell you, is only the saffron.

The people of Srirangam do not call the seated figure a statue. Their name for it is tan ana tirumeni, "his own form", and by it they mean something plain and large. They mean that Ramanuja did not leave Srirangam when he died. He is in the shrine, and he has been in the shrine through everything that has happened to the island since.

2. The old man and the temple

By the tradition's count Ramanuja was born in 1017, in Sriperumbudur, and lived a hundred and twenty years. What is certain is that he spent most of the long middle of his life at Srirangam, because a dying teacher had asked him to.

The teacher was Yamunacharya, head of the Srirangam seat, who lay on his deathbed with three wishes unfulfilled and three fingers on his hand curled shut. A young man he had once seen only from a distance, at Kanchi, was sent for. The story says that as Ramanuja stood before the body and made three vows (to write a commentary on the Brahma Sutras, to name a disciple after the sage Parasara, to spread the teaching of the Alvars), the three fingers uncurled, one by one.

He came to Srirangam, and he stayed. The temple that he found was rich and badly run. He reorganised it with a thoroughness that the Koyil still remembers: the duties of the priests, the order of the festivals, the places of the recitation of the Tamil hymns beside the Sanskrit rites. When a Chola king who favoured another sect demanded that Ramanuja subscribe to a creed not his own, the disciple Kuresa put on his master's saffron robes and went in his place, and was blinded for it. Ramanuja crossed the hills to Karnataka and spent years in exile at Melkote before returning.

Then came the last years, and the question of what would become of him.

3. What the disciples did

His followers had known for some time that the end was close. The tradition records that when he felt it, Ramanuja gave final instructions to the leaders of the community, seventy-four of them, and then asked that his form be kept. Three images of him were made in his lifetime, it is said: one at Sriperumbudur, one at Melkote, and one at Srirangam. The first two have names that speak of his love for the places. The one at Srirangam was called tan ana, his own.

When he died, the disciples did what the Koyil holds that he himself had told them to do. They did not carry him to the river. The temple's own account says Namperumal appeared in a dream and named the place, the Vasantha Mandapam in the fifth enclosure. They laid him there in the posture of meditation and built the shrine around him. What the devotees of Srirangam say next is the part of the story that moves it from a burial to a wonder. They say that he did not decay. They say that the form which sits in the shrine is the form that was laid there, the one body of a great teacher that the temple would not let go.

That is the claim. It has been repeated in front of the shrine every day since, and it is the one thing the rest of this account turns on.

4. Smoke on the northern road

For nearly two centuries after his death nothing happened to the Koyil that was larger than its own festivals. Then the world changed, and it changed from the north.

The Delhi Sultanate had grown into a machine for taking treasure. In 1311 its general Malik Kafur marched through the Deccan and down into the Tamil country, where the old kingdoms were weak and the temples were full. What he did at Srirangam is not told clearly. The most one can say is that the temple was struck and then survived the blow, because the records speak of the images being replaced and the worshippers returning. It was a warning, and the Koyil heard it.

Twelve years later the warning became a march. Ulugh Khan, a prince of the Tughluq house who would one day be known as Muhammad bin Tughluq, came south with an army in 1323. His business was the conquest of the Madurai country. He took Warangal first, then turned toward the Pandya lands, and the road to Madurai ran by the Kaveri, past the island of Srirangam.

5. A festival day

The news reached the Koyil before the army did. The people who ran the temple had already learned what the north did to temples, and now they had a few days to decide what to save and what to risk. At the head of the Sri Vaishnava community stood an old man, Pillai Lokacharya, a teacher of great learning who had spent his life writing the doctrine of surrender in language that ordinary people could follow. He was past the age when anyone expects to run.

The accounts disagree on the details, and the disagreement is part of why the story is still argued over. They agree on its shape. The festival was under way. There were crowds in the courts. A decision was made to take the festival image, Namperumal, the processional form of Ranganatha who goes out among the people, and carry him away from the island. With him went his two consorts, Sri Devi and Bhu Devi, and a party of the faithful chosen for strength and for their ability to keep a secret.

The great sleeping Ranganatha could not be moved. He is stone and stucco, and he lies in the sanctum the size of a house. So the priests did what could be done. They built a wall in front of him. In front of the wall they set up a false image to draw the soldiers' eyes.

6. The wall and the road south

The soldiers came. The Koyil's chronicle says that the northern face of the shrine of Nammalvar was broken and that thousands of worshippers were cut down in the courts, a number the tradition gives as twelve thousand. The army stayed and used the temple as a camp, as soldiers will with a stone fortress that stands ready-made on an island.

In the sanctum, the false image was smashed. The wall held. Behind it the real Ranganatha lay unseen, as he had lain for centuries.

Outside the temple, in the middle of the carnage, there was an act that Srirangam has never forgotten. The tradition tells of a temple dancer named Vellayi who, as the story goes, led a Sultanate officer to the unfinished eastern gopuram on the pretext of showing him the temple's treasure, and then threw herself from the tower with him, to rid the temple of a man who had stayed behind to pull its towers down. The gopuram is whitewashed to this day, and it is the only white one in the temple. The Koyil says it is hers.

And through the same days, a small column of people moved south along the forest tracks with a bronze image carried among them. Pillai Lokacharya, old and failing, walked with it. They made for Madurai country, avoiding the roads, living off whatever the villages would give. When robbers came for the ornaments the old man talked to them, and the tradition says they turned away. He reached a hamlet called Jyotishkudi near Madurai, and there he fell ill. The tradition says that Namperumal spoke to him and promised liberation. He died there, and his disciples took the image onward without him.

Behind them, in the temple, the Koyil had divided itself into three parties. One would carry Namperumal south. One would stay and raise the wall in front of Ranganatha, led by an aged scholar named Sudarsana Suri. The third was to save whatever could be saved of the teaching itself, and the old man chose its keeper with care. Vedanta Desika was the youngest of the great acharyas then living, and Sudarsana Suri pressed him to go, so that at least one of them would survive to carry the doctrine of Ramanuja forward.

Into his hands the old scholar put two things. One was his two young sons. The other was the only manuscript of the Srutaprakasika, his own long commentary on Ramanuja's Sri Bhashya, built from generations of teaching handed down from teacher to pupil. Ramanuja's own great commentary on the Brahma Sutras lived on in other copies. This one was the lecture-room tradition that explained it, and it existed nowhere else.

Desika left with a group of the faithful and headed north. They were caught near Samayavaram, a few miles from the island, and many of them were cut down. The tradition says that Desika did the one thing left to do. He saw heaps of the dead lying around him, had the two boys lie down beneath some of the bodies, and lay down next to them with the book. The soldiers who passed in the dark took the living for the dead and went by. Before dawn he rose, gathered the boys, and slipped away. Sudarsana Suri did not live. He fell with the party that stayed behind to hold the wall.

Desika made his way to Satyamangalam in the hills, and the Koyil remembers that he carried the manuscript the whole way. He composed his hymn of fear and hope there, and spent years of exile in the hill country, and in Melkote, the old refuge of Ramanuja himself. Every scholar of the Sri Vaishnava school who opens the Sri Bhashya today does so with the Srutaprakasika beside it, and in the shrines that keep Desika's image he is shown with a hand raised in teaching and the manuscript held in the other.

7. The shrine behind the wall

Between the sanctum and the fifth enclosure there is a distance of a few hundred paces, and the written records are silent across all of it. The chronicles speak of the great wall before Ranganatha and the false image set in front of it. They say nothing of the shrine in the fifth enclosure. What follows is the temple's own tradition, told by the priests of the shrine, and no chronicle located for this piece confirms it.

The priests of Udaiyavar's shrine knew what the soldiers would do to a place like theirs. A seated teacher, covered in saffron, with open eyes, would draw the first blow from men who had come to break images, and there would be no second chance to carry him away. He could not be lifted and hidden as Namperumal could. So, the tradition says, they did the only thing the ground allowed. They raised a wall across the shrine, and they raised it to look like nothing. The plaster was weathered to match the older stone. The doorway was closed over, and the place where it had stood was made to read as blank enclosure wall, one more stretch of masonry in a temple that was made almost entirely of it. A soldier walking the fifth enclosure would see no shrine, only a stretch of blank wall.

The priests who did it did not leave records, and the Koyil does not remember their names. It remembers only that they stayed in the temple as long as they could, bringing no lamp near the false wall, and that behind it Ramanuja sat in the dark as he had sat for two centuries.

The camp came and went. The Madurai Sultanate rose on the ground the army had taken, and the south stayed under its rule for decades. The worship of the Koyil thinned and nearly stopped. When the Koyil's people returned, the wall was opened, and the seated figure was where he had been left, and the saffron was applied again.

The devotees say that this was no accident of geography. A body that had refused to decay was not something the Koyil would hand to soldiers, and the priests who sealed him in understood that. They say that he stayed, and that it was the staying that held the rest of the temple together. It is a story told with feeling, and it is the one that the Koyil tells.

8. Forty-eight years

For nearly five decades the festival image of Ranganatha was a refugee. After Jyotishkudi the party carried him through the Tirunelveli country, over the southern hills, and into Kerala, and from there he is said to have travelled north to Melkote in Karnataka, the very place where Ramanuja had once taken refuge, and then to Tirupati, where he waited. Nothing is easier to imagine than a company of exhausted priests carrying a god through a country at war, and nothing is harder to reconstruct from the records than where they slept.

In Srirangam the sanctum stayed behind its wall. In the Madurai Sultanate the temple was a ruin, and its priests were scattered.

Then the Sultanate's power began to fail. In the north of the peninsula a new kingdom, Vijayanagara, was rising, and its princes, Kampana among them, pushed south. A general named Gopana, a devout man, led the campaign that took Senji and then the Kaveri country. His intelligence came from local Vaishnavas who still knew the island, and the Sultanate garrison at Srirangam fell without a long fight. Kampana's wife, Gangadevi, wrote a poem about the war, and in it the temple appears as an abandoned place, grass in its courts and its gods gone.

9. The return

In 1371 Namperumal came home. A procession brought him from Tirupati, escorted by Gopana's troops. The tradition says that when the image arrived, there were people who doubted it was the original, because so many years had passed and so many hands had held it. An old man who had lived through the exodus, a washerman in some tellings and blind in others, was brought before the bronze, and said by touch that it was Namperumal. The account does not tell what he felt. It tells only that the doubt ended there.

Gopana's men took down the wall. Behind it Ranganatha was where he had been left. The temple was consecrated again, the festivals began again, and the lamps that had been dark for forty-eight years were lit. An inscription of that year records the restoration.

And in the fifth enclosure, an old priest brought the bowl of green camphor and saffron, as his predecessors had, and began the paste.

10. Still there

Walk the fifth enclosure today and you will find the shrine without trouble. The queue is shorter than at the sanctum and the light is lower. Inside, the seated figure is the colour of old turmeric, his hands folded in the gesture of knowledge, his eyes open on whoever has come.

The scholars of the Koyil have argued about the wonder, about the dates, about which of the chronicles is the most reliable and how many were killed in 1323. The arguments are real, and they belong in the notes. What the worshippers say is simpler. A teacher once promised never to leave the island. The island was burned, and the walls of the temple were broken, and the god himself was carried away into the forest. When everything was gone, the old man was still in his place. That, they say, is why the Koyil survived.

Sources and Evidence

The account of the 1323 flight and the return in 1371 rests on temple tradition and later chronicle, with a few firmer anchors. The numbers of the dead, the story of Vellayi and the exact route of Namperumal vary from one account to the next, and are told here as the tradition tells them.

Inscriptions and contemporary record

  • The inscription in the second enclosure of the Srirangam temple recording Gopana's restoration of Ranganatha in 1371, as summarised in the temple history on ramanuja.org.
  • Gangadevi's Madhura Vijayam, the Vijayanagara court poem of the southern campaign, which describes the temple as abandoned.
  • The works of Vedanta Desika, including the Abhiti Stava, written in the years after the sack.

Chronicle and tradition

  • The Koyil Olugu, the temple chronicle, compiled long after the events and mixing memory with devotion.
  • Sri Vaishnava accounts of Pillai Lokacharya and the exile of Namperumal, and of Desika's escape with the Srutaprakasika and Sudarsana Suri's two sons, as retold in the SriPedia archives and in M. Narasimhachary's monograph Sri Vedanta Desika (Sahitya Akademi).
  • The volunteer-run temple portal srirangam.net and temple guides, for the Udaiyavar shrine in the Vasantha Mandapam of the fifth enclosure, the twice-yearly saffron and camphor, and the names and order of the seven enclosures.
  • V. N. Hari Rao, The Srirangam Temple: Art and Architecture (1967), for the Delhi Sultanate occupation of the temple as a fortress camp.

Tradition of the shrine

The account of Ramanuja's body is the tradition of the Srirangam shrine, told here from the Koyil's own point of view, which is how the shrine's devotees understand it. Some Sri Vaishnava scholars read the image as fashioned over his interred relics, and the matter has never been settled by examination. The walling of the Udaiyavar shrine during the 1323 occupation is told as temple tradition: no written source located for this piece records it, and the text marks it as tradition where it appears.

Open points

Some Sri Vaishnava accounts place Desika back in Srirangam at the restoration under Gopana, yet his traditional dates (1268 to 1369) end two years before the 1371 return, so the timing of his homecoming is left open. The Srutaprakasika is the commentary of Sudarsana Suri on Ramanuja's Sri Bhashya, not a manuscript in Ramanuja's own hand. Social media threads and blog retellings were used only to find leads and are not relied on for any claim.
