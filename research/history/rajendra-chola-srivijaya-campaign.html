The Fleet That History Forgot: Rajendra Chola I's Campaigns Against Srivijaya and the Limits of the Conquest Narrative

In 1025 CE a Tamil dynasty sent a fleet across open ocean to raid a Southeast Asian empire, and the popular version of that story, from the "blue-water navy" to the royal marriage, does not survive contact with the primary sources.

Abstract

In 1025 CE, and, per recent scholarship, possibly as early as 1017 CE, the Chola emperor Rajendra I of southern India dispatched a maritime expedition against Srivijaya, a thalassocratic empire controlling the Strait of Malacca. The episode is unique in premodern South Asian history: a purely Indian polity projecting armed force across open ocean against a Southeast Asian state. Yet the popular understanding of this campaign, which holds that Rajendra deployed a standing "Chola Navy," achieved a total conquest of Srivijaya, and sealed a subsequent dynastic reconciliation through royal marriage, does not survive close contact with the primary and academic record. This paper argues that the 1025 campaign is best understood not as a naval conquest in the modern sense, but as an armada of repurposed commercial shipping (ships taken up from trade, or STUFT) deployed for a punitive raid whose precise causes remain genuinely contested among specialists, whose long-term political consequences were limited, and whose popular retelling, both historically and in a 2025 political commemoration, has consistently overstated what the sources support.

1. Introduction

Medieval historiography is not short of forgotten wars, but few are as strange in their obscurity as the naval campaign Rajendra Chola I launched against Srivijaya. A Tamil dynasty, more commonly associated with temple architecture and land revenue administration, assembled a fleet capable of crossing the Bay of Bengal and the Strait of Malacca, striking a dozen port-cities across what is now Malaysia, Sumatra, and the Nicobar Islands, and capturing a reigning king. No comparable act of Indian seaborne power projection would recur for centuries; if the campaign fits anywhere in a "great empires" canon dominated by Rome, the Mongols, and later European maritime powers, it does so awkwardly, and mostly not at all.1

This paper does not attempt to restore the campaign to popular memory through triumphalist retelling, a task increasingly attempted by others and discussed in Section 7, but to establish, as precisely as the surviving evidence allows, what can and cannot be said about it. Three questions organize the inquiry: What actually happened, according to contemporary inscriptions and external corroboration? Why did it happen, given that the primary sources are explicitly silent on motive? And what happened afterward: did the raid produce a lasting conquest, a diplomatic reconciliation, or something considerably less dramatic than either?

The method here follows a specific evidentiary discipline: primary epigraphic sources (Chola copper plates and temple inscriptions) are treated as authoritative for what they describe, but as court panegyric for why they describe it that way; independent external sources (Chinese Song-dynasty records) are used to test claims of Chola inscriptions against outside corroboration; and popular or tertiary claims lacking a traceable scholarly or primary source, however widely repeated, are treated as unverified until shown otherwise.

2. The World Before 1025: Two Trading Powers, One Cordial Relationship

The Chola dynasty, centered on the Kaveri river delta in present-day Tamil Nadu, reached its peak under Rajaraja I (r. 985–1014) and his son Rajendra I (r. 1014–1044), a period during which Chola military and administrative reach extended into Sri Lanka, the Deccan, and, under Rajendra, as far north as the Ganges river, commemorated by his new capital, Gangaikondacholapuram ("the city of the Chola who took the Ganges").2

Srivijaya, based at Palembang in southeastern Sumatra, was by the eleventh century a mature thalassocracy: a state whose power rested on control of maritime trade routes rather than territorial administration in the conventional sense. From the late seventh century onward, per O. W. Wolters' foundational study, Srivijaya worked to dominate the Strait of Malacca specifically to protect the commercial interests it had inherited from earlier trading polities on the same coast, and Wolters characterizes one of the main themes of the subsequent five hundred years of Srivijayan history as an escalating, and ultimately failing, effort to compel foreign merchants and seamen to conform to a system of maritime communications that Srivijaya itself had imposed.3 This is not incidental background: it establishes that Srivijaya's economic model depended on coercive control of shipping lanes for half a millennium before the Chola campaign. This was a structural condition, not a momentary policy, and any account of Chola motive must be read against it.

Critically, the two powers were not strangers or rivals at the dynasty's outset. The Larger Leiden Grant, a copper-plate charter dated to 1006 CE and formalized under Rajendra I around 1019, records Rajaraja I's donation of village revenues to the Chudamani Vihara, a Buddhist monastery at the Chola port of Nagapattinam built by the Srivijayan king Maravijayottunggavarman in memory of his father.4 A Shaivite Hindu emperor endowing a foreign Buddhist monastery is a striking data point in itself, but its evidentiary value here is narrower and more important: it dates cordial, formal Chola–Srivijaya diplomatic relations to within a generation of the first recorded attack (in the case of the 1019 formalization, mere years). Whatever drove the Cholas to war, it was not a longstanding enmity.

3. What the Inscriptions Actually Say

The most detailed contemporary account of the campaign appears on the walls of the Brihadisvara ("Big") Temple at Thanjavur, built during Rajendra I's reign. Per the translation given by K. A. Nilakanta Sastri, the inscription praises the king for having dispatched, in 1025, many ships into the open sea, and for having caught Sangrama-vijayottunga-varman, the king of Kadaram, along with the elephants of his army, before seizing the treasure that king had accumulated and capturing, with noise, the ceremonial gateway (Vidhyadhara-torana) at the "war gate" of Srivijaya's capital city.5

The inscription names twelve port-polities as targets, spanning the Malay Peninsula, Sumatra, and the Nicobar Islands: Pannai, Malaiyur, Mayirudingam, Ilangasoka, Mappapalam, Mevilimbangam, Valaippanduru, Talaittakkolam, Madamalingam, Ilamuridesam, Manakkavaram, and Kadaram itself.6 The identification of several of these names with modern locations (Mevilimbangam with Palembang, for instance) rests on epigraphic reconstruction dating to the late nineteenth century and should be treated as probable rather than certain.7 A separate, briefer record, the Tiruvalangadu copper plates (issued in Rajendra's fourteenth regnal year), corroborates the Kadaram conquest but adds little narrative detail; notably, it contains a verse comparing the general's overseas crossing favorably to Rama's construction of a bridge to Lanka in the Ramayana, a literary flourish that underscores the panegyric, rather than documentary, character of these texts.8

Two methodological points follow. First, both inscriptions are temple dedications commissioned by the victorious king; they are not neutral military records, and their function was to glorify Rajendra, not to transmit objective operational history to posterity. Second, and more consequentially for the historiography discussed in Section 7: nothing in either inscription specifies a cause for the campaign. As Kulke states directly, the sources are silent about the campaign's exact causes, a silence that has generated a genuine, ongoing scholarly debate rather than a settled explanation.9

3.1 Was There an Earlier Raid?

A significant complication to the standard "single campaign in 1025" narrative comes from Tansen Sen, who argues, using Chinese sources in addition to the Indian epigraphic record, that Rajendra Chola launched two attacks on Srivijayan territory: a smaller, less-documented raid in 1017 CE, and the well-known larger campaign in 1025.10 Virtually every popular account of this episode treats 1025 as a singular, unprecedented event. If Sen's reconstruction is correct, the 1025 campaign was not a bolt from the blue but an escalation of an already-initiated conflict, a materially different narrative shape than the one that circulates in nearly all popular retellings, including recent Indian state commemorations (Section 7).

4. Why Did It Happen? Four Theories, No Consensus

Because the primary sources do not state a motive, the causation question has generated multiple, non-consensus scholarly explanations. Presenting them honestly as competing hypotheses, rather than resolving them artificially, is itself a historiographical finding: a war large enough to be remembered for a thousand years remains, at the level of motive, genuinely unexplained.

Trade obstruction. K. A. Nilakanta Sastri, whose 1935 study of the dynasty remains the foundational academic history of the Cholas, proposed that the Cholas may have faced some attempt on the part of Srivijaya to obstruct Chola trade with the East.11 Sastri's own language here is tentative, signaling informed speculation rather than a documented finding.

Digvijaya (conquest for prestige). Sastri considered this explanation more likely than trade obstruction: a simple desire on Rajendra's part to extend his digvijaya to the countries across the sea, adding lustre to his crown.12 This reading fits a broader pattern in Rajendra's reign; his celebrated Ganges expedition of 1019–1021, commemorated by the very construction of Gangaikondacholapuram, established a template of long-distance campaigns undertaken substantially for dynastic prestige rather than territorial administration.13

Politics of plunder. George W. Spencer, in a 1983 study focused specifically on the Chola conquests of Sri Lanka and Srivijaya, situates the Southeast Asian campaign within an existing Chola pattern of resource-extraction raiding already practiced domestically in South India and in Sri Lanka.14 Spencer additionally suggests Rajendra may have been motivated in part by a need to match or exceed the achievements of his father, Rajaraja I, a legitimation dynamic also visible in the construction of the new capital and temple complex at Gangaikondacholapuram.15

Trade war (a specific mechanism). The most textually specific of the available theories comes from Tansen Sen, building on earlier suggestions by Sastri and Wolters. Sen cites the Zhufan Zhi ("Description of the Barbarians," compiled 1225 CE by the Chinese official Zhao Rugua), which records that Srivijaya compelled foreign ships to stop at its ports, attacking those that refused.16 On this reading, the Chola raids constituted an ambitious maneuver aimed at removing a hindrance from the trade route connecting South India to Song-dynasty China: a war fought over access to the most lucrative commercial artery in the medieval Indian Ocean world, rather than over prestige or plunder as such.17 Read against Wolters' account of Srivijaya's five-century pattern of coercing shipping through the Strait of Malacca (Section 2), Sen's mechanism has the strongest structural grounding of the four theories: it does not require positing a single triggering incident, only a mature, longstanding Srivijayan practice finally provoking pushback from a newly capable outside power.

Khmer alliance. A fourth, more circumstantial theory, advanced by Kenneth R. Hall and R. C. Majumdar, situates the conflict within a wider regional alliance structure: Suryavarman I of the Khmer Empire allegedly sought Chola assistance against the Tambralinga kingdom from around 1012 CE onward, while Tambralinga in turn sought Srivijayan backing, drawing Srivijaya into what was originally a Khmer-Tambralinga dispute.18 Both the Chola and Khmer courts were Shaivite Hindu, while Tambralinga and Srivijaya were Mahayana Buddhist, raising the possibility of a religious-alignment dimension to the conflict, though this remains a contested reading, not a scholarly consensus.19

No single theory commands the field. This paper's position is that the trade-war mechanism, given its grounding in both Chinese documentary evidence and Srivijaya's long-run coercive commercial behavior, is the best-evidenced of the four, but it should be presented as the leading hypothesis, not the settled answer.

5. What Kind of Fleet? Correcting the "Chola Navy" Myth

Popular accounts of this campaign, nearly without exception, describe a standing, purpose-built Chola navy, at times explicitly labeled one of history's first "blue-water" naval forces.20 This claim does not survive contact with the specialist literature.

Vijay Sakhuja and Sangeeta Sakhuja, writing specifically on the nautical dimensions of the campaign, state directly that the Chola kings did not maintain a navy comprising warships built exclusively for naval combat; instead, an armada was assembled from ships taken up from trade, known by the modern term STUFT.21 This is a materially different picture from the popular framing: the 1025 fleet was not a dedicated military instrument maintained in peacetime, but a wartime repurposing of commercial shipping: merchant vessels engaged, under royal authority, for a specific campaign.

The point is reinforced, more forcefully, by Y. Subbarayalu, the leading specialist on Chola epigraphy relevant to this question, who states that there is practically no explicit information from any contemporary source about the organization of the Chola navy, the nature of its vessels, the numerical strength of the warriors involved, or the port towns used as naval bases.22 Subbarayalu explains this evidentiary gap structurally: the overwhelming majority of surviving Tamil inscriptions are temple endowments and land-revenue records, not military documentation, and consequently the science and technology of the period cannot be recovered from the epigraphic record directly.23

This has direct consequences for widely circulated operational claims. Figures suggesting individual transport vessels carried 600–700 soldiers, or up to 100 horses, appear in secondary and tertiary sources but are not traceable to any primary inscription or peer-reviewed source; given Subbarayalu's explicit statement that troop strength is not recoverable from any contemporary record, these figures should be regarded as unsupported rather than merely unconfirmed.24 Similarly, this paper found no academic or primary source, as distinct from popular history writing, corroborating specific claims about sewn-plank ("stitched") hull construction for Chola military vessels, or a described diversionary-fleet tactic at the Strait of Malacca; both should be treated as unverified pending further research.25

External corroboration compounds the caution. Chinese Song-dynasty sources from this period, while documenting a Chola war-elephant corps, make no mention of a Chola navy at all.26 Whatever one makes of the significance of that silence, it is consistent with, not contradictory to, a picture of the 1025 campaign as an ad hoc commercial-fleet mobilization rather than the deployment of an established, internationally recognized naval institution.

6. Conquest, or Something Less? The Aftermath Reconsidered

The most consequential correction this paper offers concerns what happened after 1025. The popular narrative frequently extends beyond the raid itself into a reconciliation story: that Rajendra Chola married Onang Kiu, daughter of the defeated Srivijayan king, sealing a dynastic alliance that supposedly restored friendly relations within a generation.27

This narrative traces, in every version examined for this paper, to a single source: the Sejarah Melayu ("Malay Annals"), a Malay-language court chronicle compiled in the fifteenth century, approximately four hundred years after the events it purports to describe.28 The Annals refer to a conquering figure named "Raja Chulan" or "Raja Shulan," whom some, though not all, modern commentators identify with Rajendra Chola. Notably, even writers sympathetic to a grand narrative of Chola achievement treat this identification and the subsequent marriage claim with explicit caution: novelist Aditya Iyengar, whose own historical fiction dramatizes the campaign, describes the marriage claim as remaining in the realm of conjecture, given that the interpretation of historical texts is fluid rather than fixed.29 Absent independent corroboration from a source closer in time to the events, this paper treats the marriage-alliance narrative as unverified legend rather than established historical fact.

A separate and better-documented episode is easily conflated with these unverified claims but should be distinguished from them. In 1068, Virarajendra Chola (r. 1063–1070), son of Rajendra I, undertook a further campaign concerning Kedah (Kadaram), recorded in his own Tamil royal inscription and discussed by Nilakanta Sastri.30 Per Sastri, this campaign arose because a Kadaram prince approached Virarajendra for military assistance in securing the Kadaram throne: a dynastic-succession intervention, not, as sometimes loosely stated, a Chola fleet coming to Srivijaya's defense against an internal rebellion. Whether this prince can be understood as representing "Srivijaya" in a meaningful sense, or a by-then more independent Kedah, is itself contested; Sastri does not resolve the underlying "is Kadaram equivalent to Srivijaya" identification question that has recurred throughout this paper's source base.31 What this episode does establish, more modestly, is that the Cholas remained militarily and diplomatically engaged with the Kedah region for over four decades after 1025, a continuity of interest, though not proof of the specific reconciliation narrative built around it in popular retellings.

What can be said with more confidence, drawing on both the epigraphic silence after 1025 and the external Chinese record, is considerably less dramatic than either "total conquest" or "reconciliation by marriage." Chinese court records show Srivijaya's king sending tribute to the Song court as early as 1028, a mere three years after the Thanjavur inscription describes the kingdom as thoroughly despoiled, indicating that Srivijayan state structures survived the raid in some functional form, rather than being permanently dismantled.32 Continued Srivijayan diplomatic activity is recorded across the following decades, including missions in 1077, 1079, 1082, 1088, and 1090, some catalogued under a label ("Sanfoqi Zhu-nian guo," roughly "the Chola country of Sanfoqi") that suggests a period of Chola political influence over at least part of the former Srivijayan sphere, plausibly Kedah specifically, without indicating outright annexation of the wider Srivijayan realm.33

The most defensible aftermath claim, consistent across multiple lines of evidence, is one of continuity rather than either conquest or alliance: Tamil merchant guilds (the Manigramam, Ainnurruvar, and Ayyavole) expanded their presence in Southeast Asia in the years following the campaign, and Chola trade with both the former Srivijayan sphere and with Song China continued.34 This is, in fact, the outcome one would predict from the STUFT finding in Section 5: a fleet drawn from commercial shipping, deployed for a punitive raid rather than sustained occupation, would be expected to leave behind renewed and perhaps improved trading conditions rather than a client-state relationship requiring ongoing military presence. The 1025 campaign, on this reading, achieved a commercial objective, disrupting Srivijaya's capacity to obstruct Chola–China trade, without requiring, or producing, territorial conquest.

7. The Campaign Remembered: 2025 and the Politics of a Millennium

In 2025, the thousandth anniversary of the campaign became the occasion for a renewed, and contested, act of public memory. On 27 July 2025, Indian Prime Minister Narendra Modi presided over the closing ceremony of the Aadi Thiruvathirai festival at the Gangaikonda Cholapuram temple, a UNESCO World Heritage site built to commemorate Rajendra's earlier Ganges campaign, releasing a commemorative coin and describing the Chola dynasty as exemplifying one of India's "golden periods," citing its military strength, architectural achievement, and administrative innovation (the kudavolai electoral system).35

This commemoration drew explicit historiographical pushback. An August 2025 opinion piece in The Wire, citing Hermann Kulke's introduction to Nagapattinam to Suvarnadwipa directly, argued that the 1025 campaign should be understood as a unique event within an otherwise long, largely peaceful history of India–Southeast Asia cultural and commercial exchange, and criticized what it characterized as the political rebranding of a brutal eleventh-century war as a moment of civilisational glory.36

Both positions here are documented and citable: the government's commemorative framing is a matter of public record, and the historian's critique cites a named, peer-reviewed academic source. This paper does not adjudicate which framing is correct, consistent with the evidentiary approach applied throughout. What the episode illustrates, rather, is a pattern with wider relevance to the study of premodern history generally: an episode whose own contemporary sources are explicit about their silences (on cause, on ship technology, on aftermath) proves nonetheless highly serviceable to present-day political narrative, precisely because those silences leave room for confident retrospective claims that the eleventh-century evidence itself does not support.

8. Conclusion

Rajendra Chola I's campaign, or campaigns, against Srivijaya deserve wider recognition as a genuine anomaly in premodern Indian history: a Tamil dynasty projecting armed force across open ocean against a Southeast Asian maritime power, in an era when the reverse, Indian polities as targets rather than agents of foreign incursion, dominates the popular historical imagination. But recognition should not come at the cost of accuracy. The available primary and academic record supports a campaign that was likely fought at least twice (1017 and 1025), waged with a repurposed commercial fleet rather than a standing navy, motivated most plausibly, though not conclusively, by disputes over access to the Chola–China trade route, and followed not by conquest or dynastic reconciliation but by a resumption of ordinary commercial relations. Every departure from this picture in the popular record (the standing "blue-water navy," the specific troop and ship figures, the royal marriage) traces to sources of markedly lower evidentiary quality than the epigraphic and academic record this paper has attempted to center. A war large enough to be commemorated a thousand years later is, on the evidence, still a war whose basic causes and consequences remain genuinely open questions, a state of affairs the historical record should be allowed to state plainly, rather than resolved by the confidence of retelling.
