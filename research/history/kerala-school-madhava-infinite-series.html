Two and a Half Centuries Before Newton: Madhava, the Kerala School, and the Infinite Series Behind Calculus

In a small town on Kerala's Bharatapuzha river, a fourteenth-century astronomer derived the infinite series for pi, sine, and cosine that Europe would credit to Newton, Leibniz, and Gregory roughly 250 to 300 years later. His own writings are lost. What survives comes from students who kept teaching it for two more centuries, in one case writing out the full proofs in Malayalam, before a British official found the texts in 1835 and mathematicians spent the better part of two centuries figuring out what to do with what he'd found.

Abstract

Between roughly 1350 and 1425 CE, the astronomer-mathematician Madhava of Sangamagrama, working in what is now Kerala, derived infinite series expansions for the sine, cosine, and arctangent functions, and used them to compute pi to eleven correct decimal places, a level of precision not surpassed in Europe until well into the eighteenth century. The specific series Madhava worked out are today taught in calculus classrooms worldwide under the names of the European mathematicians who rederived them independently two and a half to three centuries later: James Gregory in 1671, Gottfried Leibniz in 1673, and results equivalent to parts of Isaac Newton's 1665-66 work on infinite series. Madhava's own writings do not survive. What is known of them comes down through a line of successors at what historians now call the Kerala School of Astronomy and Mathematics, most importantly Nilakantha Somayaji, whose 1501 Tantrasangraha systematized the results, and Jyeshthadeva, whose Yuktibhasa, composed around 1530 in Malayalam rather than Sanskrit, is very likely the only pre-modern mathematical text anywhere that presents full derivations, not just stated results, for findings of this sophistication. This paper lays out what the Kerala School actually demonstrated, situates it within the longer Indian astronomical tradition that produced it, and addresses, without resolving, the genuinely open historical question of whether any of this knowledge reached Europe before Newton, Leibniz, and Gregory did their own independent work. That transmission question remains unsettled. Madhava's priority does not.

1. Introduction

Every first-year calculus student eventually meets the Gregory-Leibniz series, the beautifully simple statement that pi divided by four equals one minus a third plus a fifth minus a seventh, on and on forever, each term a little closer to the truth. It's usually presented as a seventeenth-century European achievement, James Gregory working it out in Scotland in 1671, Gottfried Leibniz arriving at the same result independently in Germany two years later. Fewer students, and not many more mathematicians, are told that the identical result, along with the infinite series for sine and cosine that underpin much of trigonometric calculus, had already been worked out roughly three hundred years earlier by an astronomer working on the banks of a river in what is now Kerala, in southwestern India.

That astronomer was Madhava of Sangamagrama, and the case for what he accomplished is not speculative or reconstructed from fragmentary hints. It rests on texts, composed by his direct mathematical descendants, that state his results explicitly, attribute them to him by name, and in the most remarkable of those texts, walk through exactly how he must have derived them. The Cambridge-trained historian of mathematics George Gheverghese Joseph, whose research on this topic is among the most extensively cited in the field, has put the historiographical point plainly: the priority of the Kerala School's work in this specific area of calculus is, in his assessment, no longer seriously in question among specialists.1 This paper is an attempt to explain, in some technical detail, what exactly that work was, how unusually well documented it is compared to most medieval mathematics, and what remains genuinely uncertain about the story, which is not whether Madhava got there first, but whether anyone in Europe ever found out.

2. Madhava and the Kerala School

Madhava was born around 1340 in Sangamagrama, a settlement generally identified with present-day Irinjalakuda in Kerala's Thrissur district, and died around 1425.2 He held the title Golavid, "master of spherics," reflecting his primary vocation: like nearly every serious mathematician in pre-modern India, Madhava was first and foremost an astronomer, a jyotirvid working within a scholarly tradition whose central practical task was tracking the positions of the sun, moon, and planets accurately enough to fix the Hindu calendar, predict eclipses, and time religious observances correctly. The mathematics, however sophisticated it became in its own right, grew out of that astronomical project rather than existing separately from it.2

None of Madhava's own mathematical writings have survived. What is known of his work comes entirely through citation and transmission: later mathematicians in the tradition he founded, referred to collectively as the Kerala School of Astronomy and Mathematics, quote his results, attribute specific derivations to him by name, and in some cases preserve short Sanskrit verses stating his findings in the compressed, mnemonic style typical of the genre. The most important of these successors was Nilakantha Somayaji (1444-1544), writing roughly a century after Madhava, whose 1501 treatise Tantrasangraha organized and extended the school's astronomical and mathematical results into a systematic treatise, explicitly crediting Madhava as the source of its key series expansions.3 Historian K. V. Sarma, whose decades of work cataloguing and translating Kerala School manuscripts underpins most modern scholarship on the subject, traced a continuous line of astronomer-mathematicians from Madhava through Nilakantha and beyond, spanning roughly the fourteenth through the sixteenth centuries.4

3. The Discoveries Themselves

The core of Madhava's work, as reconstructed from later citations, is a set of infinite series representing trigonometric quantities as sums that, in principle, run forever, with each additional term nudging the total closer to the exact value. The most famous is what is now called, in Kerala School scholarship, the Madhava-Leibniz series: pi divided by four equals the sum of 1, minus one-third, plus one-fifth, minus one-seventh, and so on, an identity Madhava derived from a power series expansion of the arctangent function centuries before Gregory and Leibniz independently found the same result.5 Alongside it, Madhava developed infinite series expansions for the sine and cosine functions, results that would not be matched in Europe until later still, and that are the direct ancestors of what today are called the Taylor series for sine and cosine, though "Newton" and "Taylor" are the names most calculus textbooks still attach to them.6

What sets Madhava's work apart from being merely an early approximation is the sophistication of how he handled the series' convergence. The raw Madhava-Leibniz series converges slowly, requiring enormous numbers of terms for high precision. Madhava developed correction terms, essentially early convergence-acceleration techniques, that let a modest number of terms yield extraordinary accuracy. Using one such correction and twenty-one terms of his series, he computed pi to eleven correct decimal places, 3.14159265359, a result not bettered anywhere in the world for roughly two hundred years.7 A separate value correct to thirteen decimal places is sometimes attributed to Madhava as well, though the more careful scholarly judgment is that this refinement likely belongs to one of his successors rather than to Madhava himself, a distinction this paper preserves rather than collapsing for effect.7 Madhava also produced a table of sine values at twenty-four equal intervals across a quarter circle, accurate to the seventh decimal place, a level of numerical precision that had no real practical application in the astronomy of his day and appears to reflect mathematical ambition for its own sake as much as observational necessity.8

These are not isolated curiosities. Together they represent, in the judgment of mathematicians who have studied the surviving texts closely, the conceptual foundation for treating trigonometric functions as infinite sums, and for using the passage to an infinite number of terms as a tool for exact calculation rather than a philosophical curiosity to be avoided, the same conceptual move at the heart of what would later be formalized in Europe as calculus.1

4. Reading the Actual Verses

It's worth pausing on what the underlying mathematics actually looks like, because the modern notation makes it recognizable immediately to anyone who has taken a calculus class, and the original verses make clear that these results were being stated with real precision, not gestured at. In the notation used today, the three central results attributed to Madhava are:

arctan(x)  =  x − x³/3 + x⁵/5 − x⁷/7 + ...
sin(x)  =  x − x³/3! + x⁵/5! − x⁷/7! + ...
cos(x)  =  1 − x²/2! + x⁴/4! − x⁶/6! + ...

The three Madhava series in modern notation. Setting x = 1 in the first equation gives π/4 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + ..., the identity now usually credited to Gregory (1671) and Leibniz (1673).56

What the surviving Sanskrit verses actually say is more specific than a modern reader might expect from a 600-year-old mathematical text. The arctangent series is preserved in verses 2.206 through 2.209 of the Yukti-dipika commentary on the Tantrasangraha, in a full scholarly English translation published by historians R. C. Gupta and K. V. Sarma. Rendered here exactly as translated from the Sanskrit:

"The first result is the product of the desired sine and the radius divided by the cosine of the arc. When one has made the square of the sine the multiplier and the square of the cosine the divisor, now a group of results is to be determined from the (previous) results beginning from the first. When these are divided in order by the odd numbers 1, 3, and so forth, and when one has subtracted the sum of the even(-numbered) results from the sum of the odd (ones), that should be the arc."5

Translated out of its geometric phrasing, that is a complete, general construction of the arctangent power series: form successive powers of (sine/cosine), divide each by the corresponding odd number, and alternately add and subtract. It is the same operation as the modern formula above, expressed without algebraic symbols because the notation for expressing it algebraically didn't exist yet in that tradition, not because the underlying idea was any less precise.

The same source records a second, separate result: a reformulated version of the series, substituting the tangent of 30° in place of 1, which converges far faster than the plain alternating series above. Rendered from the same verses:

"The first result should be the square root of the square of the diameter multiplied by twelve. From then on, the result should be divided by three (in) each successive (case). When these are divided in order by the odd numbers, beginning with 1, and when one has subtracted the (even) results from the sum of the odd, (that) should be the circumference."5

In modern notation, that verse describes computing the circumference c of a circle of diameter d as c = √(12d²) × [1 − 1/(3·3) + 1/(3²·5) − 1/(3³·7) + ...], equivalent to substituting x = tan(30°) = 1/√3 into the arctangent series and using π = 6·arctan(1/√3). The substitution matters enormously for how quickly the calculation converges. The plain series (x = 1) shrinks so slowly that reaching eleven correct decimal digits would require on the order of a hundred billion terms. The tan(30°) version shrinks roughly by a factor of three with each additional term, so the same eleven digits of accuracy fall out after only about twenty terms, which lines up with the twenty-one-term, correction-adjusted calculation discussed above that produced Madhava's value of pi.7 Choosing that specific substitution, out of the infinitely many available, is not the kind of thing a person stumbles into. It is a deliberate piece of numerical engineering.

One more detail from the same textual tradition is worth including simply because it's such a specific, checkable artifact of how this material was actually transmitted. Verse 2.437 of the same commentary preserves a mnemonic for the correction terms behind Madhava's eleven-decimal value of pi, encoded using the katapayadi system, a scheme in which Sanskrit consonants stand for digits, so that a numerical result can be packed into a memorable, chantable phrase. The verse reads:

vidvān, tunnabala, kavīśanicaya, sarvārthaśīlasthiro, nirviddhānganarēndrarung

Decoded through the katapayadi cipher, those five words are not poetry at all. They are the numbers 44, 33′06″, 16′05″41‴, 273′57″47‴, and 2220′39″40‴, arc-minute values Madhava needed for his correction-term calculation of pi, packaged into a phrase a student could memorize by sound rather than needing to copy out a table of digits by hand.7 It is, among other things, a reminder that "oral tradition" in this context did not mean approximate or unreliable; it meant a memorization technology precise enough to carry an eleven-digit calculation intact across generations of students with no printing press involved anywhere in the chain.

5. The Yuktibhasa: Proofs, Not Just Results

Most of what survives from the Kerala School, and from Indian mathematics generally in this period, follows a well-established regional convention: results are stated as compressed verse, often in Sanskrit, meant to be memorized and expanded upon orally by a teacher rather than serving as a self-contained written derivation. That makes one text in the Kerala School's tradition genuinely unusual, not just for India but by the standards of pre-modern mathematics worldwide.

The Yuktibhasa, composed by Jyeshthadeva around 1530, breaks from convention in two ways simultaneously. First, it is written in Malayalam, the regional spoken language of Kerala, rather than Sanskrit, making it accessible to a readership well beyond the Sanskrit-trained scholarly elite. Second, and more remarkably, it does not simply state the Kerala School's results. It derives them, step by step, providing what specialists in the history of mathematics describe as complete logical justifications, arguments that function as genuine mathematical proofs even though they don't share the specific axiomatic, Euclidean-style format that later became standard in the Western tradition.12 Historian M. D. Srinivas, examining what "proof" meant within the Indian mathematical tradition on its own terms rather than measuring it against a foreign standard, has argued that Yuktibhasa-style derivations satisfy the core demands of mathematical proof: they establish results through valid, checkable logical steps rather than asserting them on authority.13 One historical account of the text puts the significance bluntly: the Yuktibhasa may be the only mathematics book in any local, non-scholarly language from this period anywhere in the world that contains complete proofs of everything it states, and a meaningful share of those results were not independently derived in Europe until two centuries or more afterward.14

This matters for more than nationalist bragging rights on either side of the transmission debate discussed below. A result stated without derivation can always be dismissed as a lucky guess, a numerical pattern noticed without being understood. A result derived step by step, with the underlying reasoning preserved in a language ordinary readers could follow, cannot be waved away that easily. The Yuktibhasa is the strongest piece of evidence that what happened in Kerala between the fourteenth and sixteenth centuries was genuine mathematical understanding, not a fortunate coincidence of arithmetic.

6. Part of a Longer Tradition

Madhava did not work in a vacuum, and treating him as a solitary genius who appeared from nowhere undersells the tradition that produced him as much as ignoring him entirely does. Aryabhata, writing in 499 CE, had already computed pi to four correct decimal places in his Aryabhatiya, explicitly describing his value as an approximation, asanna, rather than an exact figure, itself a notable piece of mathematical self-awareness for the period.9 Roughly six centuries later, Bhaskara II, working far to the north in present-day Karnataka, described in his 1150 Siddhanta Shiromani ideas that anticipate specific components of calculus: an early treatment of instantaneous rates of change in astronomical contexts, and reasoning about quantities approaching a limiting value.15 Madhava's achievement was to take this centuries-long thread of Indian astronomical mathematics and push it decisively further, from approximation and limiting cases toward the systematic use of genuinely infinite processes as calculation tools.

The tradition continued to develop after Madhava as well, not merely preserve his results. Nilakantha Somayaji, beyond compiling and extending the series work in the Tantrasangraha, also proposed a revised planetary model in which Mercury and Venus orbit the sun while the sun in turn orbits the Earth, a partial departure from the standard geocentric model of the period that some historians of astronomy consider a meaningful conceptual step toward the kind of hybrid planetary models later associated with Tycho Brahe in sixteenth-century Europe.16 None of this was mathematics for its own sake in the modern academic sense. Every figure in this tradition, from Aryabhata through Madhava to Nilakantha, was first and foremost an astronomer working to serve a religious and civil calendar that depended on getting planetary positions right.

7. Did It Reach Europe?

The one genuinely open question in this story is not whether Madhava's results predate their European counterparts. That chronology is settled: the Kerala School texts carry internal and cross-referential dating that places them solidly in the fourteenth through sixteenth centuries, a hundred and fifty to three hundred years before Newton, Leibniz, and Gregory did their own, independent work. The open question is whether any of that knowledge crossed paths with European scholarship before 1671, and if so, whether it had any influence on what happened next.

The case for a possible connection is circumstantial but not frivolous. Cochin, close to the heart of the Kerala School's activity, was a major and well-established center of European, especially Jesuit, presence in India from the sixteenth century onward, and Jesuit missionaries of the period were frequently mathematically literate, in some cases specifically tasked with gathering local scientific and astronomical knowledge to send back to Europe.17 A 2007 research collaboration between the Universities of Manchester and Exeter, led by George Gheverghese Joseph and Dennis Almeida, argued that this circumstantial case, missionary presence, documented European interest in Indian astronomical calculation, and the timing of when Kerala School results would have been available versus when equivalent results appeared in Europe, was strong enough to warrant taking a transmission route seriously rather than dismissing it out of hand.18 Other scholars working on the same textual tradition, including Kim Plofker's detailed technical comparisons of specific sine-approximation methods across Indian and later European sources, have generally urged more caution, noting that structural similarity between two mathematical results is not, by itself, proof of a documented transmission chain, since mathematicians working on closely related astronomical problems can and do arrive at similar techniques independently.19 No direct documentary link, a translated manuscript, a missionary's letter describing the specific series, a citation trail connecting a named Jesuit scholar to a named European mathematician, has yet surfaced publicly connecting the Kerala texts to Gregory, Leibniz, or Newton specifically.

This paper takes no position on how that question will ultimately be resolved, because the honest answer is that it has not been resolved yet, and may never be, given how much depends on records that may simply not survive. What can be said with confidence is that the question is worth asking seriously rather than dismissing, and that however it turns out, the answer changes nothing about the prior question this paper is actually about. Madhava's priority does not depend on Newton having read him.

8. Conclusion

What survives of Madhava's work reached the wider scholarly world almost by accident. Charles Whish, a British civil servant with a serious interest in Indian astronomy, encountered the relevant Kerala manuscripts and, in 1835, published a paper in the Transactions of the Royal Asiatic Society describing what he had found, "the Hindu quadrature of the circle," as his title called it, and the infinite series it rested on.20 The paper drew little sustained attention for over a century. It was not until the mid-twentieth century, through the painstaking manuscript work of scholars like K. V. Sarma and the analytical work of C. T. Rajagopal and collaborators, and later the broader popularizing scholarship of George Gheverghese Joseph, that the Kerala School's achievement began receiving anything close to the attention its content warrants.10

The core facts of this story do not need embellishment to be remarkable. A line of astronomer-mathematicians working in a corner of southwestern India, over roughly two centuries, developed the specific infinite series that would later anchor European calculus, computed pi to a precision unmatched anywhere else in the world for two hundred years, and in at least one text, wrote out the full logical reasoning behind their results in the language ordinary readers of the region could actually follow. Whether that knowledge ever crossed the ocean is a real historical question, worth continuing to investigate. Whether it happened first in Kerala is not a question at all. It is simply where the record points.
