The Tiger Banner: The Cholas from Ashoka's Edicts to the Last Dated Year

From a name on Ashoka's rock to a year number on a temple wall, the story of a dynasty that came and went in the Kaveri delta, told through what the stone proves, what the poems remember, and where the two disagree.

1. Before the Empire

See this chapter on the atlas →

The first person to write the Cholas down never ruled them. In the third century BCE the emperor Ashoka had his edicts cut into rock across the subcontinent, and two of them, the second and thirteenth, look south past his frontier to list the neighbours: the Cholas, the Pandyas, the Satiyaputras and Keralaputras, and Tamraparni, usually read as Sri Lanka. The lists give names and nothing else, with no kings, no capitals and no dates. They are still the firmest footing the early Chola story ever gets, words fixed in stone during the lifetime of the man who ordered them, and they show that Pataliputra already saw the far south as a handful of distinct peoples. Tamil tradition would later speak of three crowned kings. Those three crowns hold the story in place for the next thousand years: the Cholas in the Kaveri delta, the Pandyas around Madurai on the Vaigai, and the Cheras on the western side, in what is now Kerala and the Kongu uplands.

Delta and port

The Chola country was the delta, flat silt land where the Kaveri fans out toward the Bay of Bengal. If the poems are right, its inland seat was Uraiyur, today a locality inside Tiruchirappalli, and its port was Kaveripattinam, also called Puhar or Poompuhar, at the river mouth in what is now Mayiladuthurai district. Which of the two served as the royal seat is unsettled, and it probably shifted with the reign.

The port can at least be tested against the ground, and it passes. Digs since the 1960s have turned up rouletted ware, amphora fragments, foreign pottery and coins, so Puhar did trade across the sea. Underwater surveys off the coast have added a West Asian torpedo jar and ring wells. Ring wells are dug on dry land, which means part of the old town now lies beneath the water, perhaps after floods or a tsunami between the third and fifth centuries CE, though nothing has fixed the cause. One radiocarbon date from a single wooden log has been quoted for settlement as early as the fifth or fourth century BCE. A lone sample is a thin thread for that.

What the poets remember

Where the ground stops talking, the poems start. Pattinappalai describes Puhar and its king Karikala. Porunararruppadai is a guide for bards hoping to win his patronage, and scattered verses in the Purananuru and Akananuru anthologies fill in the rest. This is where the Chola kings first become people. It is also court poetry, written to praise, selective by design, and without a single regnal year. Scholars have argued about its dates for generations, and a king like Karikala can slide by a century or more depending on whose chronology you accept.

That settles an order of trust for everything that follows. Stone and excavation come first. Poetry comes second, and it is better for what a society believed about itself than for when things happened. Anything first told centuries later comes last, and the Cholas themselves were among the later tellers: medieval Chola grants name Karikala as an ancestor and sometimes hand him deeds the early poems never mention.

The neighbours in stone

Stone does exist for the neighbours. In the Mangulam caves near Madurai, a Tamil-Brahmi inscription records a gift made during the reign of the Pandya king Netunceliyan. Mahadevan dated it to the second century BCE, and others prefer the third. At Pugalur, near Karur, another inscription names three generations of a Chera line, and by Mahadevan's reading it ties the Sangam poems to the early centuries CE. Far to the north, in Odisha, Kharavela of Kalinga boasts in the Hathigumpha inscription of breaking up a confederacy of Tamil states and of receiving pearls and horses from a Pandya king. How old that confederacy was, and when Kharavela lived, are both argued over.

Look closely at what these inscriptions name and a gap opens. They give Pandya kings and Chera kings. So far as the published record shows, nobody has found an early Tamil-Brahmi inscription that names a Chola one. The Cholas of this age live in poems and in Ashoka's list, and not yet in their own stone.

A town on the Vaigai

The richest ground evidence from the early Tamil country has come, ironically, from Pandya territory. Keeladi (also spelt Keezhadi) is a village on the Vaigai in Sivaganga district, about a dozen kilometres south-east of Madurai. The Archaeological Survey of India began digging there and the Tamil Nadu State Department of Archaeology carried the work on. They found brick structures, drains, ring wells, bead-making debris and potsherds scratched with Tamil-Brahmi letters. A charcoal sample from about 3.5 metres down was dated by Beta Analytic in the United States to roughly 580 BCE, and the state's 2019 report puts the cultural deposits between the sixth and first centuries BCE. To some scholars that reads as a second urbanisation, in the south, running parallel to the one in the Gangetic plain.

The dating is contested, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. The Survey has argued for a more cautious chronology, closer to the period after 300 BCE, and in 2025 it asked the lead archaeologist, K. Amarnath Ramakrishna, to revise his 982-page report on the first two seasons. He has defended his findings. Critics add that a single dated sample cannot prove writing existed in the sixth century BCE. What nobody seriously disputes is that Keeladi was a planned, literate-looking town, and whatever its exact age, the Tamil country had towns long before the temple age. It confirms no battle, no king and no dynasty. It shows the world those kings ruled.

On the coast near Puducherry, Arikamedu offers the classic evidence of contact with the Roman world and is often loosely labelled a Chola port. The poems link the neighbouring harbour of Virai to the Velir chiefs, so the site is better seen as a neighbour of the Chola country than as a mark of Chola power.

Venni

Into this world the poems bring Karikala, and with him the one military event that several of them agree on.

The story runs like this. Two crowned kings, the Pandya and the Chera, gather against him, and eleven lesser chieftains, many of them probably Velir lords with local land, stand with them. They meet at a place called Venni, and Karikala wins. The Chera king takes a wound in the back, which for a warrior is a disgrace. He sits facing north and starves himself to death, a recognised honourable end in the poems, and some of his companions go with him. Porunararruppadai credits Karikala with beating both crowns there, and verses in the anthologies carry the rest.

Venni is generally identified with Kovilvenni, a town near Needamangalam in Thiruvarur district, about 24 kilometres from Thanjavur. That puts the fight inside the delta, on Chola ground, which suits a coalition coming to him. The date wobbles, with some sources giving about 190 CE and others about 130. The Chera king, called Perum Cheralathan in the battle poems, is sometimes equated with Uthiyan Cheralathan, the earliest Chera ruler the poems name, though that is a scholar's judgement and not a given. The safest phrasing is the second century CE, give or take decades.

Here the order of trust bites. No inscription from the time mentions the battle, and as far as the published record shows, Kovilvenni has not been dug to test the story. What survives is a battle that several poets told the same way, which counts for something and still falls short of proof. The older reading says the victory secured Karikala's throne and gave the Cholas a kind of precedence among the three crowns. It rests on praise poems, which carry the mood well and the mechanism badly. Nobody says what the win changed, only that the poets thought it mattered.

Later tradition piled more onto him: a march north that planted the tiger banner on the Himalaya, the defeat of the Pallavas of Kanchi, campaigns in Sri Lanka. These stories first show up in grants written long afterwards, and one account puts his Pallava war around 350 CE, which cannot sit beside a Venni in the second century. They belong to legend until an earlier source says otherwise.

Not always the Cholas

Reading Venni on its own makes the dynasty look inevitable, and the poems do not allow it. The Pandya king Nedunjeliyan II is remembered for a victory at Talaiyalanganam over a coalition of Cholas, Cheras and five other rulers. What the poems preserve is constant realignment more than a single power on the rise. Each of the three crowns has its famous win, and each appears as the beaten partner in someone else's.

The river and the weir

What all three kingdoms needed, and the Cholas had in greatest supply, was water. Popular accounts credit Karikala with the Kallanai, the Grand Anicut, a low weir of unhewn stone about 329 metres long on the Kaveri near Tiruchirappalli, where the Kollidam branches off. The early poems give him flood banks along the river. Tying him to the stone weir came later, and popular dates for the first version run from the third century BCE to the second century CE, with no inscription of that age behind any of them. The structure has been reworked many times. Captain Caldwell remodelled it in 1804, and Arthur Cotton's engineering from the 1830s included the Lower Anicut across the Kollidam.

Whoever laid the first stones, the delta's rice economy depended on managing this river, and that economy paid for everything the Cholas later did.

The quiet

After the second or third century CE the record around the Cholas goes silent. When other dynasties' inscriptions pick the delta up again, the people holding it are not Cholas.

2. The Long Eclipse

See this chapter on the atlas →

The silence after Karikala is long, and almost nobody who speaks into it was there. The earliest voice that was comes from a monk.

Buddhadatta, a Buddhist scholar of the fifth century who wrote in Pali, lived at Bhutamangalam in the Chola country, and one study ties him to Uraiyur. In passing he names a ruler, Achyutavikranta of the Kalabhra clan, governing the Chola country from Kaveripattinam. That single incidental line, from a man living under the rule he mentions, is about all we have from the Kalabhra age by someone who stood inside it. It carries a second hint. A Pali scholar working in the delta suggests that learned life went on there, at least among Buddhists.

Everyone else tells the story afterwards. Tradition says the Kalabhras displaced the three old crowns between about the third and sixth centuries, and the label that stuck, the Kalabhra interregnum, is defined mostly by what is missing. Who they were is still argued. H. Krishna Sastri, editing the third volume of South Indian Inscriptions in 1920, tucked a guess into a footnote. He had noticed that an eighth-century chief at Thanjavur bore the title Kalvar-Kalvan, and he wondered whether that word was the root of the name Kalabhra, which would make the Muttaraiyar chiefs who held Thanjavur before the Cholas retook it a possible remnant of the same family. He offered it as a guess, and it should be read as one.

The fullest telling comes from the Pandya side, and it comes late. The Velvikudi plates, a copper-plate grant of about 770 CE issued under Nedunjadaiyan, found near Madurai and now in the British Museum, say that the Pandya king Kadungon freed his country from the Kalabhras the way the sun breaks out of dark clouds. They were written some two centuries after Kadungon, and a recent journal article reads them as the voice of a court asserting Brahmanical values, with the Kalabhras cast as heterodox outsiders. A grant like that shows how the Pandya court wanted its past remembered. How the Kalabhras actually governed is another question, and the plates do not answer it.

North of the river

While the Pandyas rebuilt in the south, a new power was settling in at Kanchipuram. The Pallavas enter the southern story with Simhavishnu, placed in the later sixth century, and once again the fullest account of him was written long after. It sits on the Kasakudi plates, eleven sheets of copper strung on a ring and sealed with the Pallava bull, dug up in 1879 at Kasakudi, about four kilometres from Karaikal. E. Hultzsch edited them in 1891. They record a village grant made in the twenty-second year of the Pallava king Nandivarman Pallavamalla, a contemporary of the Chalukya king Vikramaditya II, who ruled from 733 to 746. So the plates are an eighth-century document looking back on sixth-century events.

Verse 20 says Simhavishnu defeated the Malaya, Kalabhra, Malava, Chola, Pandya, Simhala and Kerala kings. Hultzsch gave three reasons for caution. By Nandivarman's day, he noted, the court remembered little about Simhavishnu's predecessors, so its sense of early Pallava history was already hazy. He thought Nandivarman himself was probably a distant kinsman who took the throne after the legitimate line ended, "chosen by the subjects" in the plates' own phrase, which gives him every reason to polish the family record. And the Udayendiram plates repeat the same list of kings, but Hultzsch suspected they were a later copy, so they cannot be counted as a second witness.

In that telling the Chola country is one more name on a list of the defeated, wedged between Kalabhra and Pandya. By the end of the sixth century the Kaveri had become a frontier, with the Pallavas on the northern side, the Pandyas on the southern, and the delta between them. Kadungon's dates differ by some decades from table to table, and the Velvikudi plates remain the only account of his work.

Crossing the Kaveri

The seventh century brought a third power. The Chalukyas of Badami, in what is now Karnataka, were the Pallavas' great rivals, and their court poet Ravikirti left the one contemporary account that reaches into the Chola country. He composed the Aihole inscription for Pulakeshin II in 634 CE, and Hultzsch's translation preserves it.

The verses read like a march south. In verse 29 the Chalukya army raises so much dust that the Pallava king's splendour fades and vanishes behind the walls of Kanchipura. Verse 30 turns toward the Cholas. When Pulakeshin set out to conquer them, the Kaveri found her current blocked by a causeway of his elephants and shrank from the sea. Verse 31 then closes the campaign on a surprise: he brought great prosperity to the Cholas, Keralas and Pandyas, a sun to the hoar-frost of the Pallavas. The poem is a eulogy, so these are Chalukya claims. Even so, the Chola country in 634 was a place an army could reach by crossing a river, and in the Chalukya telling it came out of the encounter better than the Pallavas did.

The Pallavas answered in their own plates. Verse 21 of the Kasakudi grant says Mahendravarman I wiped out his chief enemies at Pullalura, a village in the Kanchipuram area, and Hultzsch took those enemies to be the Chalukyas. Verse 22 says his son Narasimhavarman I conquered Lanka and took Vatapi, the Chalukya capital. Pulakeshin II's latest known date is 642, which fits a Pallava claim that he fell around then.

The Lankan claim has an outside check. The Mahavamsa, chapter 47, tells of a Sinhalese prince, Manavamma, who lived at the court of a king called Narasimha in India and helped him against a rival called Vallabha, which was Pulakeshin's title. The king then gave Manavamma an army to take Ceylon, and the second attempt succeeded. Hultzsch thought the identification with Narasimhavarman I could hardly be doubted. A Sinhalese chronicle confirming a Pallava boast is as close to independent corroboration as these five centuries ever come.

The ancestor they needed

Medieval grants and later poems credit Karikala with wars against the Pallavas and with settling the Telugu country. One textbook account places his attack on Kanchi in the reign of a Pallava king named Trilochana, around 350 CE. The Venni of the poems falls about 190. Both cannot belong to the same man unless one date is off by a century and a half. The likelier explanation is that the Pallava-war stories were attached to Karikala later, when the dynasty needed an ancestor who could have fought Kanchi.

So the eclipse ends without a single Chola king speaking in his own voice. The dynasty returns in the middle of the ninth century, in the delta, in the person of a chief called Vijayalaya.

3. The Revival

See this chapter on the atlas →

By the middle of the ninth century Thanjavur belonged to a family of chiefs called the Muttaraiyar. The proof is a pillar inscription at Sendalai, cut in the eighth century, which styles one of them, Maran Perumbidugu Muttaraiyan, lord of Tanjai. They held the town while Pallavas and Pandyas fought over everything around it.

Around 850 it changed hands. The man responsible was Vijayalaya, who bore the title Parakesarivarman and stands at the head of the revived Chola line. The records that tell us so are later, and they say nothing about his victim. The Tiruvalangadu plates, issued under Rajendra I in the eleventh century, say he captured Thanjavur and built a temple there to the goddess Nisumbhasudani, and a still later inscription from Kanyakumari says he built the city anew. Krishna Sastri, who introduced the third volume of South Indian Inscriptions in 1920, reasoned that the Muttaraiyar were the ones who lost it. It is a sensible inference that no record states. Little else can be pinned on Vijayalaya. Several inscriptions that older catalogues gave to him probably belong to his grandson, because the places they come from were not Chola ground until later, and even the date of 850 is worked backward from the lengths of later reigns.

His son stands on firmer ground.

The end of the Pallavas

Around 880, by most accounts, the Pandya king Varagunavarman II marched north into the Pallava country. The Pallava king Aparajita met him at Sripurambiyam with the Western Ganga king Prithivipati I as his ally. Aparajita won, though Prithivipati died in the fighting. Then Aparajita begins to fade. His own inscriptions run up to his eighteenth year and stop.

The Tiruvalangadu plates say why: Aditya I, Vijayalaya's son, conquered him in battle and took his beloved country. A claim in a plate issued more than a century later would be easy to doubt, but the ground itself backs it. At Tirukkalukkunram, in Chengalpattu district, an inscription of Aditya's twenty-seventh year ratifies a grant first made by a Pallava king and later renewed by Narasimhavarman I, the conqueror of Vatapi. At Niyamam, a record of his twenty-fourth year shows a Pallava queen named Marambavai making a grant under Chola rule, which Krishna Sastri takes as a sign that the Pallavas were fully subdued and her husband already dead. A third inscription, at Tillasthanam, gives Aditya a title meaning "he who overran Tondai-nadu." The exact date remains loose. One modern summary says 897, another puts the last Pallava king around 903, and the stones establish only that it came after Aparajita's eighteenth year.

Aditya probably took the Kongu country as well. His son's inscriptions turn up there although Parantaka never claims to have conquered it, and a late chronicle of the Kongu kings gives it to Aditya. The same Tillasthanam record adds a diplomatic detail. A man named Vikki Annan received honours from Aditya and from his Chera contemporary Sthanu Ravi, which suggests the two courts were on friendly terms.

Aditya died at Tondaimanarrur, identified with Tondamanad near Kalahasti, and his son built a Shiva temple over his ashes that is still known as Kodandaramesvara or Adityesvara. The Anbil plates, issued later by Sundara Chola, remember him as the king who built Shiva temples along both banks of the Kaveri, from its source in the Sahya mountains to the sea. His reign ran at least from 879 to 907, a floor set by his last inscription year and by the date his successor took the throne.

Madurai

Parantaka I came to the throne in 907. For once the date can be checked against the sky. Kielhorn worked it out from the details of an inscription at Gramam, and a total solar eclipse recorded in a thirty-third-year inscription at Anaimalai confirms it.

His first target was the Pandya country. From his third year, 909 to 910, his inscriptions call him Madiraikonda, "he who took Madurai." A Vatteluttu inscription at Anaimalai, six miles from Madurai, supports the capture and perhaps a short occupation, and so does the Udayendiram grant of Prithivipati II, a Ganga-Bana king who served Parantaka and who says the Chola army seized a herd of elephants along with Madurai. Later voices only add color. The Kanyakumari inscription says Parantaka killed the Pandya with his whole army and reduced Madurai to ashes. The Pandya king Rajasimha ran, and the Tiruvalangadu plates say he "entered the sea," which Krishna Sastri reads as flight to Sri Lanka.

The Pandyas did not quit. An inscription of Parantaka's twelfth year describes a battle at Velur against the combined armies of the Pandyas and the Sinhalese, and the Ganga king's grant boasts that the Chola destroyed an immense force sent by the lord of Lanka. The best check on all this comes from the other side of the water. The Mahavamsa, in its fifty-second chapter, describes a Pandya king beaten by the Chola who sends gifts to the Sinhalese king Kassapa V and asks for an army. Kassapa raises one, names a commander and goes down to the coast at Mahatittha himself to see it off, reminding the troops of earlier kings' victories. Once the ships land in the Pandya country, the Pandya king boasts that he will bring all India under one umbrella. In the chronicle's own wording, as Krishna Sastri quotes it, he "succeeded not in conquering the king of the Cholian race," and the army went home. A rival court is agreeing with the Chola inscriptions on the main point, a Chola victory over a Pandya and Sinhalese force. The chronicle's dates run decades off and need adjusting, so the match lies in the sequence of events and not the years.

Ilam

Around 944, after his thirty-seventh year, Parantaka's inscriptions add a title: "who took Madurai and Ilam." An inscription at Kuram dated in his fortieth year says he actually entered Ceylon, and the Tiruvalangadu plates add that the fire of his anger was put out only by the tears of the Sinhalese king's wives.

The Mahavamsa tells the same war from the losing side, and the two accounts split on how it ended. Under King Udaya III the Chola sent an embassy demanding the crown and regalia the Pandya king had left in Lanka. Udaya refused. A Chola army landed and killed the Sinhalese commander, the king fled to Rohana with the Pandya regalia, the Cholas could not follow him there, and they went home without what they came for. Both versions can be true at once. A Chola army did land and win a battle, which is what the title claims. It also failed to get the regalia, which the title leaves out. That is what a Chola claim looks like when a rival account can be laid beside it.

Friends in the west

By Parantaka's day the Cheras who fought the Cholas at Venni had become partners. The Anbil plates say one of his queens, the mother of his son Arinjaya, was a daughter of a Kerala prince named Paluvettaraiyar. A general serving his eldest son, the prince Rajaditya, came from Kerala, and several inscriptions at Tirunamanallur mention people from Malabar as the prince's personal servants, which suggests a Malayali retinue. A princess named Ravi-Nili, daughter of the Chera king Vijayaragadeva, made offerings at Tiruvorriyur, now part of Chennai. Krishna Sastri concludes that relations were cordial and that people from the Malabar coast were moving into the Tamil country in numbers.

The Pandya country stood in the opposite position. After Rajasimha's defeat, Parantaka's inscriptions appear around Madurai and across Tirunelveli district, and Sastri takes that as proof the Pandya country acknowledged him. His reach ran north to Nellore as well: a Tiruvorriyur inscription of his thirty-fourth year (941) records an officer returning from the conquest of Sitpuli after destroying Nellur. In the west an inscription turns up at Somur near Karur, and the Western Ganga king Prithivipati II, whose lands lay partly in Mysore, served him as a feudatory.

Takkolam

The advance hit a wall in the north. The Rashtrakuta king Krishna III, ruling from the Deccan, invaded the Chola country, and Rajaditya, who commanded the northern provinces, was the first to oppose him. He was killed at Takkolam while fighting from the back of an elephant, struck down by the Ganga prince Butuga, an ally and kinsman of Krishna III.

A record at Solapuram dates the second year of Krishna's entry into Tondai-mandalam to Saka 871, which is 949 CE, and Krishna Sastri places Rajaditya's death in 949 or 950. He adds that Krishna III is said to have taken Tanjore and Kanchipuram. The Chola side has a counterclaim, in the Kanyakumari inscription, that Parantaka defeated Krishna III, and Sastri squares the two by supposing an earlier, successful campaign before the one that cost the prince his life. Both versions of that earlier win come from the Cholas themselves, and the Kanyakumari text was cut a century later.

What he left

Parantaka lived on after Takkolam, though for how long is unsettled. Krishna Sastri puts his death around 953, resting on an inscription of his forty-sixth year that he later calls doubtful, and in another passage he reckons from 947. By the end the realm reached from Nellore in the north to Tirunelveli in the south, and from the Kongu country to the east coast, though the Rashtrakutas had hurt it badly in the north.

It was also becoming a working state. The inscriptions at Uttaramallur, now Uttiramerur in Kanchipuram district, lay out detailed rules for how village assemblies were to run, and Krishna Sastri reads them as a sign of Parantaka's close attention to local government, while pointing out that village institutions are older than his reign. The Tiruvalangadu plates say he covered the small hall at Chidambaram with gold. He was a devout Shaiva, though Sastri reads him as tolerant of other faiths within his lands.

The heir who should have followed him lay dead at Takkolam, and a Rashtrakuta army still held the north.

4. Five Kings, One Throne

Rajaditya lay dead at Takkolam, and the throne he was meant to inherit moved on without him. One grant, the large Leyden plates, says he became king after his father. The Tiruvalangadu plates, cut later, do not make him a ruler, and a prince killed in battle while his father still lived is hard to crown. By Krishna Sastri's count, five princes then held the Chola crown in about thirty-three years, in an order irregular enough that he suspected feuds inside the family. If there were feuds, they left no record. What survives is the pattern of who reigned, and the pattern is odd.

The quiet king

The second son, Gandaraditya, seems to have been next. Inscriptions of a "Madiraikonda Rajakesarivarman" have been ascribed to him, and the latest is dated in his seventeenth year. None records an event of any weight. Part of the old Chola country was probably in Rashtrakuta hands, and the dynasty's output of inscriptions thins to almost nothing. What survives instead is devotion. Tamil hymns attributed to Gandaraditya still exist, and they point to a king with a religious turn of mind. His queen, Sembiyan Mahadevi, appears in inscription after inscription making gifts to temples, and she outlived him by a long time. Their son, Madhurantaka, later called Uttama Chola, was probably a small boy when his father died, which would explain why he was passed over, though Sastri also allows that a troubled country may simply have needed an older man.

An inscription of Gandaraditya's eighth year already refers to a lord Arikulakesari with a title of respect, and Sastri suspects the word marks him as dead. That was Arinjaya, another of Parantaka's sons, who seems to have died during Gandaraditya's reign. No inscription of his own had turned up when Sastri wrote.

The king who died in the golden palace

The crown then went to Arinjaya's son, Sundara Chola, also known as Parantaka II. The Anbil plates, dated in his fourth year, name his mother as a princess of the Vaidumba line. His stone inscriptions give him a fighting epithet, "he who caused the Pandya to enter the forest," and the large Leyden plates record a bloody battle at a place called Cheur, without naming the enemy. The same grant adds a vivid image of his son Aditya, still a boy, playing at war with the Pandya king Vira-Pandya as a lion cub might with an enraged elephant. Sastri read the pieces together. The enemy was Vira-Pandya, and the boy was already marked as heir.

The Pandya war was not the only one. A later inscription from Rajaraja's reign records that in Sundara's ninth year a general named Parantakan Siriyavelar, also called Tirukkarrali Pichchan, of Kodumbalur, died in a battle in Ceylon. The Mahavamsa, in chapter 54, describes a clash in the reign of Mahinda IV in which his general defeated the army of a Chola king. Krishna Sastri matches the two once the chronicle's dates are shifted by the correction of about twenty-three years that Hultzsch applied. Venkayya had earlier tied the same chapter to a different Chola attack, so the identification is a scholar's judgement. Siriyavelar was also family: his mother was Parantaka I's daughter. The Cholas were now losing generals from their own household on Sri Lankan soil.

Later inscriptions remember Sundara as the king who died in the golden palace, and the Tiruvalangadu plates say his subjects believed Manu himself had come down to restore laws the Kali age had loosened. His queen, Vanavanmahadevi, is said to have followed him in death. His daughter Kundavai, married to a Pallava chief named Vandyadevar, set up an image of her mother in the temple at Tanjore. Inscriptions also name another queen, a Chera king's daughter, so the western alliance of Parantaka's day carried on. That ties the Chera thread, which began at Venni with a king starving himself to death in disgrace, to a marriage in the Chola palace.

The prince who took a head

Sundara's elder son, Aditya II, called Karikala, was the chosen heir and, for a short time, the actual king. Inscriptions call him "who took the head of Vira-Pandya," and the Tiruvalangadu plates say he killed the Pandya in battle and set the severed head up as a pillar of victory. His own records are few and turn up only in the south, the latest dated in his fifth year. A second king with the same epithet appears in many inscriptions across Tondai-mandalam, one styled Parthivendravarman, whose latest year is the thirteenth. Sastri thinks he may have been a prince of the family acting as viceroy of the Pallava country. Neither record tells us how Aditya Karikala's short reign, which Sastri puts at about six years, came to an end. The Tiruvalangadu plates say only that when he died, the people wanted his younger brother Arunmolivarman for their king.

The prince who said no

Here the story turns. Arunmolivarman refused the crown. According to the Tiruvalangadu plates, he said that as long as his uncle Uttama Chola desired dominion, he would be content with the rank of heir apparent. So the son of Gandaraditya, who had been set aside as a boy, finally took the throne in 969, at the request of the cousin who might have claimed it. An inscription at Tiruvidaimarudur ties Uttama's thirteenth year to the Kali year 4083, which gives 969 as the first year of his reign, and the Madras Museum plates carry him to at least his sixteenth. He styled himself Parakesarivarman, though by the old pattern a son of a Rajakesarivarman should have been the other kind, and Sastri reads that oddity as a sign that he took the throne by consent and not by right.

The tale of noble refusal needs a warning label. It comes from the Tiruvalangadu grant, a document issued under Rajaraja's own son, and it casts Rajaraja as the prince who waited. The stones back only the fact: Uttama reigned from 969 and Rajaraja did not. Why the line of succession bent around him is the part no contemporary record explains, and the family that won gets to tell it.

Sixteen years of waiting

The dates in this stretch do not close neatly. Gandaraditya ruled at least seventeen years, Sundara roughly ten, Aditya about six, and between Parantaka's death near 953 and Uttama's accession in 969 there is not enough room for all of them. Sastri tried to fit them by shifting Parantaka's death to 947 and giving Sundara a tentative decade, and his own arithmetic still overshoots. Later scholars have redrawn these reigns, and the pages that follow use only the one fixed point, 969, set by a dated inscription and an astronomical year.

From that point the waiting lasted sixteen years. In 985 the throne went to the prince who had turned it down.

5. The King Who Wrote It Down

See this chapter on the atlas →

In the summer of 985 the throne went to the prince who had refused it. Kielhorn, working backward from the astronomical details in Rajaraja's dated inscriptions, placed the accession between 25 June and 25 July of that year. The Tiruvalangadu plates call him Arunmorivarman and say that when he was born the wives of the serpent Adisesha danced for joy, since the boy would take the weight of the earth off their husbands' backs. He was the second son of Sundara Chola, the younger brother of Aditya Karikala and of Kundavai, and he came to the crown young. Venkayya, who wrote the account of his reign for the second volume of South Indian Inscriptions, thought he looked like a minor when his father or elder brother died.

His parents had spent their last years as Shaiva devotees at Chidambaram, the temple Tamil tradition simply calls "the temple," and that side of their lives appealed most to his sister. Kundavai set up images of both of them in the great temple at Tanjore and endowed their worship, and she went on to outlive her brother. Venkayya, who had only the stones to go on, still saw her influence everywhere in the king's character.

Eight quiet years

For his first eight years Rajaraja fought no war that any inscription records. The fighting begins around 994, after the eighth year. Venkayya guessed that the quiet hid preparation, a young king recruiting and training the army he would need, and the guess fits what came after, though nothing in the stones says so.

Three crowns

His first campaign went west. His earliest inscriptions boast that he destroyed a fleet at Kandalur Salai, a port that later records place in the Chera king's country. The Tiruvalangadu plates, which tell the story at length, never mention it. They open with the war on the Pandyas instead, saying Rajaraja seized the Pandya king Amarabhujanga while his general took the port of Virinam, and Venkayya wondered whether Kandalur lay near it. The Tanjore inscriptions keep returning to a phrase that sounds like Venni again: the conquest of the Chera king and the Pandyas together, in Malai-nadu, the western hill country. The old coalition of the second century seems to have been fighting him some eight hundred years later. A stronghold called Udagai was burned, and poems named for later Chola kings remember Rajaraja cutting off eighteen heads in revenge for an insult to an envoy, though they do not say when.

By his fourteenth year, 998 to 999, an inscription at Melpadi gives him a new title, Mummudi-Chola. Read in the obvious way it means the Chola king who wears three crowns, the Chera, the Chola and the Pandya. Venkayya read it that way, and the three crowns that anchor this whole story, set side by side in Ashoka's lists, were finally on one head. A footnote to the same volume adds a complication. His earliest inscriptions, from the third, fourth and tenth years, spell the title with a form that means "the thrice powerful Chola," as in the Telugu and Kannada titles Mummadi-Bhima and Nurmadi-Taila. The grander reading may not be the original sense.

East and north

Before that fourteenth year he had also taken Vengai-nadu, the Eastern Chalukya country, along with Gangapadi and Nurambapadi in what is now Mysore, and a region called Tadigaipadi that scholars have placed in the same area. The Eastern Chalukya grants say Vengi had gone without a king for twenty-seven years, and Venkayya reasoned that the Cholas ended the gap in 999 by placing Saktivarman on the throne. It is an inference, but a tidy one. A Chola inscription at Malambi in Coorg names his general there. About fifteen years later Vimaladitya of Vengi was making gifts at Tiruvaiyaru near Tanjore, and about the same time he married Rajaraja's daughter, who bore the family name Kundavai.

Lanka

The Sri Lankan campaign belongs to the same busy years. A Tamil inscription of his twenty-seventh year, 1011 to 1012, stands at Padaviya in Ceylon, and villages in the island were assigned to pay revenue to the Tanjore temple, so the Chola hold was real, at least in the north. The island came to be called Mummadisora-mandalam after his title. The Sinhalese chronicle, the Mahavamsa, is hard to line up with all this. Hultzsch showed that its chronology in this stretch is off by about twenty-three years, and on his correction the Chola invasion falls between 1001 and 1004. A record at Tenkarai calls Rajaraja "conqueror of the world," which Venkayya took to mean that by about his seventeenth year the last of the enemies he meant to acquire were behind him.

A new name

Between his eighteenth and twenty-first years no inscription records any war, and the titles change. Sri-Rajaraja turns up for the first time in a nineteenth-year inscription, and so does Sivapadasekhara, "he who has the feet of Shiva as his crest." Venkayya's guess was that the temple authorities at Chidambaram bestowed them. A late chronicle of the Kongu kings says he made gifts to Chidambaram in 1004, which suits the guess. He named his own stone temple after the first of the two titles.

Satyasraya

The Cholas now collided with a rival of their own size, the Western Chalukya king Satyasraya, and the hostility has no stated cause. Venkayya noted only that the Chola conquest of the Mysore provinces had brought the two powers face to face. The campaign fell in the twenty-first or twenty-second year, 1005 to 1006. The Tiruvalangadu plates and the large Leyden plates claim a victory, and the Tamil inscriptions mention the conquest of the "seven and a half lakhs" of Rattapadi.

What makes the war unusually checkable is that the enemy left his own version. In Satyasraya's Hottur inscription of 1007 to 1008, the Chola king, whom he calls Nurmadi-Chola Rajendra, gathers nine hundred thousand men and pillages the country, killing women, children and Brahmans, and Satyasraya says he put the Chola to flight. An inscription at Uttattur, from Rajendra's third year, confirms that the prince himself fought Satyasraya and that a warrior fell in the fight. So the sources disagree about who won. They agree on who was in command, and on the scale of the violence, which Krishna Sastri found hard to read without regret. On his return Rajaraja gave gold flowers to the temple at Tanjore.

The stone temple

The temple already existed in some form by that homecoming. How long it took is the question Venkayya asked of the inscriptions, and he reasoned like a builder. The name Sri-Rajarajesvara plainly borrows the title Sri-Rajaraja, so no foundation can be earlier than the nineteenth year. Gifts pour in between the twenty-third and twenty-ninth years, which suggests the building was well advanced by the twenty-third. On the 275th day of the twenty-fifth year, around 1010, the king presented a copper pot to crown the pinnacle of the central shrine, and that, for a temple of this kind, is the last thing placed. Stone temples were not common at the time. The royal order itself uses a word, tirukkarrali, "the stone temple," that signals how unusual the material was, and there is no hill near Tanjore from which the stone could have come.

The order that makes the building legible is dated the twentieth day of the twenty-sixth year. It commands that the king's gifts, his sister's, his queens' and everyone else's be cut into the temple walls. Kundavai's gifts were recorded on the central shrine itself, next in rank to the king's, while the queens and officers were given the niches, the pillars and the enclosure. Lokamahadevi, probably the chief queen, built a shrine of her own at Tiruvaiyaru.

The endowments read like the budget of a small state. The temple was provided with gold images and vessels, and with villages to supply its paddy. It kept musicians to sing the Devaram hymns, dancing girls, Brahmin servants, accountants, treasurers, goldsmiths, carpenters, washermen, barbers, astrologers and watchmen. Eighteen of the musicians were former soldiers, twelve of them from the Velaikkara regiments, and some regiments were given minor shrines to manage. Venkayya counted thirty-one named regiments in the Tanjore inscriptions, many named after the king or his son. Almost all the booty of the wars, he judged, went to the temple.

The man behind some of the building was a soldier too. A Brahmin named Krishnan Raman, who was both a general and, for some years, chief secretary, built at least two of the enclosing verandahs, and inscriptions on the south and west enclosures say so.

The survey

While the temple rose, the king was counting his land. A record at Tiruvisalur from the twenty-fourth year refers to a revenue survey carried out earlier, and the Tanjore inscriptions show land measured down to fractions so small that the smallest unit is a fifty-two-billionth part of a veli. Venkayya suspected that the general Kuravan Ulagalandan, whose title means "he who measured the earth," earned it in the survey. Out of that settlement came an order, issued on the 124th day of the twenty-fourth year and engraved on the 143rd. Landholders who had gone three years without paying their dues would lose their land, and the village could sell it. The order names villages of Brahmins, of Vaikhanasas and of Jainas side by side, which shows the Jainas stood on equal terms.

His toleration shows elsewhere. He permitted a feudatory to build a Buddhist shrine at Negapatam and gave the village of Anaimangalam to it, a grant recorded on the large Leyden plates.

Why we know

None of this would be readable without a habit Rajaraja started. He was, as far as Venkayya knew, the first king in the south to open every inscription with an account of his own conquests. Parantaka I conquered half the country, yet his stone inscriptions mention only Madurai, and even that only so that readers could tell him from his grandfather of the same name. Rajaraja's successors copied the practice, and it is the reason the later Chola dynasty can be dated and followed at all. He also ordered the old inscriptions on a temple wall at Tirumalavadi copied into a book before the wall was pulled down, then re-engraved them afterward. Venkayya judged that self-glorification was not the whole motive, and whatever the instinct, the result is the same.

By 1055 the founding of the temple had become a popular story. An inscription of the fourth year of Rajendradeva provides for the performance of a play, the Rajarajesvara-Nataka, on a festival day.

The last year

Even in his twenty-ninth year, with the temple done, the king sent an expedition against the twelve thousand islands. Venkayya confessed he could not decide which group in the Indian Ocean the phrase meant. To celebrate, Rajaraja was weighed against gold or pearls in the tulabhara ceremony, and Lokamahadevi passed through a golden cow. By Venkayya's reckoning his son Rajendra had already been crowned a year before the old king died.

The army that the father spent his first eight years raising was the one the son inherited. The inscriptions call it "the great warlike army," and Rajendra would take it across the Bay of Bengal.

6. The Ganga and the Sea

See this chapter on the atlas →

Rajendra took the throne in 1012, and he did not wait. By his seventh year, 1018 to 1019, his inscriptions were reporting the capture of an island in the west called Sandimattivu. The Tiruvalangadu plates, issued in his sixth year, explain why he went: he had heard of the disgrace the kings of the earth suffered at Parasurama's hands, and meant to answer it. An inscription of the Tanjore temple dated in his seventh year ends its list of conquests with the island. What he is said to have carried off matters more than the name: a crown of pure gold that Parasurama had deposited there when he judged the fortifications unassailable. In the same year, a later inscription implies, he named a co-ruler. Rajadhiraja's regnal years are counted from 1018, which means he was nominated as joint ruler that year.

Crowns were becoming his signature. The Tiruvalangadu plates credit him with seizing the crowns of the Pandya, the king of Ceylon and the king of Kerala. The editor of the third volume of South Indian Inscriptions thought this earned him the title Mudigonda Chola, "he who took the crown," which he seems to have commemorated by founding a town called Mudigondacholapuram.

The old regalia

The longest list of what he won survives on a wall of the Rajarajesvara temple, in a Tanjore inscription dated on the 242nd day of his nineteenth year, around 1030 or 1031. Hultzsch edited and translated it. One line in it closes an old account. Rajendra took, it says, the crown of the queen and king of Ilam, and with them the crown of Sundara and the pearl necklace of Indra, "which the king of the South had previously given up to that king of Iram."

The regalia that the fleeing Rajasimha had left in Lanka, and that Parantaka I's army could not recover when the Sinhalese king retreated to Rohana, had at last come back to the Tamil country, or so the inscription says. It reads as the end of a seventy-year chase. The Chola record is the only voice here, a boast cut into a temple wall, and the Sinhalese chronicle used in earlier parts gives no confirmation of a handover.

The same list takes in Idaiturai-nadu, which Fleet identified as the Raichur doab, Vanavasi, Kollippakkai and Mannaikkadakkam, along with the seven and a half lakhs of Iratta-padi, which he claims to have taken from the Western Chalukya king Jayasimha, "who turned his back at Muyangi and hid himself." The same list that names Jayasimha is, once again, the only Chola version of that war, and it is a boast.

North

The wall then turns north, and the list becomes a long march. It names Sakkara-kottam, Madura-mandalam, Namanaikkonai, Panchappalli and Masuni-desam, places the translation leaves unlocated. It records the capture of a king named Indiradan, of the old lunar race, with his family, in a fight in a hall at Adinagar. Odda-vishayam follows, difficult of approach and subdued in close fights, then Kosalai-nadu, "where Brahmanas assembled." Next comes Tandabutti, which Hultzsch read as Dandabhukti, taken after the destruction of Dharmapala in a hot battle. Takkanaladam, which he read as Dakshina-Lata, fell after an attack on Ranasura. Vangala-desam, "where the rain did not cease," saw Govindachandra flee. Elephants and "treasures of women" were taken, the inscription says, after Rajendra frightened Mahipala on a battlefield and left him stripped of his slippers, bracelets and earrings. Uttiraladam, the Uttara-Lata, comes next, and then the line the whole march exists to reach: the Ganga, whose waters sprinkled sacred bathing places.

The march has more than one witness. The Tanjore wall carries the longest telling, and a hill at Tirumalai, in the old North Arcot district, carries another, cut into a smooth rock beside a rock-cut Jaina figure and dated in Rajendra's thirteenth year, between the middle of 1024 and the middle of 1025. Both are the king's own words. A first check from outside comes from Bengal. Inscriptions of Govindachandra, king of Vanga, have turned up within the limits of the old kingdom he is said to have ruled, and N. K. Bhattasali, publishing two of them in Epigraphia Indica, wrote that they confirmed the Tirumalai text, placed the king in his region, and showed him reigning for at least twenty-three years. Mahipala I of the Pala dynasty, whom the Chola text names as the last king met, is known from his own records for the years c. 995 to 1043. Bengal's historians read the Chola text as a map of the country: Dharmapala in Dandabhukti, roughly the Midnapore district, Ranasura of the Sura family in Dakshina-Radha around Howrah and Hooghly, Govindachandra in the east beyond the Bhagirathi where the rain never stops, and Mahipala in northern Radha.

What the Bengal side does not supply is an account of the raid itself. No Pala record of it has been consulted, and the Chola texts remain the only telling of what happened. Modern summaries add that a general, Araiyan Rajarajan, led the army, that the campaign began in Kalinga against the ruler Indraratha, and that it fell between about 1019 and 1024. Historians also disagree about the purpose. Venkayya read it as a pilgrimage to fetch the waters of the Ganga, and Nilakanta Sastri and others refuted that. A later Sanskrit text, the Siddhantasaravali, says Shaiva Brahmans were brought from Bengal to the Chola country, a claim that sits well with the priests from Gaudadesa at Tanjore.

The thrust left a name. The inscription itself is dated from the king's palace at Gangaikonda-Sorapuram, the city of the one who took the Ganga, with a royal hall called Mudikonda-Soran inside its palace. A gift issued from that hall provides paddy for the priests of the Rajarajesvara temple, naming among their pupils men who are natives of Aryadesa, Madhyadesa or Gaudadesa. If those names stand for northern India, the Gangetic plain and Bengal, the Tanjore temple was being served by priests from the lands the king's armies had marched through.

The sea

The list ends at sea. After the Ganga the inscription turns to a fleet. Rajendra, it says, dispatched many ships over the rolling sea and caught Samgramavijayottungavarman, the king of Kadaram, together with his rutting elephants, and took the treasure that king had gathered. A string of place names follows: the Vidyadhara-torana, a gate at the war-gate of the enemy city; Vijayam; Pannai, "watered by the river"; the ancient Malaiyur on its high hill; Mayirudingam; Ilangasogam; Mappappalam; Mevilimbangam; Valaippanduru; Talaittakkolam; Madamalingam; Ilamuri-desam; Manakkavaram; and Kadaram again, "protected by the neighbouring sea."

Hultzsch could identify only the last, and he took it to be a place in the Ramnad district, an identification that later work has moved. The Tirukkalar copper plates, dated in the eighteenth year of the reign, already end their list of conquests with the capture of Kadaram, so the sea campaign lies somewhere between the twelfth year and the eighteenth. What kind of conquest it was, and why it happened, are questions historians still argue over. That argument has an article of its own, The Fleet That History Forgot.

The list stops

The most telling fact about the great list is how it ends. Inscriptions at Tiruvallam dated in Rajendra's twenty-first, twenty-sixth and thirty-first years repeat the same historical passage, and Hultzsch concluded they showed he made no further conquests after his nineteenth year. A king whose list of conquests kept growing from the twelfth year to the nineteenth stopped adding to it for the next twelve. The inscriptions give no reason, and the quiet is all they show.

He was still issuing inscriptions in his thirty-first year, around 1043. His co-ruler had by then been on the throne for a quarter of a century. Rajadhiraja's later inscriptions claim that he destroyed Kalyanapura, the Chalukya capital, and held a heroes' anointing there under the name Vijaya-Rajendra. At the Airavatesvara temple at Darasuram, in the Thanjavur district, a doorkeeper image carries an inscription saying it was brought home from that war.

7. Crowned on a Battlefield

Rajadhiraja had been co-ruler for twenty-six years when his father died, and he spent the next eight years on the throne fighting the same enemy. The Western Chalukya king Ahavamalla, also known as Someshvara I, would not stay beaten. The war reached its crisis at Koppam, on the Tungabhadra in what is now Karnataka, in 1052 or 1054 depending on the scholar, with Kielhorn's astronomy putting Rajadhiraja's reign at an end about 1050. Modern summaries, which lean on Nilakanta Sastri, say Rajadhiraja led from the back of a war elephant to rally his retreating troops and was killed there by a barrage of arrows. They add that Chola memory named him for the manner of his death, Yanai-mel-thunjina Devar, the lord who died on the elephant. The phrase echoes the name Sundara Chola carried a century earlier, the lord who died in the golden palace, so two kings now bear names that record where they died.

The only full account of Koppam is the victor's. It comes from an inscription of the fourth year of Rajendra II, the dead king's younger brother, cut into a temple wall at Manimangalam, in the old Chingleput district. The Chalukya king, the text says, heard that the Chola had reached Iratta-mandalam and was laying waste to its rivers, districts and towns. He cried out that it was a disgrace, sprang up with burning eyes and rode to Koppam, a position hard to describe in its strength. His arrows then struck the Chola king's elephant on the forehead and the king himself on the thigh and shoulders, and the warriors riding with him fell. In the inscription that king is Rajendra, with no mention of his brother's death. Fresh regiments were thrown in, the Chalukya king's younger brother Jayasimha and two chiefs named Pulikesi and Dasapanman were killed, and Ahavamalla fled. The Cholas took elephants, camels, the boar banner of the Chalukya house and two queens, Sattiyavvai and Sangappai, and the king, the inscription says, performed the anointment of victory on the spot.

That last line is probably the root of a later tradition that Rajendra II crowned himself on the battlefield and raised a victory pillar at Kolhapur before going home. The inscription itself, which a temple wall preserved, says only that the king won, was wounded and was anointed. One modern summary notes that the Chalukya account of the battle survives only in a later inscription, so the fight has one fully visible side.

The names of the crowns

The same inscription opens with a list that does something quieter. It records the crowns and titles Rajendra II had conferred on his relatives. One brother received the title Chola-Pandya, a prince is listed as Chola-Kerala, and a younger brother, styled lord of Koli, which the editor identifies as Uraiyur, received the title Karikala-Solan, "Karikala Chola, praised on earth." So the ancient riverside capital and the second-century hero were still names that a king could bestow more than eight centuries later. The Pandya and Chera crowns, whose kings gathered against Karikala at Venni, had become honorific titles worn by Chola princes.

The titles hid a harder reality. Rajendra II's successor, Virarajendra, gave the Pandya country to his son Gangaikonda-Chola as a province, with the title Chola-Pandya, and the Tondai country to another son, Madhurantaka, as Cholendra. Yet the same inscription records that Virarajendra killed a Kerala king, trampled a Pandya king, and cut off the head of the king of Pottappi. The crowns could be worn by Chola princes, and the kingdoms underneath them kept fighting.

Kudalsangamam

Rajendra II's Tamil inscriptions also claim Ceylon. An army sent south, the Manimangalam text says, beheaded Vira-Salamegan, called a king of the Kalingas, in Lanka and captured two sons of the Ceylon king Manabharanan. Rajadhiraja had earlier beheaded another Vira-Salamegan, who is said to have come from Kanyakubja. Hultzsch noticed that the Sinhalese chronicle, the Mahavamsa, also names princes called Manabharana and Kittisirimegha. Its chronology puts them decades after the Chola kings, so the names cannot belong to the same men.

Virarajendra's reign is known from a handful of inscriptions, among them one on the Pasupatisvara temple at Karuvur, issued from the royal bathing hall in the palace at Gangaikonda-Solapuram. It is a victory text. He drove the Chalukya prince Vikkalan from Gangapadi back across the Tungabhadra. He destroyed an army sent into Vengai-nadu, killed its commander Chamundaraja and mutilated his daughter, who was the wife of a chief called Irugayan. The third time, the inscription says, the Chalukyas fought him at Kudalsangamam, the "junction" of two rivers. Ahavamalla's sons Vikkalan and Singanan fled, the Chalukya camp fell, and the Cholas took the wives, the boar banner, an elephant called Pushpaka and a herd of war elephants. Scholars identify Vikkalan with Vikramaditya VI and Singanan with Jayasimha III, and place the battle at the meeting of the Tungabhadra and the Krishna, or of the Tunga and the Bhadra.

Kielhorn's calculations fix one date firmly. Kulottunga I was crowned in 1070, and everything between Rajadhiraja's death and that date, Hultzsch concluded, must be fitted into the reigns of Rajendra II and Virarajendra.

The grandson

By modern accounts Virarajendra died in 1070 and his son Athirajendra came to the throne in the same year. His reign lasted months. A civil disturbance broke out, whose cause the available summaries leave unclear, and Athirajendra was killed. The fullest account of the aftermath comes from the Western Chalukya side. The court poet Bilhana, in a Sanskrit life of Vikramaditya VI, says that Virarajendra had given a daughter to Vikramaditya in marriage and then died, and that Vikramaditya set out for home. Within days of his return, according to the poem, news reached him that Athirajendra had been killed in a fresh rebellion and that Rajendra Chalukya had seized the throne as Kulottunga. Vikramaditya marched against him. Bilhana was a poet at Vikramaditya's court, which makes him a partisan witness. The Chola inscriptions agree on the date of Kulottunga's crowning, 1070.

The man who took the throne had no Chola father. Rajendra Chalukya was born to the Eastern Chalukya king Rajaraja, who came to the throne in 1022, and to Ammangadevi, a daughter of Rajendra I. His paternal grandparents, in Hultzsch's account, were Vimaladitya of Vengi, who took the throne in 1011, and Kundavai, daughter of Rajaraja I and younger sister of Rajendra I. That marriage, which looked in 1013 like a piece of diplomacy to settle Vengi, had produced within two generations a prince with the blood of both houses. A later poem, the Kalingattu-Parani, seems in Hultzsch's reading to imply that Rajendra I adopted him and named him heir. If so, the boy had been meant for the Chola throne long before 1070.

For the first time in more than two centuries the throne had passed to a man whose father was not a Chola. Vikramaditya VI, Virarajendra's son-in-law on the other side of the Tungabhadra, did not accept it.

8. The King Who Abolished the Tolls

See this chapter on the atlas →

Kulottunga waited eight years for a crown that arithmetic said was his. The copper-plate grants give his father, the Eastern Chalukya king Rajaraja, a reign of forty or forty-one years from 1022, which would put the son's accession around 1062. But the Telugu inscriptions of Kulottunga I carry both a Saka year and a regnal year, and they place it in 1069 to 1071, and Kielhorn's calculations from Tamil and Kannada inscriptions narrowed it to a window between 14 March and 8 October 1070. Hultzsch, who gathered the king's inscriptions in 1903, thought the gap explained itself. Rajaraja's brother Vijayaditya VII took Vengi in 1062, and Virarajendra, the Chola king, claims to have conquered Vengi and handed it to him. It looks, Hultzsch wrote, as if the rightful heir had been pushed aside by his uncle with Chola help. The Chola king who helped displace him was the predecessor of the throne he would later take.

The man who finally took it had two sets of names. He was born Rajendra-Choda, a name the hostile Chalukya poet Bilhana renders as Rajiga, and in the Tamil inscriptions of his second, third and fourth years he is Rajendra-Choladeva. His grandfather's name sits inside his own. According to Hultzsch's reading of the Kalingattu-Parani, a poem written in his honour, Rajendra I, who seems to have had no son of his own, adopted him and named him heir apparent. The poem and an inscription of his second year credit him, while still a prince, with capturing elephants at Vayiragaram and beating the king of Dhara at Sakkarakottam. He took the name Kulottunga, and by his fifth year it appears in inscriptions.

The empty country

The first inscription to call him Kulottunga-Choladeva is dated in his fifth year, 1074 to 1075, and stands at Kanchipuram. It is a victory text with a revealing silence. It says he defeated the king of Kuntala, crowned himself king of the Chola country and decapitated an unnamed Pandya king. It also describes the country he entered in terms that suggest nobody was in charge: the Lakshmi of the south dishonoured, the goddess of the Kaveri country alone. The same picture appears in the Kalingattu-Parani, which adds that "the king of kings" had met his death before Kulottunga arrived.

Bilhana tells it from the other bank of the Tungabhadra, and he and the Chola texts agree on the outline while quarrelling over the rest. His poem names the man who took the throne of Kanchi as Rajiga, lord of Vengi, which Hultzsch identified as Rajendra-Choda, and it gives him an ally, Vikramaditya's elder brother Somesvara II. Vikramaditya, Bilhana says, put Rajiga to flight, took his brother prisoner and ascended his own throne in 1076. The "king of kings" whose death the Kalingattu-Parani mentions was, Hultzsch thought, Athirajendra.

Neither side can be trusted to tell the later war honestly. Chola inscriptions of Kulottunga's eleventh year claim he drove Vikkalan, their name for Vikramaditya, from Nangili in the Kolar district by way of Manalur to the Tungabhadra, and later versions add Alatti and the Konkan, and "a thousand elephants at Navilai." Bilhana's hero loses nothing. He says Vikramaditya "had once more to extinguish the Chola" before entering his capital at Kalyana, and later, after a long peace, put the Chola to flight again and took Kanchi. One physical fact cuts through the two claims. An inscription of Kulottunga's thirtieth year, about 1100, was issued from his palace at Kanchipuram, so he still held the city then, whatever Bilhana's hero did there.

South to the cape

In the fourteenth year, 1083 to 1084, the inscriptions turn south. They say he put "the five Pandyas" to flight and cut off the western part of their country, taking in the Gulf of Mannar, the Podiyil mountain, Cape Comorin and Kottaru, a town then in Travancore State, near Nagercoil and about ten miles north of the cape. He shrank the Pandya kingdom to the Madurai district, planted garrisons along the roads through Kottaru, and, in the language of another inscription, left a Pandya king's head lying outside his city "being pecked by kites." He also overran Kudamalai-nadu, the western hill country, whose warriors, in Hultzsch's summary of the record, perished to the last man defending their independence. On the coast the poems name Vilinam and Salai. The Chera king's ships were burned at Salai twice, according to the Vikkirama-Solan-ula, the same port where Rajaraja I had started his career of conquest ninety years before.

Back in the east, Kalinga fell some time before the twenty-sixth year, 1095 to 1096. As with the wars of 1070, a poem, the Kalingattu-Parani, carries most of the detail. The Kalinga ruler of the period was Anantavarman Chodaganga, who reigned from 1078 to about 1142. Vikrama-Choda's own inscriptions place the war before his coronation, which means it fell within his father's lifetime.

Vengi, sons and tolls

Vengi, where Kulottunga had begun, was held by viceroys. First was his uncle Vijayaditya VII, then his second son Rajaraja II for a year, then his third son Vira-Choda, who took office on 23 August 1078, then a chief named Choda of Velanandu. The Telugu country that the Chola house had married into since 1011 stayed its eastern province.

Of the man himself the inscriptions say more in his last decades. By the fourteenth year he had added Chakravartin, emperor, to the old title Udaiyar, lord, and by the twentieth Tribhuvanachakravartin, emperor of the three worlds. His capital was Gangaikondacholapuram, which his grandfather had founded, and the second city was Kanchi. He married Madhurantaki, a daughter of the Chola king Rajendradeva, and the grants give them seven sons. Several of his inscriptions refer to "the mistress of the whole world," and Hultzsch took that to be her. Three further queens are named in an inscription of the twenty-sixth year, and the Kalingattu-Parani says that one of them, Tyagavalli, shared authority with the king himself.

He is also remembered by a nickname, Sungandavirtta, "he who abolished tolls." An inscription at Pallavaram in the thirty-ninth year of Sungandavirtta-Kulottunga-Soladeva carries it, and the Vikkirama-Solan-ula confirms that he did abolish tolls. The poem Kalingattu-Parani adds yet another name for him, Karikala-Chola, the old hero's name worn again by a king who had earned the throne at the end of a long war.

His son Vikrama-Choda, who had governed Vengi after Vira-Choda, was crowned in the Chola country about 29 June 1118, apparently as co-regent, a date Kielhorn later reckoned from the son's inscriptions. The father's own inscriptions run up to his forty-ninth year, and the Chellur plates of his grandson give him forty-nine years in all, which carries him to 1118 or 1119.

9. The Long Afternoon

Vikrama-Choda governed Vengi for about a quarter-century before anyone asked him to rule the Chola country. Hultzsch worked it out from the copper plates and inscriptions: Vikrama took over the Telugu province from his brother Vira-Choda, whose latest known date is 1092 to 1093, and held it until his coronation. Kielhorn calculated that coronation from his inscriptions as about 29 June 1118. The Tamil texts say only that he left the north for the south, took the tiger crest and put on the hereditary crown. Hultzsch took this as a coronation, apparently as co-regent, with Kulottunga I dying shortly afterward, about 1119.

The north did not wait for him. A Pithapuram inscription says Vengi was left without a ruler after Vikrama's departure, and an inscription of the chief Choda of Velanandu, whom another Pithapuram record calls Kulottunga's adopted son, shows him by 1120 to 1121 as a vassal of the Chalukya king Vikramaditya VI. Chalukya inscriptions at Draksharama carry on to 1123 or 1124. Vikrama-Choda then took the region back, and Telugu inscriptions of his reign at Chebrolu and Nidubrolu are dated 1127 and 1135. Earlier, as a prince, he had led the Kalinga expedition and beaten a chieftain called Telunga Bhima of Kulam, one of the Nayakas of Ellore, if the inscriptions are read as Hultzsch read them.

His surnames were Tyagasamudra, "the ocean of liberality," and, in a Sevilimedu inscription of 1134, Akalanka, "the spotless one." His queen Mukkokkilanadigal appears beside him in the inscriptions up to the eighth year and then disappears, so Hultzsch concluded she died in the ninth year, 1126 to 1127, and a favourite named Tribhuvanamulududaiyal took her place at his side. The last date for him is the eighteenth of April 1135, in a Telugu inscription of his seventeenth year at Nidubrolu. The plates of his successor Kulottunga II date from 24 March 1143, though Kielhorn had to correct the plates' own Saka year, which their composer had got wrong.

Fifteen years on a temple wall

Of the next kings, Kulottunga II, Rajaraja II and Rajadhiraja II, the stone is thinner, and the order has to be pieced together. The proof that Rajaraja II came before Rajadhiraja II sits on a wall at the Ekamranatha temple in Kanchipuram. An inscription there of Rajadhiraja II's eighth year counts fifteen years from the month of Tai in the nineteenth year of Rajarajadeva to Aippasi in the eighth year of the emperor of the three worlds, Rajadhirajadeva. Hultzsch read it as showing that Rajaraja II preceded Rajadhiraja II by some years, though not necessarily immediately.

The one big event that can be placed in this stretch comes from the Sinhalese side. The Mahavamsa, in chapters 76 and 77, describes an invasion of the Pandya country by the Ceylon king Parakramabahu. The Pandyas were quarrelling over Madurai. Vira-Pandya, the son of Parakrama-Pandya, had the Sinhalese behind him, and a rival named Kulasekhara had the Cholas. Venkayya matched that war to the reign of Rajadhiraja II. So two hundred and some years after Parantaka I, a Chola army and a Sinhalese army were again on opposite sides of a Pandya succession.

The minister and the Pandya war

Kulottunga II, by the summaries of his reign, preferred to live at Chidambaram rather than at Gangaikondacholapuram, celebrated his coronation there and paid for an elaborate renovation of the temple, which a poem, the Kulottunga Cholan Ula, describes. His successor Rajaraja II is credited with the Airavatesvara temple at Darasuram, begun in his reign and finished either by its end or early in the next, and a poet of his court, Ottakkuttar, names Darasuram as the capital. The man who ran much of this was a minister, Tiruchchirrambalam-udaiyan Perumanambiyan, called Pallavarayan. When Rajaraja II died, four years after naming a grandson of Vikrama Chola as heir, it was Pallavarayan who had the young man anointed as Rajadhiraja II.

The first years of the new reign were taken up by a war the Mahavamsa describes from the Sinhalese side. S. R. Balasubrahmanyam's study of the later Chola temples reconstructs it as a story of partners swapped. A Sinhalese army sent by Parakramabahu to support Vira-Pandya was met by a Pandya rival, Kulasekhara, who stormed Madurai and killed Parakrama-Pandya, his queen and children. The Sinhalese general pressed on, and Kulasekhara's army was beaten. He asked the Chola king for protection, and Pallavarayan led a large Chola army that put him back on the throne. Then Parakramabahu withdrew his support from Vira-Pandya and bought Kulasekhara with presents, and Kulasekhara turned on the Chola chiefs in the Pandya country. The Chola ruler answered by telling Pallavarayan to install Vira-Pandya instead. Hultzsch thought this Vira-Pandya was probably the man Kulottunga III later humbled. Summaries add that out of this war the Pandya power rose, strong enough to swallow both the Chola and Sinhalese kingdoms.

Taking Madurai twice

Kulottunga III was crowned between 8 June and 8 July 1178, a window Kielhorn fixed from twenty of his inscriptions, and the Nellore inscription that couples his nineteenth year with Saka 1119 confirms it. The war of the Pandya succession had not ended. An inscription of his ninth year, around 1187, says Kulottunga helped Vikrama-Pandya against the son of Vira-Pandya, defeated the Marava army, drove the Sinhalese into the sea, took Madurai from Vira-Pandya and gave it to Vikrama-Pandya. An inscription of the eleventh year adds that Vira-Pandya revolted, and that Kulottunga "took his crowned head." Hultzsch glosses that phrase carefully. It does not mean beheading. It means the Chola king, seated on his throne, placed his feet on the Pandya's crown. The vocabulary of conquest had by now softened into ritual humiliation, and the Pandya king lived on to fight again.

An inscription of the nineteenth year, about 1196, says Kulottunga marched north and entered Kanchi. It also records that Vira-Pandya gave battle once more at Nettur, was beaten, and was pardoned together with the Chera king. A Pandya of "the family of the Sun" received presents. By the twenty-first year the inscriptions claim Ilam, Ceylon, as well, and the formula grows with each decade: Madurai, then Madurai and Ilam, then Ilam, Madurai, the Pandya's crown and Karuvur. The Nettur of the inscription and the Maravas it defeats are both named in the Sinhalese chronicle's account of the Pandya war, which gives the geography an outside witness.

Behind the boasts the empire was a web of vassals. Tammusiddhi, a Telugu-Choda chief, made a grant at Nellore in 1203 to 1204. A Ganga chief at Kanchipuram, Siyaganga, gave a lamp to the Ekamranatha temple in 1205, and the Tamil grammar Nannul was composed in his time. Sambuvaraya chiefs made land grants. The Yadavaraya chiefs rebuilt the Venkatesa Perumal temple on the Tirupati hill. Another chief, a Kadava named Manavalapperumal, shows up in records of 1191 and 1195, and an Alagiya-Pallavan Kopperunjinga is named in the thirty-fifth year, 1213. Summaries describe the man who was soon to lock a Chola king in a fortress as an officer in the Chola government until about 1213.

Kulottunga III's own inscriptions run to his thirty-seventh year, which brings us to about 1215. In 1216 both Rajaraja III and the Pandya king Maravarman Sundara-Pandya I came to the throne.

10. The Last Dated Year

Two kings came to power in the same year, 1216. One was Rajaraja III, the last long-reigning Chola. The other was Maravarman Sundara-Pandya, who within two years was invading the Chola country. A chronological table prepared for the twelfth volume of South Indian Inscriptions puts his coronation at Ayirattali in 1218 or 1219, and says he restored the Chola kingdom to Kulottunga III and crowned Rajaraja III. The Pandya king had come to power, and for a moment the Chola crown was his to give.

The Hoysalas then took over the role. The table records the Hoysala king as styled "the establisher of the Chola king" about 1216, and again in 1221 to 1222, with his son Vira Narasimha II taking the title. In 1222 to 1223 Rajaraja III stopped paying tribute to the Pandya, and the Pandya invaded again, and Narasimha stepped in. By 1225 Maravarman Sundara-Pandya claimed in an inscription of his ninth year at Srirangam to have burnt Tanjore and Uraiyur. Uraiyur had been the first Chola city the poems named, a locality of Tiruchirappalli when this story began. A Pandya king claimed to have set fire to it.

The jailer

Inside the Chola country, the king's own chiefs had begun to act for themselves. Kopperunjinga, a Kadava chieftain of Pallava descent who had served Kulottunga III, took advantage of the confusion. The table puts the battle at Tellaru, after which Rajaraja III was confined at Sendamangalam, in 1231 to 1232, and notes that Kopperunjinga I "probably began to issue records in his own name" in the same years. Summaries of his inscriptions add that he took the epithet "Alagiyasiyan, who imprisoned the Chola and took the Chola country." Summaries say a Hoysala army moved to free the king. The table's later entry for the battle of Perambalur, in 1241 to 1242, says the Pallava chieftain kept the Hoysalas in confinement and levied tribute from the Pandyas, so the jailer had not stayed beaten.

The order of events from here is a long list of the same quarrels. The Hoysala king Narasimha was camped at Kanchi in 1230. Somesvara succeeded Narasimha in 1233 to 1234, and in the same years the Telugu-Choda chief Tikka captured Kanchi and left an inscription there dated Saka 1156. A chief named Gandagopala claimed in 1239, in the twenty-third year of Rajaraja III, to have killed a Hoysala king. Kopperunjinga II took over from his namesake in 1243. In 1246 Rajendra III came to the throne, and a general named Singana reached the country around Vedaranyam. Hoysala, Kadava, Pandya, Kakatiya and Telugu-Choda records all turn up in the Chola country in these years, and for some of the time two kings' inscriptions stand in the same temple at Tiruvannamalai.

Two Pandyas

The Pandyas had been quiet only in appearance. Jatavarman Sundara-Pandya I came to the throne in 1251 and Jatavarman Vira-Pandya in 1253. In 1258 a Pandya king besieged Sendamangalam, beat the Pallava chieftain and returned his land to him, a gesture of mastery that mirrored the Chola king's own habit of restoring defeated rivals. Vira-Pandya's record of his fourteenth year, 1267, says he collected tribute from a Kadava chief while camped at Chidambaram. Chidambaram was the temple where Rajaraja I's parents had spent their last years. By this table Maravarman Kulasekhara I took the Pandya throne in 1268. Jatavarman Sundara-Pandya I's last year is 1271.

The Chola side was not only retreating. Summaries of Rajendra III's reign credit him with an attempt at recovery. He is said to have made two Pandya princes submit, one of them Maravarman Sundara-Pandya II, and to have left inscriptions as far north as Cuddapah, until the Hoysala king Vira Someshvara sided with the Pandyas and Jatavarman Sundara-Pandya I rose in 1251.

The last entry in the table is the shortest. For 1279 it lists the last year of Kopperunjinga II, his thirty-sixth, and of Rajendra-Chola III, his thirty-third.

That is the last dated year of a Chola king in the records examined. Textbook accounts that cite Nilakanta Sastri's The Cōḷas add a battle: Maravarman Kulasekhara Pandya I, they say, met and defeated the combined armies of Rajendra III and the Hoysala king Ramanatha in 1279, and the Chola country passed to the Pandyas. One summary adds that Rajendra III is not known to have been killed, and that no Chola inscriptions are found after 1279. Only the last of those points is supported by the chronology examined here. No inscription seen describes the battle. What the stones show is a king who had reigned for thirty-three years in a country where a Hoysala king, a Kadava chief, a Telugu-Choda, a Pandya and a Ganga each claimed a share, and whose last dated record is a year number in 1279.

The kingdom that began in the Kaveri delta with a name carved on Ashoka's rock, a battle at Venni and a port at Puhar has no ending in the dated records examined, only a stopping place. The Pandyas, who stood beside the Cholas in that first list, and whose capital Madurai Kulottunga III twice claimed to have taken, were the ones camped at Chidambaram collecting tribute in 1267, twelve years before the last Chola date.
