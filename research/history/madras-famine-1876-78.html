A Pound of Grain and an Anna: The Madras Famine of 1876–78

Two monsoons failed across the Deccan and the south. What turned a drought into one of the deadliest famines of the nineteenth century was a run of decisions about grain, wages and money, and those decisions left a paper trail long enough to read.

Key takeaways

  • Published estimates of excess deaths run from about 5.6 million to 9.6 million, with a careful demographic estimate of 8.2 million; no figure for the Madras Presidency alone was found.
  • The January 1877 Temple wage gave a labourer 450 grams of grain and one anna a day; Madras's own Sanitary Commissioner argued for at least 680 grams plus protein.
  • Davis reports a record 6.4 million hundredweight of wheat exported to England during the famine; the figure is for India as a whole and is not traced to Madras stocks.
  • Two of the Congress's founders, Hume and Wedderburn, were unsettled by the official handling of this famine; claims that textbooks left it out are reported by several sources, but a deliberate decision to do so is not established.

1. Ragi, rain and the Deccan

In the country around Kolar and Bangalore the trouble began two years early. In 1874 heavy rain flattened the ragi crop, the millet the region lived on, and thin rainfall the following year dried up the lakes and cut into the stores people would need. By 1876 the failure had spread across the Deccan Plateau, and for two years it held the south and southwest of the subcontinent: the Madras and Bombay presidencies, and the princely states of Mysore and Hyderabad. In 1877 it crept north into the Central Provinces, the North-Western Provinces and a corner of Punjab. The Imperial Gazetteer of India, taking stock in 1907, put the affected area at about 670,000 square kilometres and the people in distress at 58.5 million.

The weather was not a local accident. Climate studies tie the drought to the 1877–78 El Niño acting together with an active Indian Ocean Dipole, a pattern that dried out India, China, South America and parts of Africa in the same years.

Rain explains the hunger. It does not explain why so many people starved, and for that the record turns from the sky to the offices of the Government of India.

2. The Commissioner and the bill

Three years earlier, in the Bihar famine of 1873–74, the government had shipped in rice from Burma and kept mortality low. It was then criticised for the cost. Sir Richard Temple, Lieutenant-Governor of Bengal at the time, took the criticism to heart, and when the south failed in 1876 he returned as Famine Commissioner for the Government of India, anxious not to be accused of extravagance a second time. A ThePrint analysis adds that Parliament's brief to him was to see that the colonial state did not spend too much on relief.

His policy had three parts. The grain trade was to be left alone, with no state interference. The tests for qualifying for relief were made stricter. And the rations were made smaller. Relief came in two forms: works for able-bodied men, women and working children, and charitable relief for small children, the elderly and the destitute. The Viceroy, Lord Lytton, backed him, and Mike Davis reports Temple's own priority as the disbursing of the smallest sum of money, with everything else subordinated to it.

3. A pound of grain and an anna

In January 1877 Temple cut the wage in the relief camps of Madras and Bombay. The new rate, which came to be called the Temple wage, was 450 grams of grain, about a pound, plus one anna for a man, and slightly less for a woman or a working child, in return for a long day of hard labour without shade or rest. The thinking behind it was a conviction of the age that generosity bred dependency, which officials called demoralisation.

The case against it came from inside the administration. W. R. Cornish, Sanitary Commissioner of Madras, drew on his own research into diets in the prisons of the Presidency and argued that a labourer on the works needed at least 680 grams of grain, with vegetables and protein besides. William Digby, who witnessed the crisis in the Madras Presidency and wrote the standard contemporary account of it, also opposed the cuts. Lytton sided with Temple. In March 1877 the Madras government raised its ration to 570 grams of grain and 43 grams of dal, about halfway to Cornish's figure, and by then many more people had died. In the Bombay Presidency the harder qualifying test pushed relief workers into strikes.

Davis puts the Temple wage at about 1,627 calories for a day of hard labour and sets it beside the 1,750 calories of the diet at Buchenwald, which also involved hard labour. The calculation and the comparison are his.

By early 1877 Temple was telling the government that he had the famine under control. Digby's answer, as Davis reports it, was that a famine can hardly be called controlled when it leaves a quarter of the people dead.

4. The grain that left

While Madras waited on its rations, the export machinery went on turning. Davis reports that Lytton oversaw the shipment to England of a record 6.4 million hundredweight of wheat, roughly 320,000 tons, during the famine years, and that the government held to its hands-off line on the grain trade. The historians who stress the exports also point to the spread of cash crops and the commodification of grain as part of why the drought cut so deep.

Care is needed over what the figure proves. It counts wheat leaving India as a whole, and the sources read for this piece do not break it down by district or show that any of it came out of Madras stocks. What it does show is the direction of policy. In 1873 the state had imported grain to feed a famine area. In 1876 it left the trade to the market and kept exporting.

5. Counting the dead

Tim Dyson, the demographer, cautions that estimating famine deaths is not straightforward: assumptions are needed, they are often left unstated, and political considerations can bend them. The published figures sit across a wide range. Official data for the British-administered provinces imply about 5.6 million excess deaths, as Visaria and Visaria note. Dyson cites Arup Maharatna's careful estimate of around 8.2 million, and at the top end Digby's 9.4 million for India. David Fieldhouse, using a different count, gives 4.3 million in widely spread areas in 1876–78 and another 1.2 million in the North-Western Provinces and Kashmir. The range most often quoted for the whole famine runs from 5.6 to 9.6 million.

The sources read here give no death toll for the Madras Presidency alone, and that number is left out rather than guessed. They do give other measures. The population of Mysore State fell by 874,000 against the 1871 census. In the second half of 1878 a malaria epidemic killed many more people who were already weakened by hunger. Across the Madras and Bombay presidencies the excess mortality cancelled out the natural growth of population between the censuses of 1871 and 1881. Afterwards, a large number of agricultural labourers and handloom weavers from south India left to work as indentured labourers on plantations in British tropical colonies.

6. What it cost the state

The Imperial Gazetteer kept an account. The Government of India spent Rs 30 million relieving 700 million units in British India, a unit being one person relieved for one day, and a further Rs 7.2 million on 72 million units in Mysore and Hyderabad. Land revenue of Rs 6 million was postponed or not enforced, and donations from Britain and the colonies came to Rs 8.4 million.

Dividing the first two totals by their units gives an average of about 0.69 anna per person per day in British India and 1.6 annas in the two princely states. That arithmetic is done here from the reported totals; the Gazetteer does not state it. Hall-Matthews makes a related comparison. The spending in the Bombay Presidency came to less than a fifth of what the Bihar famine of 1873–74 had cost, even though the 1876 famine covered far more ground and lasted longer.

Mysore, which governed itself, took a different route. It opened relief kitchens, imported grain from the neighbouring Madras Presidency, let people graze cattle in the forests for a time, and built and repaired tanks. People walked to Bangalore for work on the railway line to Mysore, paid in grain. Its Dewan, C. V. Rungacharlu, put the cost to the state at 160 lakhs, with a debt of 80 lakhs.

7. What the survivors sang

The famine lives on in Tamil literary tradition. A large number of kummi folk songs about it have been documented. Kamil Zvelebil describes a satire, the Panchalakshna Tirumugavilasam, published in 1899 by Villiappa Pillai, one of the court poets of Sivagangai. It runs to about 4,500 lines of humour and biting irony on the condition of the people in the great famine of 1876, and in it the god Sundaresvara of Madurai pleads his helplessness before the problems of the people hit by it.

Songs and satire are not statistics. They are the voice of the period from inside it, and they were still being composed and collected a generation later.

8. The Commission and the Codes

The scale of the dying forced the question of relief and protection back onto the government's desk. It led directly to the Famine Commission of 1880 and, in time, to the Indian Famine Codes. A ThePrint analysis reports that when the Government of India circulated the Famine Code in 1883 it laid down the principles of the famine wage as a directive for the provincial governments. The argument over rations that Cornish and Temple had fought out in 1877 thus ended as written procedure.

9. A party born of a famine, and what the textbooks say

Some British officials were unsettled by how the Government of India handled the famine, and in particular by the stifling of official debate about the best form of relief. Among them were William Wedderburn and A. O. Hume. Less than a decade later they helped found the Indian National Congress, and through it they influenced a generation of Indian nationalists. Two of that generation, Dadabhai Naoroji and Romesh Chunder Dutt, made the Great Famine a cornerstone of their economic critique of the Raj.

What the schoolbooks said is a thinner matter, and the sources are of mixed weight. Michel Danino, who chaired the NCERT committee that drafted the new social science textbooks, told ThePrint that famines were almost completely absent from earlier textbooks and are slowly becoming better known. A group reviewing a Telangana state Class 8 textbook in 2018 reported that it said the famines killed thousands, until its editorial board changed the word to millions after their critique. The Wire has reported that the new NCERT Class 8 book touches on famine and the drain of wealth. On the other side, the Indian History Congress has stated that the older NCERT books were written by leading scholars, among them Romila Thapar, Bipan Chandra and Satish Chandra.

The sources found report thin coverage of famines in older textbooks, but they do not establish a decision by any party to leave the famine out, and no source names the 1876–78 famine specifically. A claim of deliberate suppression would need the records of the textbook committees, which have not been seen here.

Sources and Evidence

The sources are grouped by how close they stand to the events. Each figure in the text carries the name of the scholar or document it comes from.

Contemporary and official

  • William Digby, The Famine Campaign in Southern India, 2 vols (1878), the contemporary account and the source of the 9.4 million estimate.
  • Imperial Gazetteer of India, vol. III (1907), chapter on famine, pp. 475–502, for the area and population affected, the relief accounts and the malaria epidemic.
  • Report of the Indian Famine Commission (1880), Part I.
  • Sir Richard Temple, Men and Events of My Time in India (1882).

Modern scholarship

  • Tim Dyson, A Population History of India (2018), for the range of death estimates, including Maharatna's 8.2 million.
  • David Hall-Matthews, "Historical Roots of Famine Relief Paradigms," Disasters 20(3) (1996), and "Inaccurate Conceptions," Modern Asian Studies 42(1) (2008), for the Temple wage, the spending comparison with Bihar and the link to the founders of the Congress.
  • David Arnold, "The 'discovery' of malnutrition and diet in colonial India," Indian Economic and Social History Review 31(1) (1994), for Cornish and the Madras ration.
  • Mike Davis, Late Victorian Holocausts (2001), for the wheat exports, Temple's remark on expenditure and the calorie comparison.
  • David Fieldhouse, in P. J. Marshall (ed.), The Cambridge Illustrated History of the British Empire (1996), p. 132; Tirthankar Roy, The Economic History of India, 1857–1947 (2006), for the census effect and emigration; Deepti Singh, "Climate and the Global Famine of 1876–78," Journal of Climate (2018).
  • Kamil Zvelebil, Tamil Literature (1974), pp. 218 ff., for the satire of Villiappa Pillai.

Journalism, commentary and advocacy

  • ThePrint, on famine wages, for Parliament's brief to Temple and the 1883 Famine Code; ThePrint interview with Michel Danino.
  • The Wire, on the new NCERT Class 8 textbook; the Indian History Congress statement of 14 July 2021, archived on the South Asia Citizens Wire; the eSamskriti critique of the Telangana Class 8 textbook (2018). These speak for their own positions and are used only to report what each says.

Open points

No source read for this piece gives a death toll for the Madras Presidency alone, or shows how much of the record wheat export came from Madras stocks. The cause of the thin textbook coverage is not established by what was read. A figure of 3.2 million tonnes of wheat exported, which appears on one popular site, conflicts with the 6.4 million hundredweight in Davis and was left out.

Author's note: the sources above were read in secondary and online editions, chiefly a compiled encyclopedia summary of the scholarship, not in the originals. Page references should be checked against the books before this piece is cited elsewhere.
