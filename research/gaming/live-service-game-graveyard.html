The Live-Service Graveyard: A Comparative Case Study of Why These Games Keep Failing

Highguard lasted 45 days. Concord lasted 14. The Day Before lasted 4. A comparative look at a decade of live-service shutdowns shows that launch numbers, development pedigree, and even publisher budget predict almost nothing about survival, and one factor predicts almost everything.

Abstract

Live-service games, online titles designed to run for years on continuous content updates rather than sell once and end, have become the video game industry's dominant high-budget strategy despite a visible and growing graveyard of high-profile failures. This paper compares eleven recent live-service shutdowns against one clear success case (Helldivers 2) using publicly reported launch dates, shutdown dates, peak and sustained player counts, and development costs where available, to test which factors actually predict survival.

The comparison shows that neither launch size, development budget, nor studio pedigree reliably predicts survival. XDefiant launched to over 3 million players and 300,000 concurrent users, then fell below 20,000 average players within three months. Concord, backed by a reported $200 to 400 million budget and an internal comparison to Star Wars, lasted 14 days. Highguard, built by a studio founded largely by veterans of Apex Legends and Titanfall, attracted 2 million players at launch and lost 90 percent of them within the first week, closing 45 days later. The one factor that does track cleanly with survival across this dataset is whether the game launched as a sequel or continuation of an already-established player base, as Helldivers 2 did, versus asking players to adopt an entirely new property from zero, as every failure examined here did. The paper closes by examining why live-service failures happen so much faster than single-player game failures: the economics of ongoing server and content costs, rather than a one-time sunk development cost, make a game with insufficient retention a live financial liability rather than a static loss, and publishers now act on that liability within weeks rather than years.

1. Introduction

The live-service model, an online game supported for years with seasons, battle passes, and continuous content, became the games industry's preferred answer to a specific problem: a single-purchase game generates revenue once, while a successful live-service title can generate it indefinitely. Fortnite, Destiny 2, and Genshin Impact are the model's proof of concept, and the revenue those games generate has pulled publisher after publisher toward chasing the same outcome. What gets discussed far less than the successes is how short the odds actually are, and how quickly a live-service failure becomes visible and final compared to almost any other kind of game failure.

This paper treats that visibility as useful data. A single-player game that undersells quietly fades from sales charts; a live-service game that fails to hold players gets shut down, on a specific announced date, with a specific reported player count trajectory behind it, because the ongoing cost of running it no longer justifies keeping the servers on. That creates an unusually rich, publicly documented dataset of failure, and this paper uses it to ask a specific, testable question: across a decade of these shutdowns, what actually predicted which games would survive and which wouldn't?

2. Methods

Eleven live-service shutdowns were selected for comparison, chosen for having the most complete publicly reported data on launch date, shutdown date, and player counts, spanning 2021 through 2026: Knockout City, Back 4 Blood, CrossfireX, Redfall, The Day Before, Foamstars, Concord, Suicide Squad: Kill the Justice League, XDefiant, and Highguard, the most recent and most extensively documented case. Helldivers 2 was included as a contrast case, being one of the very few titles in the same launch window and genre space to succeed decisively. Player count and date data are drawn from developer statements, platform storefront statistics (primarily Steam, via SteamDB and secondary reporting), and contemporaneous games-industry press coverage. Development cost figures, where reported, are flagged explicitly as unconfirmed industry estimates rather than audited financial data, since none of the companies involved have published verified budget figures for any title examined here. Where sources disagree on a specific number (for instance, Concord's development cost), the reported range is given rather than a single figure presented as more precise than the underlying sourcing supports.

3.2 Case Study: Highguard

Highguard, a free-to-play 3v3 hero shooter from Wildlight Entertainment, a studio founded largely by veterans of Respawn Entertainment's Titanfall and Apex Legends, was revealed at The Game Awards in December 2025 after more than three years in development and launched on January 26, 2026 across PC, PlayStation 5, and Xbox Series X/S, backed by Tencent funding.6 It attracted a genuinely large launch audience: 2 million players and close to 100,000 peak concurrent users on Steam alone.1 That audience did not hold. By mid-February, most of Wildlight's staff had been laid off, and the studio announced on March 3 that the game would close permanently, with servers going offline on March 12, 45 days after launch.4 Reporting following the shutdown noted the player base had fallen by roughly 90 percent within the first week alone, well before the layoffs were announced.5 The game's director publicly denied conspiracy theories about the cause of the collapse, stating that "nobody will know the true story of the studio or game,"3 while outside commentary pointed to a crowded hero-shooter market and the difficulty of repeating a previous team's success on a new, unestablished property.2

3.3 Case Study: Concord

Concord took a different, slower path to a similarly abrupt end. Firewalk Studios spent roughly eight years developing the hero shooter before Sony, which had acquired the studio in 2023, launched it on August 23, 2024.7 Reported development costs range from a $200 million initial development deal, per Kotaku's sourcing, up to a widely cited $400 million total figure once studio acquisition and additional spending are included, though neither figure has been officially confirmed by Sony.89 Internally, the game was reportedly described as a potential "Star Wars" for the PlayStation brand, and former staff described a "toxic positivity" culture that discouraged internal criticism during development.11 None of that translated to players: peak concurrent viewership on Steam has been reported at under 1,000, and total sales are estimated at around 25,000 copies.10 Sony pulled the game from sale and offered full refunds on September 6, 2024, 14 days after launch,12 and shut Firewalk Studios down entirely that November, laying off all 174 remaining staff.7

3.4 The Contrast Case: Helldivers 2

Helldivers 2, developed by Arrowhead Game Studios and published by Sony, launched in February 2024 and became the clearest live-service success of this entire period: over 12 million copies sold by the end of 2024, ranking third among best-selling US games that year, and more than 22.9 million total players reported by 2026.18 The distinction most commentary draws between Helldivers 2 and every failure examined above is that it is a sequel, continuing and expanding an existing, if smaller, established fanbase from the original Helldivers rather than asking players to adopt a wholly new property with no prior audience or goodwill.18 Sony's own trajectory over this period reflects the same lesson learned the hard way: a 2022 plan to ship a dozen live-service titles by 2025 was cut to six by 2024, and further projects (including a live-service God of War title and The Last of Us Online) were cancelled outright following Concord's collapse.1617

3.5 The Broader Market Pattern

The failures examined here are not occurring in a market with room to spare. Steam alone saw more than 20,000 game releases in a recent year, and industry analysis found that only about 15 percent of total time spent on the platform went to games released that same year,19 meaning new titles, live-service or not, are competing for attention against an enormous and growing back catalogue of already-established games with existing player bases. A new live-service game doesn't just need to be good; it needs to pull players away from whatever live-service game already has their time, attention, and, often, their existing in-game purchases.

4. Discussion

4.1 Why Launch Numbers Don't Predict Survival

XDefiant is the clearest single data point against using launch size as a survival predictor. It launched to over 3 million unique players and 300,000 concurrent users, numbers most of the failures in Table 1 would have taken as an unambiguous win. Within three months, its average player count had fallen below 20,000, a collapse of well over 90 percent, and active development ceased roughly thirteen months after launch.14 A strong launch, in a live-service context, measures curiosity, not commitment. Every game examined in this paper except Helldivers 2 had a launch large enough to look, in the moment, like the beginning of a success story.

4.2 Why Studio Pedigree Doesn't Predict Survival Either

Highguard's development team included veterans of two of the genre's most successful recent titles, Titanfall and Apex Legends, and that pedigree was explicitly part of the pitch investors and press responded to before launch. It didn't transfer. Having built one successful live-service game does not appear, from this dataset, to meaningfully raise the odds of building a second one from scratch, a pattern industry commentary following Highguard's shutdown explicitly identified: "past live service hits do not equal future live service hits."2 The skill required to design compelling core gameplay and the skill required to build and hold a sustainable live-service audience for an unproven new property appear to be much more separable than pre-launch marketing for these games tends to suggest.

4.3 What Actually Predicts Survival in This Dataset

The one variable that cleanly separates Helldivers 2 from every failure examined here is whether the game launched with an existing, established player relationship already in place. Helldivers 2 inherited goodwill, a known IP, and a pre-existing (if modest) fanbase from the 2015 original. Every failure in Table 1 asked players to adopt a brand-new property with zero prior relationship, competing directly against live-service games where players had often already invested hundreds of hours and real money into their existing account, cosmetics, and progress. Switching live-service games isn't like trying a new single-player title over a weekend; it means abandoning sunk investment in whatever a player is already playing. A new live-service game has to be good enough to justify that abandonment, and this dataset suggests that bar is cleared far less often than launch-week enthusiasm implies.

4.4 Why Live-Service Failures Happen So Fast

The speed of these collapses, days or weeks rather than the years a struggling single-player franchise might limp along for, follows directly from the economics of the model itself. A single-player game's development cost is sunk the moment it ships; a disappointing launch is a bad quarter, not an ongoing liability. A live-service game's servers, live-operations team, and content pipeline are an ongoing cost that only makes sense against ongoing revenue. The moment a game's trajectory makes clear that revenue won't materialize at the scale needed to justify that ongoing cost, and Highguard's 90 percent week-one drop, or Concord's sub-1,000 concurrent peak, are both exactly that kind of signal, continuing to operate the game is a growing loss with no offsetting asset, rather than a one-time loss already absorbed. That is precisely why these shutdowns get announced in weeks: the financial logic that would let a publisher wait and see is absent by design.

4.5 Limitations

This is a comparative case study built from publicly reported data, not an audited financial or statistical analysis. Development cost figures, especially Concord's widely cited $200 to 400 million range, remain industry estimates sourced from anonymous insiders and have not been confirmed by the publishers involved; they are presented here as reported ranges, not verified facts. The eleven failure cases were selected for data completeness and public visibility rather than through a systematic, exhaustive survey of every live-service shutdown in the period, which means this dataset likely overrepresents high-profile, heavily covered failures relative to smaller, less-publicized ones that may follow different patterns. Finally, a sample of one success case (Helldivers 2) against eleven failures cannot establish that the sequel/existing-audience factor is sufficient for success, only that its absence was common across every failure examined; other successful live-service titles (Fortnite, Genshin Impact, Destiny 2) were not included in this comparison and a fuller study would need to test whether the same pattern holds across a larger set of successes as well as failures.

5. Conclusion

Across eleven live-service shutdowns spanning 2021 to 2026, neither a strong launch, a large budget, nor an experienced development team reliably predicted which games would survive. XDefiant's 300,000-concurrent launch collapsed within months. Concord's reported $200 to 400 million budget bought it fourteen days. Highguard's team of Apex Legends and Titanfall veterans built a game that lost 90 percent of its launch audience in a week. The one pattern that does hold across this dataset is that the sole clear success, Helldivers 2, launched with an existing player relationship already in place, while every failure asked players to abandon whatever they were already invested in for something entirely new and unproven. And because a live-service game's costs are ongoing rather than sunk, the moment that bet visibly fails to pay off, within days for Concord, within a week for Highguard's real player retention, publishers now act on it fast. The graveyard keeps growing not because these teams don't know how to make good games, several of these titles received genuinely positive critical coverage, but because good is not the bar a new live-service game has to clear. Getting players to leave what they're already playing is.
