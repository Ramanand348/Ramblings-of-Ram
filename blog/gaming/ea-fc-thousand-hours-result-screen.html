A Thousand Hours of EA FC, and Why I No Longer Trust the Result Screen

Most sessions had some version of the same failure. Then I read what EA has patented and published, and the suspicion stopped looking paranoid.

Notebook Gaming October 2026 ~4 min read

I have 350 hours in FC 26 and roughly 1,000 across the last five games in the series. That is enough time to know what a normal match feels like, which is why the abnormal ones stand out. A comfortable lead evaporates in ten minutes. The opposing keeper starts saving everything. A defender who tracked back perfectly for eighty minutes freezes on the one run that costs a goal. In Clubs, where I spend most of my time, some version of this was present in almost every session I played.

The failures I can point to

Some of it has nothing to do with feel. I have had half my team dropped from a match by server problems and been left to finish a full game a player or more down. I have had an opponent's live join hang the game for everyone, with quitting as the only way out, and the game recorded that quit as a loss for my side. I have watched kick-off glitches decide the first seconds of a match before anyone had a say.

Other players report the same things. EA's own forums carry threads about live joins that freeze matches, a game frozen at 5-0 when the opponent made a substitution, opponents quitting at 0-0 with the result counting against the team that stayed, and players frozen in place and kicked mid-match. EA has even acknowledged kickoff goals: a gameplay producer described changes made to make them less frustrating for the defending team. Those are bugs, and bugs cost real results, and the game's answer is to log a loss.

Then I read the patents

The bugs I could live with. What changed how much I trust the rest was reading what EA has filed and published. EA holds a granted US patent for dynamic difficulty adjustment that describes changes the player cannot detect, driven by predicted retention. It holds a separate patent on matchmaking built around engagement, and it co-authored a 2017 paper that tested that idea on real data from one of its own games. EA's public denials cover the first patent and say nothing about the second. When a 2020 class action tried to test the claim, it ended in private briefings with EA's engineers, not a public audit.

That is not proof my matches were rigged, and I am not going to pretend it is. No public proof of per-match manipulation exists, and only EA can release the data that would settle it. I also cannot prove that nearly every second ball and 50-50 went to the opponent in my games. I can only say that is what I saw, over a very long time.

The money explains the mood

Suspicion comes easily once you look at how the game earns. At FC 26 launch, EA put 100k packs on sale on the first day of early access after promising a gentler power curve, and later admitted the curve had been too aggressive. In the Netherlands, a fine over Ultimate Team packs was overturned on the reasoning that packs sit inside a broader game of skill. Meanwhile EA's own patch notes confirmed a bug in which defenders outran attackers whatever their pace attributes. A game cannot comfortably be sold as skill in court while its own documentation shows attributes not deciding the race.

What would change my mind

Very little would. EA could publish match outcome data, or allow an independent audit of its matchmaking and difficulty code. It could also stop counting a server failure as the player's loss. Until then, I read the result screen as a report from a system with a motive, not a verdict on how I played. The full version, with every source, is in the research piece: Scripting, Engagement, and the Limits of Skill in EA Sports FC.
