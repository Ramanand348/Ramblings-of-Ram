The Completion Rate Elden Ring Was Built to Fix

FromSoftware didn't make Elden Ring easier than Sekiro. The achievement data shows something more specific: a deliberate pivot toward more paths to victory, and one side effect nobody planned for turned a single player into a folk hero.

Notebook Gaming September 2026 ~5 min read

Two hundred hours in, Elden Ring is still the game I'd point to first if someone asked what makes an open world actually worth exploring. Most of what gets written about why it succeeded lands on some version of "FromSoftware finally went open world, and the difficulty gives the exploration stakes." That's true, but it's also the explanation everyone already has. There's a more specific, better-documented story sitting in the game's own achievement data, and it starts with FromSoftware admitting, on the record, that their previous game had a problem they wanted to fix.

The Sekiro problem, stated plainly

In a PlayStation Blog interview published January 28, 2022, a month before Elden Ring's release, director Hidetaka Miyazaki said something FromSoftware doesn't usually say out loud: "I think more players will finish it this time." He was comparing Elden Ring directly against the studio's back catalogue, and the subtext was Sekiro specifically. Sekiro's own Steam achievement data backs up why that comparison mattered: just over 23 percent of Steam buyers reached the game's most common ending, with the other three endings sitting between 15 and 19 percent. Miyazaki was careful to add a caveat that gets dropped in most retellings of this story: "we have not intentionally tried to lower the game's difficulty." The fix wasn't going to be an easier game. It was going to be a differently structured one.

That distinction matters more than it sounds like it should, because it predicts exactly what changed. Elden Ring didn't nerf its bosses relative to Sekiro's. It gave players more ways to route around a wall they couldn't get through yet: an open world where the next challenge is rarely the only challenge available, spirit ash summons that let a struggling player bring an AI ally into a boss fight, and a much less restrictive multiplayer summon system for bringing in actual other players. None of that makes Malenia weaker. It makes giving up on Malenia today and coming back to her in twenty hours, better equipped, an option that Sekiro's more linear structure didn't really offer.

What the achievement data actually shows

Steam's own global stats page for Elden Ring is public, and it tells a more interesting story than "more people finished it." As of recent tracking, 71.3 percent of players have beaten Margit, the game's first major roadblock. That number drops steadily but not cliff-like through the game's demigod bosses: Godrick at 65.4 percent, Rennala at 58.2 percent, Radahn at 53.4 percent, Morgott at 49.7 percent, Mohg at 40.7 percent, Maliketh at 41.5 percent. By the time you reach the actual endings, the numbers land at roughly 19.6 percent for the default Elden Lord ending and 25.9 percent for Ranni's Age of Stars questline, whichever a given player got there first.

Here's the part that undercuts the tidy "way more accessible" narrative: those ending numbers aren't dramatically higher than Sekiro's 23 percent for its most common ending. If the story were purely "FromSoftware fixed the completion problem," you'd expect a much bigger gap. What the data shows instead is that Elden Ring's real gain is in breadth, not just completion. Far more players are getting meaningfully far into the game (that 71 percent Margit number, or the roughly 22 to 34 percent who've beaten Malenia, an entirely optional, notoriously brutal boss most players never even need to find) than the ending-rate alone suggests. Overall full completion sits around 10 percent on Steam and PlayStation, which is unusually high for anything in this genre. The pivot wasn't "more people see the credits." It was "more people get deep enough into the game that quitting feels like their choice rather than the game's."

A side effect nobody put in a design doc

The multiplayer summon system built for this purpose produced something FromSoftware almost certainly didn't plan for as a business outcome, but that ended up mattering for exactly the reason the system existed: a single player becoming a genuine folk hero because the game made it structurally easy for him to help strangers.

In April 2022, a player named Klein Tsuboi started showing up in other people's games under the summon sign "Let Me Solo Her," wearing nothing but a jar-shaped helmet, wielding dual katanas, and soloing Malenia for players who'd summoned him for help. By May, he'd done it over a thousand times, livestreamed to thousands of viewers. By that summer he'd passed two thousand. Bandai Namco noticed. They sent him an actual sword, engraved with "Rise, Tarnished," a full-tang blade shipped alongside a wood carving of Malenia and a hand-written note, delayed for weeks by French customs flagging a package containing a large bladed weapon.

None of that happens without the specific design choice underneath it: an open, low-friction summon system that lets any player drop a sign in someone else's world without a restrictive covenant or item requirement gating who gets to help whom. That's the same system built to solve the completion problem described above, and it produced, as a byproduct, a community-run support layer that the achievement data can't see but that almost certainly pulled some real number of players past Malenia who would otherwise have logged off for good right there. Tsuboi wasn't unique in kind, just in scale; the same summon sign is a fixture in front of every hard boss in the game, left there by thousands of players doing a smaller version of the same thing.

Why this is the more interesting success story

"Open world plus FromSoftware difficulty" explains why Elden Ring is good. It doesn't explain why it sold over 30 million copies and kept 45 percent of its Steam audience playing past the 100-hour mark, a genuinely unusual retention figure for any single-player game, let alone one built around repeated boss deaths. The more specific explanation is that FromSoftware identified a real, measurable problem in their previous release, said so publicly before Elden Ring even launched, and shipped a structural fix rather than a difficulty slider. The achievement data is the closest thing to a public receipt for whether that fix actually worked, and it shows something more precise than "yes": not a dramatically higher finishing rate, but a dramatically wider base of players who got far enough in to feel like the game was theirs before they ever reached the end, with an entire unplanned community support system helping carry people the rest of the way.
