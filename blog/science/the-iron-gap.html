The Iron Gap: Why More Than Half of Indian Women Are Anaemic, and Why It Got Worse

India has run a dedicated national anaemia program since 2018, with a name that translates to "Anaemia-Free India." The most recent national survey found anaemia among women had gone up, not down. The explanation is not one cause. It is a stack of them, several with deep cultural roots.

Notebook Science August 2026

Here is a number worth sitting with: 57 percent. That is the share of Indian women aged 15 to 49 found to be anaemic in the National Family Health Survey's fifth round (NFHS-5, 2019 to 2021), the country's largest and most rigorous health survey, based on direct blood testing rather than self-report. It is not a rural-poverty statistic that spares the cities, and it is not a historical figure inherited from a poorer past. Five years earlier, in NFHS-4, the number was 53 percent. In the interval between those two surveys, the Indian government ran a dedicated national program, Anaemia Mukt Bharat, literally "Anaemia-Free India," built specifically to bring that number down. It went up instead.

That is the puzzle worth working through here: not just why so many Indian women are anaemic, since the broad answer, not enough usable iron reaching the bloodstream, is well known, but why a well-funded, well-designed national program aimed directly at the problem seems to have coincided with it getting worse. The honest answer is not that the program failed for one reason. It ran into a stack of separate structural and cultural obstacles, several of which a supplement pill was never going to fix on its own.

The numbers, and the state-level spread

NFHS-5 covered 315,069 women of reproductive age nationally, and the aggregate figures break down further into 57.2 percent of non-pregnant women and 52.2 percent of pregnant women affected. Among the eight so-called Empowered Action Group states, a set of states the government itself flags as lagging on health indicators, the anaemia rate among women runs even higher than the national figure. A separate analysis of India's 112 "Aspirational Districts," administrative units specifically targeted for accelerated development, found the anaemia rate there rose from 58.7 percent to 61.1 percent across the same two survey rounds, with over 60 percent of those districts moving in the wrong direction and a quarter of them rising by ten percentage points or more. The two highest-prevalence districts recorded in the data, Simdega in Jharkhand and Udalgiri in Assam, reached 78.2 and 81.5 percent respectively. West Bengal, Tripura, and Assam post the highest state-level rates in the country among the larger states, in the 66 to 67 percent range.

The same data lets researchers identify who is most affected within that already-large group. Women with three or four children, women currently breastfeeding, women from Scheduled Tribe communities, and women in the poorest wealth quintile all showed significantly higher odds of anaemia than their counterparts. Notably, the increase between the two surveys was not concentrated only among the poorest: overweight women, a group usually assumed to be better nourished by conventional measures, saw one of the larger increases in anaemia prevalence of any subgroup, a detail that fits a broader pattern running through this whole picture, that anaemia in India is now substantially a problem of diet quality and iron bioavailability, not simply calorie or food quantity.

Where the government program ran into trouble

Anaemia Mukt Bharat, launched in 2018, was not a token effort. It bundled six interventions together: iron and folic acid (IFA) supplementation, biannual deworming, behaviour-change communication, digital anaemia testing, food fortification, and attention to non-nutritional causes of anaemia. On paper it followed international best practice closely. In practice, a review of the program's own supply chain found it plagued by the same problems that undermined every anaemia program India has run since the 1970s: frequent stock-outs of supplements, inconsistent training for the frontline health workers who deliver them, and chronically low compliance among the women who are supposed to take them. One evaluation of a grassroots supplementation drive found IFA compliance sitting around 30 percent, meaning seven in ten girls given the tablets were not reliably taking them, for reasons ranging from side effects to simple habit failure to a lack of felt urgency about a condition with vague, easily dismissed symptoms like tiredness.

A separate and arguably more fundamental critique came from researchers using India's Comprehensive National Nutrition Survey data: they found that deficiencies in vitamin A, vitamin B12, vitamin D, zinc, and folate, not iron alone, were significant contributors to the country's anaemia burden, and concluded that a strategy built almost entirely around iron and folic acid tablets was treating one input in a genuinely multi-nutrient problem. Iron deficiency is estimated to cause perhaps half of anaemia cases in India, which leaves a great deal for iron pills alone to be blamed for not fixing.

A diet that actively works against iron absorption

Even where dietary iron intake looks adequate on paper, a specific and often overlooked mechanism blunts how much of it the body actually uses. Iron comes in two forms: heme iron, found in meat, which the body absorbs efficiently, and non-heme iron, found in plants and grains, which it absorbs far less efficiently and which is highly sensitive to what else is eaten alongside it. The Indian diet is overwhelmingly built around the second kind. Wheat, rice, and pulses, staples of most Indian meals, are rich in phytates, compounds that the Food and Agriculture Organization notes can block 51 to 82 percent of the non-heme iron in a given meal from being absorbed at all. Indian food science research has also measured that ordinary home cooking of cereals and pulses causes a further 22 to 24 percent loss of their iron content before it even reaches the plate.

Layered on top of that is a genuinely distinctive cultural variable: tea. Black tea is heavily consumed throughout India, often multiple times a day, and its polyphenols bind to non-heme iron in the gut and prevent absorption; researchers have measured a single 250ml cup of black tea cutting non-heme iron absorption from an accompanying meal by roughly half, an effect that does not require the tea to be consumed simultaneously with food to take hold. None of this means Indian diets lack iron on paper. It means a meaningful share of the iron that is eaten never actually becomes usable, which is a different and more stubborn problem than simple undereating, and one that a supplement handed out without dietary guidance does not automatically solve.

A 2023 qualitative study of rural households in Haryana makes the point unusually concretely. Researchers found that the majority of households studied were following an adequate, even generous diet by conventional measures: more than the recommended daily calorie intake, and sufficient protein and fat. Only 34 percent of those same households had sufficient iron intake, and just 20 percent had sufficient folate. Over half of household members were clinically anaemic regardless of adequate calories. The paper's own conclusion was blunt: it is not only the quantity of food that determines nutritional health, but its quality and source, and by that measure a full plate in much of rural India can still be a low-iron plate.

The vegetarian B12 gap

Vitamin B12 exists naturally only in animal-source foods, and India has, by a wide margin, the largest vegetarian population of any country in the world. The consequence shows up starkly in the clinical literature. Estimates of B12 deficiency across the general Indian population range from roughly 47 to 75 percent depending on the study and threshold used, and the number climbs further among strict vegetarians specifically, with multiple Indian studies finding deficiency rates of 60 to 70 percent in that group against roughly 30 percent among non-vegetarians in the same communities. One Delhi-based study found B12 deficiency in more than 60 percent of pregnant women, a population for whom the vitamin is directly tied to neural tube development in the fetus. B12 deficiency is not the same condition as iron deficiency, and it does not always produce anaemia on its own, but the two frequently coexist, and B12's own contribution to anaemia is well established enough that the CNNS-based research cited earlier flagged it as a significant independent factor.

The vitamin D paradox

India receives, by most estimates, around 300 clear sunny days a year, and vitamin D is synthesized in skin exposed to sunlight rather than obtained mainly through diet. By any naive expectation, deficiency should be rare. Instead, surveys have found roughly 80 percent of urban Indians and 70 percent of rural Indians deficient, a gap researchers openly describe as a paradox. The explanations that have emerged point to modern indoor lifestyles and long working hours under artificial light, air pollution dense enough in major cities to meaningfully block the ultraviolet wavelengths that trigger vitamin D synthesis in skin, and, particularly relevant to women, clothing norms: extensive body coverage is markedly more common for women than men across much of India, especially in the north, and is consistently identified in the clinical literature as an independent risk factor for deficiency, on top of whatever time is actually spent outdoors.

Menstruation, stigma, and a food gap that compounds itself

Menstrual blood loss is a well-established driver of iron deficiency in women everywhere, but in India it intersects with a specific set of cultural practices that plausibly widen the gap further. Menstrual taboos rooted in longstanding cultural and religious norms remain widespread, and in many households they extend specifically to food: women following certain dietary restrictions during menstruation, including foods commonly considered "sour," such as curd, tamarind, and pickle, based on traditional beliefs about menstrual health, sometimes alongside broader restrictions on movement, cooking, and participation in household or religious life during their period. Separately, national surveys have found a large share of menstruating women in India still lack reliable access to hygienic sanitary products, a distinct problem from diet but one that researchers studying menstrual health policy in India have argued reflects the same underlying pattern: a subject treated as something to manage in silence and shame rather than a routine physiological process with routine physiological needs, of which replacing lost iron is one.

What the fortification effort has, and has not, achieved

Recognizing that supplementation compliance was a weak link, India has increasingly turned to food fortification, most notably double-fortified salt (DFS), which delivers both iron and iodine through a staple already present in nearly every household's kitchen regardless of literacy, income, or health-system contact. Results have been genuinely positive in some settings: one program in twenty districts of Madhya Pradesh reported a 6 percentage point drop in anaemia, and a controlled study among schoolchildren in southern India recorded anaemia falling from 15.1 to 5 percent. But a national economic modeling study of DFS's likely impact found something more sobering specifically for women: the same intervention modeled to cut iron-deficiency anaemia in men from 10.6 percent to 0.7 percent, essentially solving the male side of the problem, was projected to move the number for women only from 23.8 to 20.9 percent, a modest improvement against a starting point roughly three times higher than men's. The researchers running that model attributed the gap directly to menstrual iron losses that a uniform, population-wide fortification dose was never calibrated to fully offset for women specifically.

What this adds up to

None of these threads is exotic on its own. Cereal-heavy diets, tea culture, a large vegetarian population, indoor working life, menstrual stigma, and a supply-chain-strained supplementation program are each, individually, well-documented and unsurprising. What the NFHS-4-to-NFHS-5 rise makes clear is that they are not offsetting each other. They are compounding, on a baseline of biological need, menstruation, pregnancy, and breastfeeding, that was already higher for women than for men before any of these other factors entered the picture. A national program built mainly around handing out iron tablets was always going to struggle against a problem this layered, since a pill does not neutralize phytates in the evening's roti, does not change what a household believes it is safe to eat during a period, and does not put more usable iron into a fortification dose calibrated for the general population rather than for women's specific losses.

None of this is a case for self-diagnosis or unsupervised supplementation, since iron in particular can cause real harm in excess and the standard blood tests for anaemia are simple, widely available, and already built into most antenatal and general health checkups in India. But given how consistently these numbers keep moving in the wrong direction nationally, a persistent feeling of fatigue is worth treating as a question for a blood test to answer, not a condition to assume is normal.

This piece draws on NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 data as analyzed in peer-reviewed studies on Aspirational Districts and Empowered Action Group states, a 2024 review of Anaemia Mukt Bharat program implementation, Comprehensive National Nutrition Survey-based research on multi-nutrient contributors to anaemia, FAO research on dietary iron bioavailability in Indian diets, published studies on vitamin B12 and vitamin D deficiency prevalence in India, a 2023 qualitative study of menstrual dietary practices in rural Haryana, and published economic modeling of double-fortified salt's impact by sex.
