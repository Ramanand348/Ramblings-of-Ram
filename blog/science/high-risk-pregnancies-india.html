Half of India's Pregnancies Are Now High-Risk

Nearly half of pregnancies surveyed under NFHS-5 were classified high-risk. Maternal deaths, meanwhile, keep falling. Both things are true, and the gap between them says something about how India's maternal health system has changed.

Notebook Science September 2026 ~1,260 words ~6 min read

A recent analysis of National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5) data, drawn from a sample of 23,853 currently pregnant women, put a number on something obstetricians in India have been saying informally for years: 49.4 percent of pregnancies now fall into the high-risk category. Not a subgroup. Roughly every other pregnancy in the country.

"High-risk" isn't a loose descriptor here. It's a specific classification built from maternal factors like age and prior complications, current medical conditions, lifestyle risks, and adverse outcome history, tracked against a woman's health status at the time of survey. And the share of pregnancies falling into it has been climbing for two decades, not just this survey round. Self-reported maternal complications in India rose from 43.6 percent to 53.7 percent between the 2005-06 and 2015-16 rounds of NFHS. Researchers could trace only about a fifth of that increase to the socio-demographic and service-utilization variables they had data for, things like education, wealth, and healthcare access. Most of the rise remains unexplained by the usual suspects.

What's actually tipping pregnancies into high-risk

An 11-year observational study at a tertiary perinatal referral centre offers the clearest breakdown of what moves a low-risk pregnancy into a high-risk one. Gestational diabetes accounted for 36.3 percent of reclassifications, hypertensive disorders for 17.7 percent, and thyroid disorders for 14.9 percent. These are metabolic and endocrine conditions, not the obstetric emergencies that dominate public perception of pregnancy risk, and their rise tracks a broader shift in the health of Indian women well before pregnancy ever enters the picture.

Obesity among women of reproductive age in India has climbed steadily across every NFHS round since the surveys began tracking it. Combined overweight and obesity among women went from 12.6 percent in 2005-06 to 20.7 percent in 2015-16 nationally, and in urban India specifically the figure moved from 23 percent to 33 percent between 2005-06 and 2019-21. Looked at over the longer arc, obesity alone among ever-married women of reproductive age roughly tripled, from 5.1 percent in 1998-99 to 15.7 percent by 2019-21, with women aged 35 to 49 carrying nearly four times the odds of obesity as younger women in the same surveys. Diabetes prevalence has moved in step, rising with age across both national survey rounds. Age at conception plays a smaller but real role too: the same tertiary-centre study found the share of women conceiving after 35 climbing to 5.7 percent, still modest in absolute terms but moving in one direction.

Some of the rise in "high-risk" classification is almost certainly better detection rather than a true increase in illness. Digital anaemia testing, wider antenatal screening, and more routine bloodwork catch conditions that earlier survey rounds would have simply missed. But detection can't explain a metabolic disease trend line that shows up independently in body-mass and diabetes data collected outside the maternal health system entirely. Both things are happening at once, and they compound each other: a population entering pregnancy with more underlying metabolic risk, screened by a system that's gotten better at finding it.

The system's response has leaned on surgery

What happens once a pregnancy is flagged as high-risk is where the picture gets more uneven. India's national caesarean section rate rose from 17 percent in NFHS-4 (2015-16) to 21.5 percent in NFHS-5 (2019-21), already above the 10 to 15 percent range the WHO considers the point past which C-sections stop improving maternal or newborn survival at a population level. Nationally representative estimates put the number of avoidable C-sections in India at 1.8 million, among the highest counts of any lower-middle-income country.

The national average understates how uneven this is. Telangana's overall C-section rate reached 60.7 to 63.7 percent depending on the study, with private-facility rates in the state crossing 80 percent by 2021. Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Kerala, and Jammu and Kashmir all report elevated rates well past the WHO range, concentrated overwhelmingly in private facilities. And the public-private split is widening rather than closing: between 2016 and 2021, C-section rates in public hospitals declined slightly nationally, from 15.5 to 14.3 percent, while private hospitals kept climbing. Researchers studying the trend point to financial incentives, scheduling convenience, and, in some regions, patient preference shaped by fear of prolonged labour or beliefs about auspicious delivery timing, as the drivers behind the private-sector rise, rather than medical necessity tracking the actual rise in high-risk pregnancies.

This matters past the individual delivery room. A hospital performing surgery at two to three times the medically justified rate is absorbing capacity and resources that a genuinely higher caseload of complicated pregnancies also needs, and it makes the C-section data far less useful as a signal of where real risk is concentrated. The state with the country's highest surgical birth rate is not obviously the state with the country's most medically complicated pregnancies.

Preterm births, and a mortality trend that cuts the other way

NFHS-5 data also shows a sharp rise in preterm birth rates across India, with wide variation by state, and preterm birth carries a 3.24-fold increase in neonatal mortality risk. That's the part of this story that reads as straightforwardly worsening.

Set against it is a number that complicates any simple narrative of a maternal health system in decline: India's maternal mortality ratio has fallen sharply and consistently, from 130 deaths per 100,000 live births in 2014-16 to 93 in 2019-21 and 88 in 2021-23, a decline of well over 80 percent since 1990. Rising complications and falling deaths sound contradictory until you consider what sits between them: institutional delivery rates, antenatal care coverage, and access to emergency obstetric services have all expanded substantially over the same period, meaning more complications are being caught and managed before they become fatal, rather than more complications simply appearing. The gap between the EAG states, Bihar, Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh, and Assam, which together accounted for roughly three-quarters of India's maternal deaths, and the rest of the country also hasn't closed, which is its own separate problem sitting underneath the national average.

The two trends, more complications and fewer deaths, aren't actually in tension. They describe a system doing meaningfully more to keep women alive through pregnancies that are, on average, medically harder than they used to be, while still leaning on a surgical intervention that the WHO's own evidence says doesn't move that mortality number once you're past roughly 15 percent of deliveries.

A few threads, not one story

Rising obesity and diabetes among women of reproductive age. Better screening that catches conditions earlier surveys missed. A private hospital sector performing surgery at rates disconnected from the underlying medical need. A public sector managing more complexity with a shrinking surgical footprint. Falling maternal deaths sitting alongside all of it. None of these threads on its own explains why "high-risk" now describes half of Indian pregnancies, and taken together they don't resolve into a single culprit either. What they describe instead is a maternal health system absorbing a real increase in clinical burden while still getting substantially better at keeping women alive through it, with the C-section data suggesting that the system's response to that burden hasn't been calibrated as carefully as the outcomes themselves have improved.

This piece draws on NFHS-4 and NFHS-5 data as analyzed in peer-reviewed studies on high-risk pregnancy classification, an 11-year tertiary-centre study on risk reclassification during pregnancy, published research on overweight, obesity, and diabetes trends among Indian women of reproductive age across NFHS rounds, PLOS One and PLOS Global Public Health studies on preventable and district-level caesarean section rates, and Sample Registration System bulletins on maternal mortality in India.
