The Iron Gap: Why So Many Women Are Running Low on Iron and Vitamins Right Now

Modern women in wealthy countries have more access to food, supplements, and medical testing than any generation before them, and a large, and by some measures growing, share of them are still deficient. The explanation is not one cause. It is a stack of them, several of which are quite recent.

Notebook August 2026

Here is a strange fact about the wealthiest, best-fed societies in human history: a large share of the young women living in them are short on iron. Not in a famine, not in a food desert necessarily, but in the ordinary course of eating three meals a day in a country with a full grocery store on every corner. And the gap is not shrinking. On several measures it has been widening for two decades.

The instinct is to reach for a single villain: bad diets, or too much screen time indoors, or some new drug everyone is suddenly taking. The real picture is less satisfying and more interesting. It is a handful of separate, mostly unrelated shifts in medicine, diet, and lifestyle that happen to land on the same population at the same time, plus a diagnostic system that was, for most of its history, calibrated without much thought for the group it now most needs to catch.

The numbers, stated plainly

Start with the most basic government data available. The CDC's National Center for Health Statistics tracks anemia (a shortage of healthy red blood cells, most commonly caused by too little iron) through direct blood testing in a nationally representative survey called NHANES. Their most recent report, covering August 2021 through August 2023, found anemia in 17.4 percent of American girls aged 12 to 19, and 14.0 percent of women aged 20 to 59. The equivalent numbers for men and boys in the same age brackets were 0.9 percent and 3.9 percent. This is not a small gap. Teenage girls in the United States are currently about nineteen times more likely to be anemic than teenage boys.

That gap is not new, but it has been getting wider. The same CDC report notes that anemia prevalence among women aged 15 to 49 rose from 7.8 percent in 2000 to 11.5 percent in 2018, even as the World Health Organization has spent that entire period trying to cut the number in half as a stated global health target. A 2025 review in JAMA put the picture in starker terms for wealthy countries specifically: in high-income nations, roughly 38 percent of nonpregnant women of reproductive age have some degree of iron deficiency even without full-blown anemia, alongside another 13 percent with iron-deficiency anemia on top of that. That first number, 38 percent, describes a condition most of those women have probably never been told they have.

A measurement problem built into the system

That last point is not incidental. Part of why so many cases go unflagged traces back to how the standard blood test for iron status, serum ferritin, was calibrated in the first place. Reference ranges for lab tests are usually built by measuring a large sample of "healthy" people and treating the middle 95 percent of their results as normal. A 2023 paper in the American Society of Hematology's education program pointed out the flaw specific to ferritin: an estimated 30 to 50 percent of women have little to no iron stored in their bone marrow, meaning a huge share of the "healthy" reference population used to define normal ferritin was already iron deficient. The bottom of the normal range for women ended up defined by women who were not actually fine, just common.

The consequence shows up in clinics today. A 2025 hematology conference abstract describing patients screened at one health system found that nearly a third had ferritin levels below the threshold most specialists now consider clinically low (30 nanograms per milliliter), while still sitting above their lab's official lower limit of normal, and most of those cases had never been diagnosed or treated. Over ninety percent of the affected patients were female. None of this required anything to get worse in the population. It only required the reference range itself to have been drawn incorrectly from the start, largely on the backs of study samples that never adequately screened for menstruation, pregnancy, or prior childbirth as risk factors before labeling someone's ferritin "normal."

What actually changed in the diet

Set the measurement issue aside and there is also a real dietary shift behind part of the numbers. Heme iron, the form of iron found in meat and by far the most easily absorbed kind, is two to three times more bioavailable than the non-heme iron found in plants. A 2025 nutrition review noted that iron deficiency among American women aged 15 to 49 has been increasing over roughly the past fifteen years, attributing part of that rise specifically to declining beef consumption and its replacement with chicken and other lower-iron proteins, a shift partly encouraged by dietary guidelines that have consistently pushed Americans toward less red meat on cardiovascular and cancer-risk grounds.

That dietary shift is not gender-neutral. A 2024 study published in the journal Sex Roles tracked dietary habits among 12,704 students at an American university over fifteen years and found the entire national rise in vegetarianism was attributable to women. Female students identifying as vegetarian or vegan rose from 4.3 percent to 8.7 percent between 2008 and 2023, while the male rate stayed flat, and even ticked down slightly. Separate national polling backs this up: American women are consistently and substantially more likely than men to adopt plant-based diets, most often citing ethical concerns about animal welfare, where men more often cite environmental reasons. Since vitamin B12 exists naturally only in animal-source foods, and iron absorption is markedly lower without them, a diet shift concentrated among women plausibly compounds directly onto a baseline of higher iron needs that menstruation already creates. One widely cited estimate puts B12 deficiency at up to 40 percent among vegetarians and as high as 62 percent among vegan women during pregnancy specifically, a population already carrying the highest nutritional stakes of any group in this discussion.

The sunlight problem

Vitamin D adds a third, largely separate thread. It is not really a dietary vitamin at all for most people; it is manufactured in skin exposed to sunlight, and food sources are limited. National Geographic's 2026 reporting on the topic pointed to two lifestyle factors that studies have found are more common among women than men: heavier sunscreen use and, again, plant-based eating, since vitamin D in food occurs mostly in fatty fish, egg yolks, and fortified dairy. Neither factor is something doctors are eager to reverse, since sunscreen use prevents skin cancer and plant-based diets carry their own benefits; the honest recommendation from researchers is closer to "know you may need to test for this and supplement accordingly" than "change the underlying behavior."

Two very current complications

Two more contributors are recent enough that they barely existed as population-level factors five years ago.

The first is the GLP-1 weight-loss drug wave. Ozempic, Wegovy, Mounjaro, and their relatives are used at meaningfully higher rates by women than men: a late-2025 KFF poll found 15 percent of women currently taking a GLP-1 drug versus 9 percent of men, and a RAND survey the same year found women aged 30 to 49 more than twice as likely as men in that bracket to have used one. These drugs work partly by sharply cutting appetite and food intake, and a growing body of observational research has linked their use to lower vitamin D, iron, and B-vitamin status, plausibly by simple reduction in food volume rather than any direct drug toxicity. One pilot study found measurably reduced intestinal iron absorption after just ten weeks of semaglutide use, and a large registry analysis found GLP-1 users had a 54 percent greater risk of low ferritin than similar patients on a different class of diabetes medication. Researchers studying this are careful to note that causality is not fully settled, since many patients likely started with lower nutrient stores to begin with, but the pattern is consistent enough across studies that clinicians increasingly recommend baseline nutrient screening before starting these drugs.

The second is a shift in contraceptive preference. Survey data from the past several years shows a rising number of women moving away from hormonal birth control, citing concerns about hormonal side effects, toward non-hormonal options including the copper IUD. The mechanism connecting this to iron status is well established in the medical literature: multiple studies dating back decades have measured menstrual blood loss increasing by roughly 50 to 55 percent in the months after a copper IUD is placed, and a systematic review comparing copper IUD users to those using a hormonal IUD found a corresponding drop in average hemoglobin and ferritin levels in the copper group. Most women tolerate this fine. Some do not, and a device chosen specifically to avoid one set of side effects quietly introduces a different one.

Is this actually new, or are we just looking harder?

It is worth asking the skeptical question directly, since it is the same question this site tries to ask of any claimed trend: is iron deficiency really increasing, or has testing and awareness simply improved enough that more existing cases are being caught?

The honest answer is a bit of both, and the two effects run in different directions on the actual numbers. The NHANES anemia trend from 2000 to 2018 is not a testing artifact. It comes from the same direct hemoglobin blood test applied consistently across a nationally representative sample for two decades, which means the measured rise in anemia reflects an actual change in the population, not a change in who happened to get tested. At the same time, the ferritin reference-range problem cuts the opposite way: it means the true historical burden of iron deficiency without anemia was almost certainly always higher than official statistics showed, underestimated for decades by a flawed lab threshold rather than freshly created by anything happening now. Put together, the picture is not "this problem appeared out of nowhere." It is closer to "a real, measurable increase in anemia has been layered on top of a much larger, older pool of undiagnosed non-anemic deficiency that clinical medicine is only now starting to properly count."

What this adds up to

None of the individual factors here is dramatic on its own. Somewhat less red meat, a bit more sunscreen, a popular new drug class, a birth-control trend, a badly drawn reference range from decades ago; any one of these sounds like a footnote. What makes the picture worth writing down is that they are landing on the same population simultaneously, a population that already has a meaningfully higher baseline nutritional requirement than men because of menstruation, pregnancy, and breastfeeding, requirements that most of these systems, dietary guidelines, drug trials, and lab reference ranges alike, were not originally built with much attention to. The 2023 hematology paper on ferritin ranges put it plainly: there is no biological reason serum ferritin should differ by sex at all. The fact that it does in practice, and has for decades, says more about who these reference ranges were built to describe than about anything inherent to being a woman.

None of this is a case for self-diagnosis or supplementing without testing first, since both iron and vitamin D can cause real harm in excess and the symptoms of deficiency (fatigue, brain fog, hair thinning) overlap with a long list of unrelated conditions. But if a standard blood panel keeps coming back "normal" while the fatigue does not go away, it is at least worth knowing that the definition of normal here has a documented, decades-long history of not quite fitting the people it was supposed to describe.

This piece draws on the CDC/NCHS National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data brief on anemia prevalence (December 2024), a 2025 JAMA review on iron deficiency in adults, a 2023 American Society of Hematology education paper on ferritin reference ranges, a 2024 Sex Roles study on gender differences in vegetarianism, a 2026 National Geographic report on vitamin D deficiency in women, RAND and KFF 2025 survey data on GLP-1 drug use by sex, and published research on copper IUD effects on menstrual blood loss and iron status.
