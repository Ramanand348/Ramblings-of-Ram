The Sun, a Sweet Fruit, and an Army of Monkeys: Two Legends Behind the Hanuman Chalisa

Tulsidas is said to have written the Hanuman Chalisa in a Mughal prison cell, freed only when an army of monkeys stormed the palace. Centuries later, one of its verses started circulating as proof he'd calculated the distance to the sun. Neither story holds up quite the way it gets told, and both are more interesting once you see why.

Notebook History September 2026 ~1,800 words ~8 min read

The Hanuman Chalisa is, by any measure, one of the most recited pieces of text on earth. Forty verses in Awadhi, composed in praise of Hanuman, said daily in millions of homes, playing from phone speakers in taxis and temples and construction sites across North India. It's also, for a devotional hymn, unusually surrounded by claims that sound less like faith and more like trivia: that it was written in a Mughal prison cell under siege by an actual army of monkeys, and that one of its lines, if you do the arithmetic, gives you the distance to the sun to a precision that would make NASA jealous.

Both claims get repeated constantly. Neither is quite what it's presented as. Working out what's actually going on with each one turns out to be a better story than the versions that circulate.

A saint, a prison, and a monkey uprising

The version of the origin story that travels best goes something like this: the Mughal emperor Akbar, having heard of a saint in his domains capable of miracles, summoned Tulsidas to his court, in some tellings at the Red Fort in Delhi, in others at Fatehpur Sikri. Akbar asked him to perform something wondrous, bring a stone idol to life, or free himself from chains. Tulsidas replied that he was a servant of Rama, not a magician, and had no tricks to offer. Furious, Akbar had him thrown in prison. Alone in his cell, Tulsidas turned to devotion, composing forty verses in praise of Hanuman over the following weeks. On the day he finished, or so the story goes, thousands of monkeys descended on the palace, tearing through gardens and chambers, throwing the court into chaos that no guard could stop. Akbar's ministers told him the monkeys had appeared the moment the saint began his prayers. Shaken, the emperor released Tulsidas and, in some versions, personally apologized.

It's a wonderful story, and it's worth being honest about what kind of story it is. Tulsidas and Akbar were real contemporaries. Tulsidas is generally dated to 1511 through 1623, and Akbar reigned from 1556 to 1605, so their lives did overlap by decades, and Tulsidas would have been a well-known figure in North India for much of Akbar's reign. Some traditions describe a real friendship between Tulsidas and Abdur Rahim Khan-i-Khanan, one of Akbar's most celebrated courtiers and a noted poet in his own right, and a Jaipur museum painting is sometimes cited as depicting a meeting between Tulsidas and Akbar on riverboats near Kashi. That much is at least plausible, and consistent with Akbar's well-documented interest in scholars and holy men of every tradition.

The prison and the monkey siege are a different matter. What's missing is any trace of the episode in the sources that would have been most likely to record it. Akbar's court chronicler, Abu'l-Fazl, produced the Akbarnama, an enormous and famously detailed record of the reign, down to court appointments and minor diplomatic visits. Neither it nor its companion volume, the Ain-i-Akbari, mentions Tulsidas at all, let alone an incident dramatic enough to involve an emperor publicly humbled by a monkey invasion of his own capital. For a chronicle this thorough, that silence is conspicuous. The earliest written traces of the imprisonment story appear to surface generations after both men had died, in devotional hagiographies rather than court records, the kind of literature whose purpose was never chronology so much as testimony to a saint's power. Asked directly whether there's real historical evidence Tulsidas was imprisoned by Akbar, one careful answer making the rounds online is refreshingly blunt: evidence that the two were contemporaries, yes; evidence for the imprisonment specifically, essentially none.

None of that makes the story worthless, and it isn't this piece's job to tell anyone their tradition is wrong. It's a distinction worth holding onto regardless: a legend can be centuries old, beloved, and completely absent from the historical record all at once. Most scholarly accounts place the Hanuman Chalisa's actual composition later in Tulsidas's life, in Varanasi, the city where he spent his final decades and where he wrote most of his major work, including the Ramcharitmanas, his monumental retelling of the Ramayana. A hymn to Hanuman written in the same accessible Awadhi, aimed at the same ordinary devotees rather than Sanskrit-trained elites, fits the pattern of everything else Tulsidas spent his life doing. It just doesn't come with a monkey army attached.

The verse that supposedly beat NASA to it

The second claim is stranger, because it isn't really about history at all. It's about a single line, verse eighteen of the forty, and a piece of arithmetic that's been circulating online for at least a couple of decades:

Yuga sahasra yojan par bhanu, leelyo tahi madhur phal jaanu
"The sun, at a distance of yuga-sahasra yojanas, you swallowed it, thinking it a sweet fruit."

The line refers to a well-known episode from the wider Ramayana tradition: Hanuman as an infant, seeing the rising sun and mistaking it for a piece of fruit, leaping up to grab it. It's one of the more charming and cosmically outsized images in the whole devotional canon, an infant god-child casually leaping toward a star. What turned it into an internet talking point is the phrase describing the distance: yuga sahasra yojan, "yuga-thousand-yojanas."

The calculation that circulates goes like this. A "yuga" here is read as the total length, in divine years, of a full cycle of the four ages of Hindu cosmology: Satya Yuga at 4,800 divine years, Treta at 3,600, Dvapara at 2,400, and Kali at 1,200, summing to 12,000. "Sahasra" simply means a thousand. A "yojana" is an ancient Indian unit of distance, commonly rounded in popular retellings to about eight miles. Multiply it out: 12,000 times 1,000 times 8 miles comes to 96,000,000 miles. The real average distance from the Earth to the sun, the figure NASA and every other space agency uses, is about 93,000,000 miles, or roughly 150 million kilometers. Ninety-six million against ninety-three million looks, at a glance, like a sixteenth-century Awadhi poet landing within a few percent of a distance nobody could have measured for another three hundred years.

Doing the math properly

Look closely and the calculation has three separate problems, and they're worth walking through individually, because each is doing different, quiet work in making the "prediction" look better than it is.

The first is the unit that's supposedly being measured. A yojana was never a fixed, standardized distance the way a mile or a kilometer is today. Ancient and medieval Indian sources across astronomy, geography, and religious literature use yojana values ranging from roughly four miles to roughly nine miles, depending on the era, the text, and the specific tradition of measurement being used. The eight-mile figure that makes the popular calculation come out close to the modern distance isn't a fixed ancient constant; it's one convenient value picked from a range wide enough to produce almost any answer you want. Run the same multiplication with a yojana of four miles and you get roughly 48 million miles, half the real distance. Run it with nine miles and you overshoot to 108 million. The "accuracy" of the popular version depends entirely on having already chosen, after the fact, the one yojana value that fits.

The second problem is that even the generously chosen version doesn't actually match as well as it's usually presented. Ninety-six million miles against the real 93 million is off by a bit over 3 percent, not an exact match, and the metric version that frequently accompanies the same email forward, multiplying out to roughly 153.6 million kilometers against a real distance of about 149.6 million, carries the same few-percent gap. A few percent is closer than most wild guesses would land, which is part of why the claim is fun to share. It is not the precision-instrument accuracy the framing usually implies.

The third problem is the more fundamental one, and it's less about the numbers than about what's being multiplied. "Yuga," in the derivation above, is a unit of time, twelve thousand divine years. "Yojana" is a unit of distance. Multiplying the two together and calling the result a distance only works if you quietly drop the units partway through and treat "12,000" as a bare number rather than a count of years. That's not a translation of the verse so much as a repurposing of its vocabulary: two culturally resonant, historically flexible numbers, multiplied together because the vocabulary was available, not because the verse was doing dimensional astronomy. Ancient Indian texts do contain a real, serious tradition of cosmological measurement, the same broader literature that describes the sun's own size in yojanas, that Wikipedia's own catalogue of numbers drawn from Hindu scriptures records values wildly larger and smaller than the modern distance depending on which text and which cosmological layer you're reading. The line in the Hanuman Chalisa was never positioned as an entry in that tradition. It's one descriptive phrase inside a devotional hymn, doing the same work "a million miles away" does in casual English: gesturing at something impossibly, cosmically far, not filing a coordinate.

What's actually going on here

Both stories, the prison and the monkeys, the yuga and the yojanas, follow the same shape. Something genuinely remarkable, a saint's devotional intensity, a poet's gift for cosmic imagery, gets reframed decades or centuries later as something more literal: a historical event with no contemporary record, a scientific measurement no one was trying to make. In both cases the reframing isn't malicious. It comes from the same impulse that makes any good story better with retelling, and from a reasonable desire to find hidden depths in a text people already love.

But the actual depths were never hidden, and they don't need the embellishment. A poet writing in the vernacular so that ordinary people, not just Sanskrit scholars, could access devotional literature, doing so with enough craft that his hymn is still recited from memory by millions of people four centuries later; that's a genuinely significant achievement, verifiable and well documented, with no prison cell or planetary distance required to make it interesting. The sun-swallowing image works the same way. It isn't a smuggled astronomy lesson. It's a description of a baby reaching for something impossibly out of reach and getting it anyway, which is, if you sit with it for a second, a pretty good description of what devotion is supposed to feel like in the first place.

This piece draws on Tulsidas's biographical dating as recorded in standard reference sources, historical assessments of the Akbar-imprisonment legend's absence from the Akbarnama and Ain-i-Akbari, Wikipedia's compiled catalogue of distance and time units in Hindu scriptures (including the four-to-nine-mile documented range for the yojana), and widely circulated popular breakdowns of the yuga-sahasra-yojana calculation, cross-checked against NASA's published Earth-sun distance figures.
