What If the Mitochondrion Was a Guest That Never Left?

Complex cells have no surviving intermediates and a genome written by two parents. Grant one merger between an archaeon and a bacterium, and see how much of that it explains.

Key takeaways

  • The leading account of how complex cells began is a single merger: an archaeon from the Asgard group hosting a bacterium that became the mitochondrion.
  • Asgard archaea carry genes once thought to belong only to eukaryotes, and two have been grown in the lab; the closest relatives sit inside or beside the Heimdallarchaeia, but no Asgard cell has been seen engulfing another.
  • The energy argument says mitochondria let cells afford large genomes by multiplying their energy-making membrane; it is contested, and the piece sets out both sides.
  • The membrane is the clearest unresolved problem: eukaryotic lipids are bacterial-type, while an archaeal host would have started with the other kind.

1. The Cell With No Ancestors

Put a smear of cells from the inside of your cheek under a microscope and the first thing you notice is a dark spot near the middle of each one. That spot is the nucleus, and it gives a whole branch of life its name. Eukaryote means “true kernel.” Every animal, plant, fungus and amoeba you have ever seen is built from cells that carry one.

Bacteria and archaea do not. For well over a billion years they were the only cells on Earth, and they were not idle. They colonised oceans and sediments, invented photosynthesis, fixed nitrogen from the air, and eventually filled the atmosphere with oxygen. Then, somewhere around two billion years ago, a different kind of cell appeared. It was larger, often by a factor of a thousand or more in volume, and it was furnished inside like a workshop: a nucleus holding the DNA behind a double membrane, a network of internal membranes passing proteins from station to station, a scaffold of filaments that let the cell change shape and crawl, and, scattered through the interior, mitochondria burning food with oxygen.

The trouble lies in what the record fails to contain. Ordinary evolution leaves intermediates behind, living relatives that show a trait half built. Eukaryotes offer none. Compare the genomes of animals, plants, fungi and the single-celled protists spread across the eukaryotic tree, and ask what their last common ancestor must have possessed. The answer, a cell biologists call LECA, was already complete. It had a nucleus, mitochondria, internal membranes, a cytoskeleton, and the genes for meiosis and sex. The complexity sits at the root of the tree, not along its branches. No half-built eukaryote survives to be studied.

For a time, it seemed some did. In the 1980s a group of single-celled eukaryotes that lacked mitochondria were taken for living fossils, cells caught before the great acquisition, and were given a name meaning “ancient.” Then the genomes came in. Each of them carried tiny leftover organelles, hydrogenosomes and mitosomes, that turned out to be stripped-down mitochondria. A gut-dwelling protist reported in 2016 has lost the organelle entirely, yet its ancestors had it. The apparent missing links were descendants that had given something up, not ancestors that had yet to gain it.

The genes tell a second story, and it is stranger. The machinery a eukaryotic cell uses to handle information, copying DNA, reading genes and building proteins, resembles the archaeal version. Much of its everyday metabolism resembles the bacterial one. A eukaryote reads like a document written by two authors who never met. For decades the textbook tree showed bacteria, archaea and eukaryotes as three branches of equal rank, which made the origin of complex cells look like a third, unexplained beginning. As more genomes were sequenced, the eukaryotes kept sliding closer to the archaea, until many trees placed them inside that group rather than beside it.

Two oddities, then. A cell with no surviving ancestors, and genes that cannot decide which of two parents they take after.

2. One Event, Granted

Suppose it happened once.

Suppose that at a single time and place, an archaeon and a bacterium entered a partnership so close that neither lived apart again. The archaeon was the host, drawn from the group now called Asgard archaea. The bacterium was a respiring one, from the lineage that gave rise to the alphaproteobacteria, and it came to live inside the archaeon and stayed. The host cell became the eukaryote. The guest became the mitochondrion. The nucleus, the internal membranes, the sexual cycle, all of it arrives afterwards, built by a cell that had been handed one extraordinary advantage.

That is the hypothesis, and it is not a fringe one. Some form of it is the leading account among people who work on the problem, and the evidence for its second half is sitting in every cell you own. A mitochondrion is wrapped in two membranes. It carries its own circular chromosome, much like a bacterium’s. Its ribosomes are of the bacterial type, and it multiplies by splitting in two, on its own schedule. Its genome has shrunk to almost nothing: the human version holds 37 genes in about 16,500 letters of DNA, yet the organelle works with over a thousand different proteins. Nearly all of those are encoded in the cell’s nucleus and shipped in. A bacterium with thousands of genes, it seems, handed most of them to its host over an immense stretch of time. When the mitochondrial genome is compared with bacterial ones, it falls on a branch of the alphaproteobacteria, or right beside it.

Symbiosis of this kind has also happened since, which keeps the idea from being a thought experiment. Chloroplasts, the green engines of plant cells, descend from a cyanobacterium taken in by an early eukaryote. A freshwater amoeba named Paulinella did the same thing independently, roughly a hundred million years ago, and its captured cyanobacterium is still partway through the same long surrender of genes to the host. The process is real, and it can be caught at different stages.

The first half of the hypothesis rests on the mosaic of genes from Chapter 1. If the host was an archaeon, then the eukaryote’s information machinery should look archaeal, and it does. The bacterial character of its metabolism is then simply what the guest brought along.

Much is left open on purpose. How the bacterium got inside is unsettled. It may have been engulfed, or it may have been wrapped by protrusions of a host that already lived beside it in a chemical partnership. The hypothesis does not need to choose. It asks only that a bacterium and an archaeon ended up sharing a single cell, once, and that the arrangement held.

The discipline for the rest of the piece is a strict one. One merger is allowed. Every other feature of the eukaryotic cell has to come out of that merger, or out of ordinary evolution acting on its consequences afterwards. If a second miracle is needed anywhere, the hypothesis owes an explanation for it.

3. The Host

The first Asgard genome came out of mud. In 2015 a team led from Uppsala took sediment from a seafloor core on the Arctic Mid-Ocean Ridge, about 15 kilometres from the Loki’s Castle vent field and more than three kilometres down, sequenced everything in it, and sorted the fragments into genomes. One of them belonged to an archaeon they named Lokiarchaeum. By every ordinary test it was an archaeon. It also carried genes that biologists had come to think of as eukaryotic property: relatives of actin, which builds the cell’s internal scaffold, small GTPases, the switches that steer traffic between membrane compartments, a ubiquitin-like tagging system, and parts of the ESCRT machinery that pinches membranes into new shapes. Its discoverers were careful about what that meant. The genome, one of them said afterwards, made the gap smaller without closing it.

Relatives followed, named for Norse gods in a running joke that stuck: Thor, Odin, Heimdall, and together the Asgard archaea. Their genomes repeated the pattern, some holding even more eukaryote-style genes than Lokiarchaeum had. A field that had spent decades without a candidate suddenly had a crowd of them.

A living cell arrived in 2020. After roughly twelve years of patient coaxing, researchers in Japan grew a member of the group from deep-sea sediment and named it Prometheoarchaeum. The cell is tiny, about half a micron across, and it grows slowly, feeding on amino acids with the help of partner microbes that consume the hydrogen it releases. It has no nucleus and no internal membranes. Its surface sprouts long, branching protrusions, and it was never seen swallowing anything. It seemed to hold its partners in a tangle of arms.

A second cultured relative followed. In 2022 a team in Vienna and Zurich reported a highly enriched culture of Lokiarchaeum ossiferum, grown from shallow sediment and dividing every one to two weeks. Cryo-electron tomography showed round cells with branched protrusions and a single-membrane envelope, and a network of actin-like filaments running through the cell body and out into the protrusions. The authors proposed that an elaborate actin-based cytoskeleton existed before the first eukaryotes did.

Which Asgard lineage sits closest to us is a question of branches. A 2023 Nature study added 63 genomes from eleven sites and placed eukaryotes as a clade nested inside the Asgard archaea, sister to the Hodarchaeales, an order within the Heimdallarchaeia. It also found that Asgard genomes grew by gaining many duplicated genes and losing few, a pattern that resembles how eukaryotic genomes expanded, and that the lineage leading to eukaryotes had moved from hot water into milder conditions and a lifestyle based on eating organic matter. A 2025 reanalysis, correcting for genomes that turned out to be chimeras of Asgard and TACK sequences, put eukaryotes slightly deeper, as sister to the whole Heimdallarchaeia group. The two answers differ by one branch. Both place the host in the same neighbourhood.

The closest relatives were seen as cells only recently. In 2025 a Danish team labelled Hodarchaeales in Aarhus Bay sediment and imaged them under super-resolution microscopy. The cells had an elongated body joined to a rounded swelling at one end, with the DNA gathered in a compact central region. The authors read that shape as a hint of eukaryote-like organisation, and they were careful to say so as a hint. The group had not been grown in culture when the study appeared, so most of what is known about it still comes from DNA.

The host, then, is no abstraction. It is a living group of archaea, with the right ingredients in its genome, a cultured cousin that reaches out with protrusions, and a nearest relative that is only now coming into view.

4. The Partnership

Two cells do not merge for the sake of it. Something has to hold them together long enough for the arrangement to become permanent, and the hydrogen hypothesis names what: a trade in gas.

Picture a seafloor with little oxygen. A bacterium that ferments its food releases hydrogen and carbon dioxide as waste. A hydrogen-eating archaeon takes up both and builds its own biomass from them. When hydrogen piles up, the bacterium’s fermentation slows. When it is whisked away, the bacterium thrives. The two have every reason to live shoulder to shoulder, since a gas spreads out fast and the nearest consumer catches the most. Partnerships of this kind, called syntrophy, are ordinary in oxygen-poor sediments. Methane-oxidising archaea and sulfate-reducing bacteria grow in tight aggregates there, trading chemistry across a few micrometres.

William Martin and Miklós Müller proposed in 1998 that this arrangement was the seed of the eukaryotic cell. The host was an archaeon that lived on hydrogen. The guest was a bacterium that produced it, one able to live with or without oxygen. Over time the closeness tightened, the host wrapped itself around its partner, and the partner ended up inside, still feeding hydrogen to its host. Oxygen began to build up in the air around 2.4 billion years ago, yet much seawater stayed low in oxygen for a very long time afterwards, so low-oxygen mud beside a slowly oxygenating world was available for a long stretch of Earth’s history.

The cultured Asgard cell supplied a missing piece of choreography. Its protrusions, and its dependence on hydrogen-consuming partners, led to a model with three verbs: entangle, engulf, endogenise. Partners are caught in the host’s arms, enclosed as the protrusions fuse, and then kept. A 2025 phylogenomic study reported results in support of the hydrogen hypothesis, in which a hydrogen-consuming archaeal host fused with a hydrogen-producing protomitochondrion.

A trade partner also settles a puzzle that the older picture of one cell swallowing another as prey could not. Why would a meal survive? A guest that has already been supplying its host for generations is no meal. Neither side has anything to digest, and each already depends on the other.

Once the guest lived inside, a slow transfer of authority began. Whenever a guest cell broke open, its DNA could be pasted into the host’s chromosomes, and over a very long time genes moved from the guest’s small genome into the host’s nucleus. That is why the human mitochondrion, with 37 genes of its own, relies on more than a thousand proteins encoded elsewhere. The guest gave up independence a gene at a time.

5. The Energy Argument

A bacterium makes its energy at its skin. The machinery that turns food into ATP sits in the cell membrane, so a bacterium’s power supply grows with its surface area, while its needs grow with its volume. Make the cell bigger and the surface falls behind. Make the genome bigger and each extra gene has to be paid for out of the same limited budget. A typical bacterium holds a few thousand genes. E. coli has about 4,300. The human genome holds about 20,000 protein-coding genes, in cells that are far larger.

In 2010 Nick Lane and William Martin argued that the mitochondrion is what broke this ceiling. A eukaryotic cell does not have to rely on its outer membrane. It carries hundreds or thousands of mitochondria, each with a deeply folded inner membrane, so the surface area for making ATP grows in step with the cell’s volume. Oxygen is not the point of the argument, since many mitochondria work without it and plenty of free-living bacteria breathe it. The point is where the membrane sits, and that it can be multiplied. Each mitochondrion keeps only a handful of genes of its own, so adding more energy-making membrane never means copying a whole genome again. By their calculation, the average eukaryotic gene commands some 200,000 times more energy than the average prokaryotic one. A budget that large is the sort of thing that lets a genome swell, a cell grow, and a cytoskeleton, a nucleus and an elaborate membrane system become affordable.

The argument has been contested. In 2015 Michael Lynch and Georgi Marinov calculated that the lifetime cost of a gene is small beside the cost of running the rest of a cell, and concluded that no energetic barrier needs to be invoked. Lane and Martin answered that their argument concerned supply, the energy available per gene, which depends on membrane area, and not the demand that Lynch and Marinov had measured. The two sides still disagree about how tight the constraint was. The hypothesis in this piece does not rest on the number alone.

What the argument does is turn Chapter 1 around. A cell with no ancestors looked like a paradox when the nucleus and the internal membranes were treated as the starting point. If the merger came first, they are consequences. The first cell carrying a mitochondrion was not completing a half-finished design. It was operating on an energy budget no prokaryote had ever had, and a nucleus, an endomembrane system and a sexual cycle were things that budget could pay for. How quickly it all followed is unknown, and the hypothesis does not need it to have been fast.

6. What Else It Explains

The hypothesis was built to account for the two-author genome of Chapter 1, and a merger predicts that signature: informational genes from the host, a flood of metabolic genes from the guest. It also predicts mess, and the genes deliver it. A substantial share of the bacterial-type genes in eukaryotes trace to bacteria other than the alphaproteobacterial lineage, which points to gene traffic with other bacteria before and after the main event.

The cell’s toolkit comes next. The first Asgard genome was described as a ready set of parts for cellular complexity, and the cultured cells show why. Asgard relatives carry actin-like filaments, membrane-shaping ESCRT proteins and small GTPases that steer vesicles. A cell with those parts did not have to invent the scaffolding and traffic system of the eukaryote from nothing. It needed an energy budget to run them at scale, and Chapter 5 supplied one.

The nucleus has the boldest explanation. In 2006 William Martin and Eugene Koonin proposed that the guest’s arrival carried a hazard. Bacteria, including relatives of the mitochondrial ancestor, carry self-splicing genetic elements called group II introns. Released into the host’s chromosomes when guests broke open, these would have jumped to hundreds of positions in the genome. Each had to be cut out of the message before it was read, but a ribosome that started reading too early would build a garbled protein. Walling the genome off in a nucleus, so that splicing could finish before translation began, was the cure, and the spliceosome, the cell’s splicing machine, descended from the invaders themselves. The resemblance behind the idea is real, since nuclear introns are removed by the same branched-lariat chemistry that group II introns use. The model has critics, who point out that no group II introns have been found in sequenced nuclear genomes, and that observed intron invasions in archaea spread more slowly than the model needs. It remains a hypothesis inside a hypothesis, but it connects the guest to the nucleus by a mechanism rather than by a gesture.

The merger also explains an odd rule. Every eukaryote has a mitochondrion or the remains of one. The leftover organelles in the supposedly ancient cells of Chapter 1 still assemble iron-sulfur clusters, small metal cofactors that many proteins need, and a cell that loses the organelle has to find another way to make them. The one organism known to have lost it entirely got by with a bacterial iron-sulfur system acquired by gene transfer. If the guest was there from the beginning, no lineage of eukaryotes was ever without one, and the rule falls out of the story.

The last thing it explains is the question of why complex life arose once. A merger of two unrelated cells into a stable, heritable partnership is a rare event, and nothing in the hypothesis asks it to have happened twice. It also accounts for the completeness at the root of the tree. The last common ancestor of all eukaryotes was already a full cell because everything with a nucleus descends from one successful partnership. Other partnerships may have formed and failed, and others may have succeeded and left no descendants. The record cannot say.

7. The Open Edges

Several pieces of the story are unsettled, and a few of them are large.

The host’s exact place on the tree is one. Two careful analyses put eukaryotes within the Asgard archaea and differ over one branch, Hodarchaeales or the wider Heimdallarchaeia, and the difference traces partly to genomes that proved to be chimeras. More genomes, and above all more cultured cells, are the way through. The closest relatives are still known mainly from DNA.

The entry of the guest is another. No Asgard cell has been seen swallowing anything. The entangle, engulf, endogenise model is built from the behaviour of cultured cells and from reasoning, and the protrusions that inspired it are a suggestion, not a demonstration. The cultured cells so far show no visible organelles and no nucleus, which fits a partnership that came first and a complex cell that followed. Rival schools, in which the host was already a complex cell before the mitochondrion arrived, are still argued.

The clearest candidate for a second miracle is the membrane. Eukaryotic membranes are built from bacterial-type lipids, fatty acids joined to glycerol by ester links, while archaeal membranes use isoprenoid ether lipids. If the host was an archaeon, its membrane was replaced by a bacterial-type one somewhere along the road. The guest could have supplied the genes, and the old divide between the two lipid systems now looks less clean than it once did: some bacteria carry archaeal lipid genes, and Asgard genomes carry a mixture. But no living cell shows the swap half done, and an earlier phylogenomic survey did not find compelling support for tracing the eukaryotic lipid genes back through an archaeal host. The hypothesis owes an account here, and it does not have a finished one.

Two smaller edges remain. The precise position of the mitochondrial ancestor among the alphaproteobacteria is debated, and the date of the merger is loose, with published estimates spanning roughly 1.6 to 2.2 billion years ago.

What would settle matters is a short list. A Hodarchaeales cell grown in culture and imaged from the inside. An Asgard cell caught in the act of enclosing a partner. An organism with a membrane caught between archaeal and bacterial types. Asgard research has produced a major result every few years since 2015, so none of these looks out of reach.

On present evidence, the single merger accounts for the mixed ancestry of the genome, the missing intermediates, the universal presence of mitochondria or their remains, the cell’s starter set of parts and a plausible route to the nucleus, at the price of one granted event, and the membrane is the one place where it still needs help. That is what a best explanation means: the account that covers the most of the facts with the fewest additions. It stays open to replacement by any account that covers more.
