What If Life Began Inside a Seafloor Chimney?

Life needs a steady supply of energy, and a pond gives it in bursts. Grant that life began inside the walls of an alkaline vent, where a rock makes hydrogen and the ocean supplies a gradient, and see how far that carries.

Key takeaways

  • The hypothesis proposes that life began in the porous walls of alkaline hydrothermal vents, where a hydrogen supply made by rock meets an acidic ocean across a natural proton gradient.
  • Lost City, found in 2000, shows that such vents exist and run for tens of thousands of years; laboratory experiments have made formate, acetate and pyruvate from hydrogen and carbon dioxide over vent minerals, though in small amounts.
  • The deepest ancestor reconstructed from genomes appears to have lived on the same chemistry, as an anaerobic acetogen about 4.2 billion years ago.
  • It is an account of the energy engine, not of the genetic code, and critics doubt that a natural gradient could drive chemistry this simply.

1. The Engine Problem

A living cell is a continuous effort. In a dead cell chemistry runs downhill and stops. In a living one it is pushed uphill, building proteins, copying DNA, and keeping salts on the right side of a membrane, and the pushing needs a steady supply of energy. Cut the supply and the cell does not slowly fade. It fails as a system.

Life has solved the supply problem in one way almost everywhere. Cells charge their membranes. A pump drives protons across a membrane, the membrane holds them back, and when they flow home through a molecular turbine called ATP synthase, the flow is used to make ATP. Peter Mitchell worked out this arrangement in the 1960s, and it turns up in bacteria, archaea, plants and animals alike. The mitochondria of the first What If piece run on it. Something so widely shared is very likely old.

Any account of life’s origin has to say where the first energy came from, where the first carbon came from, and what held the first cell together. Darwin imagined a warm little pond. In 1953 Stanley Miller showed that sparks in a flask of simple gases make amino acids. Later came the RNA world, the idea that a single molecule could once have been both gene and catalyst. These accounts are strong on information, on how something that copies itself might arise. Their defenders point to ultraviolet light and cycles of wetting and drying as ways of driving the chemistry. A pond still delivers energy in bursts, though, and a cell needs it without interruption.

The hypothesis in this piece starts from the other end. It asks where on the young Earth a steady and free supply of chemical energy existed, and it finds a candidate in a rock.

2. One Setting, Granted

Suppose life did not begin in a pond. Suppose it began in the wall of a chimney on the floor of an ancient ocean, inside the fine network of pores that such chimneys contain, at a place where the rock beneath was already making hydrogen and the sea above was already carrying carbon dioxide.

The setting is an alkaline hydrothermal vent. Warm, alkaline fluid rich in hydrogen rises from the crust and meets an ocean that was probably acidic, thought to be near pH 6, loaded with dissolved carbon dioxide and iron. Where they meet, minerals precipitate into a porous chimney whose walls are thin, full of metal, and a natural boundary between two very different liquids.

The hypothesis then makes a claim about order. Energy came first. The earliest metabolism was the reaction of hydrogen with carbon dioxide, helped by metal minerals in the wall and driven by the natural gradient across it. The first cells were pores, mineral compartments whose walls already held a gradient, and not floating bags. Free-living cells with their own fatty membranes and their own pumps came later and left the rock.

This is the proposal that Michael Russell, Nick Lane, William Martin and their colleagues have developed over three decades. It also follows on from the first What If piece, in which the host archaeon lived on hydrogen. This piece asks where that way of living could have come from.

The same discipline applies as before. One setting is granted. Every other feature of early life has to come out of that setting and ordinary chemistry, or the hypothesis owes an explanation.

3. A Place That Was Predicted

Hydrothermal vents entered biology in 1977, when the submersible Alvin found hot springs on the Galápagos rift crowded with animals living on chemistry. Jack Corliss, a geologist on that first dive, thought at once of the origin of life. Those vents were black smokers, driven by volcanic heat, blasting out fluid at several hundred degrees, acidic and loaded with metal sulfides.

Michael Russell argued from ancient mineral deposits that a different kind of vent should exist, cooler, alkaline and built of carbonate rather than sulfide. Nobody had seen one. In December 2000 a research cruise on the vessel Atlantis found it by accident, on a seafloor mountain called the Atlantis Massif, about 15 kilometres from the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. The expedition had not set out to look for a vent field. They named it Lost City.

It is a field of white carbonate chimneys. The largest, Poseidon, stands about 60 metres tall, the height of an 18-storey building. The fluid inside is warm, roughly 40 to 90 degrees Celsius, and strongly alkaline, between pH 9 and 11. No magma drives it. The heat and the chemistry come from serpentinization, in which seawater seeps into mantle rock, converts the mineral olivine to serpentine, and releases heat, hydrogen and methane. The microbes there live on that hydrogen and methane, and the field has been active for tens of thousands of years, older than any known black smoker.

Lost City is a modern place, not a window into the Hadean. Its ocean is mildly alkaline rather than acidic, and its fluids carry very little carbon dioxide. The claim is narrower. It shows that the basic arrangement is real: a rock that makes hydrogen without a volcano, warm alkaline fluid, and porous chimneys, all at a site that keeps running for millennia. Early Earth had more exposed mantle rock than today, so serpentinizing vents were probably common.

4. The Gradient

Look at a Lost City chimney wall and you see two worlds divided by a thin film of mineral. On one side is vent fluid, alkaline and rich in hydrogen. On the other is seawater. In the early ocean of the hypothesis that water was acidic and rich in carbon dioxide.

Each unit of pH is a tenfold difference in the concentration of protons. A difference of four or five units across a thin mineral wall is therefore a difference of ten thousand to a hundred thousand times. A cell has to build pumps and keep a membrane intact to hold a gradient of that kind. The vent offers it for free. Nick Lane has likened a cell’s use of a gradient to a hydroelectric dam, and in the vent the geology supplies the water behind the dam for free.

Experiments have started to test whether the gradient can do work. In 2020 Reuben Hudson, Victor Sojo and colleagues built a small Y-shaped reactor. One channel carried an alkaline fluid, pushed through by hydrogen gas, at pH 12.3. The other carried an acidic fluid, pushed through by carbon dioxide, at pH 3.9. Where the two met they precipitated a thin film of iron-nickel sulfide, under ten micrometres thick, as a stand-in for the chimney wall. At room temperature and modest pressure the reactor made formate, the simplest product of reducing carbon dioxide, and carbon-13 labelling confirmed that it came from the gas. When the experimenters shrank the gradient, removed the hydrogen, or left out the mineral film, they detected nothing.

The yields were tiny, around a millionth of a mole per litre, so the result is a demonstration of principle and not of productivity. It still matters. Making formate from carbon dioxide and hydrogen is the first uphill step of the pathway that follows, and it is hard to achieve under mild conditions. The gradient is what made it go.

5. Chemistry That Looks Like Metabolism

The reaction of hydrogen with carbon dioxide is the heart of the hypothesis, and it is also the oldest known biological route for fixing carbon. Called the acetyl-CoA pathway, or the Wood-Ljungdahl pathway, it is used today by acetogenic bacteria and methanogenic archaea, and it is regarded as the only carbon-fixing route that also releases energy. It takes hydrogen and carbon dioxide and builds formate, then acetate, then pyruvate, a three-carbon molecule at the crossroads of metabolism.

In 2020 Martina Preiner, Joseph Moran, William Martin and their colleagues, working in three laboratories in different countries, asked whether minerals could do the same without cells. They put hydrogen and carbon dioxide in alkaline water at 100 degrees Celsius with three minerals found in alkaline vents, the nickel-iron alloy awaruite, magnetite and greigite. All three made formate, acetate and pyruvate overnight. The yields climbed down the ladder: formate up to 200 millimolar, acetate up to 100 micromolar, pyruvate up to 10 micromolar, with some methanol and methane. The products are the same steps that the living pathway takes.

The enzymes of the living pathway hold a clue. They are built around metal clusters. The central carbon-fixing enzyme carries a nickel-iron site, and an enzyme in methanogens that handles the first step holds 46 iron-sulfur clusters. Those clusters resemble the structure of small pieces of mineral such as greigite. One reading is that the earliest catalysts were the rock itself and that proteins later wrapped themselves around fragments of it. A cell’s enzyme, on this view, is a mineral catalyst that has been kept and improved.

6. The Ancestor That Fits

If life began on hydrogen and carbon dioxide, the deepest ancestor of living cells should still bear the trace. In 2016 a study of gene families shared by bacteria and archaea reached a picture of the last universal common ancestor as a hydrogen-using, carbon-fixing anaerobe. In 2024 a larger analysis led from Bristol, with Nick Lane among the authors, dated that ancestor to about 4.2 billion years ago, a few hundred million years after the Earth formed. It described a prokaryote-grade anaerobic acetogen, part of an early ecosystem, with a genome of at least 2.5 million letters encoding about 2,600 proteins. An acetogen runs on the acetyl-CoA pathway, the route of the previous chapter.

The hypothesis also has an idea about the split between archaea and bacteria. The two domains differ in their membrane lipids and in several basic enzymes. In 2012 Nick Lane and William Martin proposed that this is what we should see if the last common ancestor lived inside the rock without a free-living membrane of its own, and that bacteria and archaea escaped the chimney separately and each built their own. The deep divide in cell membranes is the open edge on which the first What If piece ended. On this proposal it appears as a feature of the escape.

The match between the oldest ancestor and the vent chemistry is what the hypothesis predicts. It is not proof. Hydrogen-using acetogens can live in many places and the pathway may have been invented once and used everywhere. The match does show that the proposal is consistent with what genomes say about the roots of the tree.

7. The Open Edges

The hypothesis has serious critics, and the criticisms are specific. In 2016 the biochemist J. Baz Jackson argued that natural pH gradients across thin mineral walls were unlikely to have driven chemistry at the origin of life. He noted that there is no evidence yet for thin mineral membranes holding sharp gradients in modern Lost City vents, and that a primitive machine that could harvest such a gradient has not been shown. Lost City itself is also short of dissolved carbon dioxide, so the early ocean has to supply what the modern one does not.

The laboratory yields are small. Formate in the micromolar range and pyruvate at ten micromolar are far from the conditions a growing system would need, and no experiment has yet produced a self-sustaining chemical cycle inside a mineral pore.

The reconstruction of the last common ancestor adds a second problem. A cell of 2,600 proteins is no beginner. Between the first hydrogen chemistry and that ancestor lies a road that includes a genetic code, a ribosome and a system for copying genes. The vent hypothesis is an account of the engine. It does not yet say how the code arose. Researchers who start with information, such as John Sutherland’s group, reply that ultraviolet-driven chemistry in shallow water produces the building blocks of RNA, and the two schools may turn out to describe different stages.

What would settle matters is a short list. Self-assembling, self-sustaining chemistry inside a mineral pore with a maintained gradient. Evidence of the first steps of the pathway from a modern serpentinizing vent. A model of how a mineral catalyst turned into a protein enzyme. The question also reaches beyond Earth, since the Cassini spacecraft detected molecular hydrogen in the plume of Saturn’s moon Enceladus, which suggests a rocky seafloor that is making it.

On present evidence the hypothesis accounts for a free and continuous energy supply, a source of carbon, a set of reactions that match the oldest metabolism, a place that fits the genomic picture of the last common ancestor, and a reason why archaea and bacteria differ so much. It leaves the genetic code and the transition from pore to cell unexplained. That is a large gap, and it is the best reason to keep testing the idea and not to declare it settled.
