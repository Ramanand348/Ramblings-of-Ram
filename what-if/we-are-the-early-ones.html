What If We Are the Early Ones?

The sky looks empty, and the usual reading is that something is wrong. Grant a different one: the universe is young, expanding civilizations are real, and we have simply arrived before them.

Key takeaways

  • The hypothesis proposes that fast-expanding civilizations are real, and that they set a deadline: advanced life that appears after their territories arrive can no longer see an empty sky.
  • It explains why we seem early. Hard-steps arithmetic says advanced life should mostly appear far in the future, and a deadline makes today the typical time to stand in unclaimed sky.
  • The source model’s fit says such civilizations expand at more than about half the speed of light, already hold 40 to 50 percent of the universe’s volume, and could reach us in roughly 200 million to 2 billion years.
  • The price is that quiet civilizations must be very rare, which is bad news for radio searches. The model is deliberately simple, rests on a hard-step count of about six, and assumes expanders exist at all.

1. The Lunch Question

In the summer of 1950, a handful of physicists walked to lunch at Los Alamos, joking about a cartoon that blamed flying saucers for the vanishing of New York’s public trash cans. Enrico Fermi, Edward Teller, Emil Konopinski and Herbert York talked about it on the way, and the talk moved on to other things. Partway through the meal Fermi asked where everybody was. The others understood him at once, and laughed. That is the story as Eric Jones pieced it together decades later from the memories of the people at the table, and like most good origin stories it has probably been polished by retelling. The question has needed no polish.

The arithmetic behind it is short. The Milky Way holds a few hundred billion stars, and many of them are billions of years older than the Sun. Suppose one of those stars gave rise to a civilization that built ships, sent them to the nearest stars at a mere one percent of the speed of light, and let each new colony do the same. The wave would cross the galaxy, a hundred thousand light years wide, in about ten million years. Allow generous pauses at every stop and the answer stretches to tens of millions. The galaxy has had more than a thousand times that long to get the job done.

Yet the sky looks like a place nobody has touched. Telescopes have found no engineered stars, no strangely dimmed galaxies, no relic probes parked in the solar system, and decades of radio and optical listening have returned nothing that holds up. The listening has covered only a small corner of the possible frequencies, directions and timings, which is a fair reason for patience. The expansion argument is harder to patch, because a civilization that spreads changes the look of everything it reaches, and the changes grow with its reach.

Seventy years of answers have fallen into a few families. Life is rarer than it looks. Civilizations destroy themselves. Civilizations choose to hide, or to leave young species alone. Each of these is a story about what is wrong with the sky. The one followed here asks what the sky would look like if nothing were wrong with it at all, and the quiet were simply what an early universe sounds like. Suppose that is true. How far does it carry?

2. Early

The universe is 13.8 billion years old, which sounds like late in the story until the schedule ahead is laid beside it. A star like the Sun lives about ten billion years. Red dwarfs, the small dim stars that make up most of the galaxy’s population, burn their fuel so slowly that the longest-lived will shine for trillions of years, some of them two thousand times longer than the Sun. About 95 percent of all stars outlast the Sun, and only about 8 percent of the interstellar gas has so far been turned into stars.

Set the present against that horizon and we are standing a percent or two of the way into the time that stars will be available. Drop a pin at random on a timeline of every planet that could ever host observers, and the pin almost never lands here.

That reasoning leans on planets around red dwarfs being able to hold life, and the point is disputed. Young red dwarfs flare violently, and planets close enough to stay warm may be tidally locked, one face turned permanently toward the star. Nobody yet knows whether those problems are fatal or manageable. Hanson and his coauthors find that favoring heavier stars by large factors is not enough to remove the puzzle. The dwarfs would have to be struck from the list almost entirely, and ocean worlds, which sidestep most of the usual objections, make that a hard case to win.

Earth’s own clock reads differently. The Sun brightens slowly, and the oceans have perhaps another billion years, give or take, before the heat becomes too much. By the planet’s reckoning we arrived near the end of the stay. By the universe’s reckoning the doors have barely opened. Whether that second reading is surprising depends on how hard the road to this point was, because the harder the road, the more the odds tilt toward arriving at the very end of whatever time is allowed.

3. Doors That Are Hard to Open

Brandon Carter noticed something odd in 1983. The Sun will stay stable for about ten billion years, and Earth took about four and a half billion to produce anyone able to ask about it. Two durations of similar size looked like a coincidence. Carter’s way out was to assume that the process is usually far slower than a star’s lifetime. Then the planets that produce observers are the lucky few that finished in time, and the lucky finishers spread their milestones roughly evenly across the window they were given. The resemblance between the two timescales stops being a coincidence and becomes a selection effect.

Earth’s record has the look of such a sequence. Life appeared early, though some read that speed as a sign the first step was easy. Complex cells built around a mitochondrion came from what looks like a single merger, roughly two billion years later, the subject of an earlier piece in this series. Animals followed after another stretch of more than a billion years. Language and technology arrived in the last instant. Each looks like a door that stayed shut for ages and then opened once. The number of such doors is a fitted quantity, not a census. Estimates from Earth history fall roughly between three and nine, and the model takes six as its middle value, with three to twelve as the plausible range.

Improbable steps have an arithmetic of their own. If several steps must happen in order, and each is so unlikely that it will almost surely not happen in the time available, then the chance of finishing all of them by time t grows as t raised to the power of the number of steps. With six steps, a planet granted twice as long is about sixty-four times as likely to produce an observer. Success is back-loaded, and heavily. Of all the planets that ever manage it, almost all will manage it near the end of whatever time they were allowed.

That is the second half of the earliness problem. Planets around long-lived dwarfs are allowed trillions of years, so observers should cluster very late. Run through the full accounting of star births and planet lifetimes, and the model puts more than 99 percent of all advanced life after today, unless the steps number fewer than three and planets cannot stay habitable beyond about fifty billion years. We turned up at 13.8 billion. Unless something shortened the allowed time for everyone, we are very unlikely to be here. Time does not shorten itself. A deadline needs an author.

4. The Deadline

Suppose that somewhere in the universe a civilization learns to expand, and keeps expanding at a steady fraction of the speed of light. From that moment the sky around it divides in two. Inside the growing sphere, the stars belong to the expanders. Outside, they still belong to no one. A young species that wakes up inside the sphere finds a settled neighborhood and knows it at once, so the model counts it out of the sample. Only species that wake up outside, in unclaimed sky, get to look around and see nothing.

That is the whole mechanism. Once one expander exists, every later arrival is on a clock, and each further expander shortens it. Among all the places where advanced life could have appeared, the ones that see an empty heaven are the ones that got there before the spheres arrived. We see an empty heaven. So we are among the early ones, and our earliness stops being a surprise. It is what the situation selects for.

This is the deadline that the hard-steps arithmetic of chapter 3 was missing. The power law that pushes success toward the end of a long window has no long window to work with, because the window closes when the expanders fill the sky.

Two features keep the explanation from being a convenient story. It asks nothing about how life gets started or what shape intelligence takes. The deadline applies whether the first mind in some corner arose from ocean-world chemistry or from anything else, so the objections about flares and tidal locking lose their grip. And it makes a claim that can be checked. A volume held by expanders should look different from empty space, and at a typical age it would span a patch of sky much larger than the full Moon.

The model keeps its definition tight. An expander expands at a common speed, visibly changes what it holds, is born according to the hard-steps power law except inside someone else’s territory, and does not die unless another expander displaces it. That definition rules out the zoo hypothesis by construction, since a civilization that lets others arise inside its territory would not set a deadline.

5. The Expanders

Why would anyone keep expanding? The model does not need anyone to want to. On Earth, species, cultures and organizations that move into unoccupied territory tend to spread, and the habits that favor spreading get selected for over repeated rounds of expansion. Individual motives matter little. A civilization that moves by stopping at local resources, developing them for a while and then reaching for the next set of stars keeps a steady pace measured against the materials around it. The stretching of space does not slow that pace, much as a plane’s speed is set against the air and not against the runway it left.

Across distances that large, nobody can coordinate, and that helps. Uncoordinated local processes tend to add up to smooth, steady trends, the way diseases move through populations and innovations through communities. A process that halts in one place rarely halts everywhere. Most civilizations may never try. The model needs only one in some large number to do it, because a few exceptions can claim a disproportionate share of the sky.

How fast do they go? The sky answers through its silence. If expanders crept along at a tenth of the speed of light, their territories would already be large and visible, and the civilizations among them would have seen each other long ago. Near light speed a selection effect takes over. A spot in the sky from which we could see an expander’s territory would already be inside it, so the places where we could look and see one are the places where we would not be standing. Speeds below about a third of light speed are greatly disfavored by that logic, and the source model’s conclusion settles on more than half.

That gives the model its third parameter, and with the hard-steps power and our own date as the other two, each is pinned down to within roughly a factor of four. It is a small machine, and it can be run forward.

6. The Map

Put the three parameters together and the model draws a sky. On average, at the dates when expanders first appear, a third to a half of the universe already lies inside expander territory. The estimate for today is 40 to 50 percent of all the volume there is. Each expander will eventually hold somewhere between about a hundred thousand and thirty million galaxies, out of a universe that carries roughly 70 million galaxies in every cubic gigaparsec.

They are not here yet. The nearest frontier is far off, and the estimate for when our own descendants would meet one runs from about 200 million years to about 2 billion. In the worked example with expansion at half of light speed and six hard steps, a quarter of civilizations like ours meet a first neighbor within about half a billion years, and three quarters within about two billion. Those are long spans by any human clock. Against the trillion-year schedule of chapter 2 they are a short stretch.

The silence overhead then has two readings. We may still be outside every territory, with the light from the nearest ones not yet arrived, which is the reading the earlier chapters have followed. Or the territories look only subtly different from empty space in ways nobody has yet noticed. In that case the model predicts long circular arcs in the sky, the borders where one territory meets unclaimed space, and a patient search of survey data could look for them.

7. What the Quiet Means

The model that explains the silence comes with a price. If expanders set the deadline, then civilizations that stay home must be very rare, or the neighborhood would be full of them. At the middle estimate of six hard steps, and assuming each quiet civilization lasts a million years, expecting even one other active quiet civilization in our galaxy right now takes more than ten million of them for every expander. Expecting one that has ever existed in our galaxy takes more than ten thousand. Expecting one anywhere in our past light cone takes more than a thousand. Put another way, for there to have been even one other in the Milky Way’s history, the chance that a quiet civilization becomes an expander would have to be below about one in ten thousand.

The authors call that bad news for radio searches, and it is. It is also why the quiet needs no further explanation. A sparse, young neighborhood looks like this. One door stays open: signals that outlast their makers, such as beacons, are far easier to catch than civilizations that have to be listening at the right moment.

For us the picture contains a clock. We are one of the early civilizations in a universe that is filling up, with the nearest expanding frontier somewhere between a few hundred million and a couple of billion years away. Whether our descendants ever become expanders is left open. The authors note that it may be desirable for them not to, and that our date counts as a data point either way, in the way that a guess about the gravity on an unfamiliar planet, made from how things seem to fall around you, tells you something even if you never jump.

The model is simple on purpose, and the authors say it is probably oversimplified. It has three parameters, each estimated only to within about a factor of four. It assumes that expanders exist at all. The hard-step count, the speed and the red dwarf question are inferred, not measured, and a different answer to any of them changes the map. What the model offers is one coherent story in which nothing is wrong with the sky.

What if we are the early ones? Then the quiet overhead is the sound of a young universe, the long gap before the frontiers of the expanders reach the places where the rest of us are waking up. We are early, the sky is mostly still free of neighbors, and the first of them are hundreds of millions of years away.

Sources and Evidence

The sources are grouped by how directly they bear on the claim: the model itself, the hard-steps argument, the habitability of long-lived stars, clues from Earth’s own history, and the history of the question. The source model calls the civilizations it describes grabby; this piece calls them expanders. The three interactive drawings were made for this piece and are schematic. The clock redraws the appearance model of the main source with the parameters that paper states, and the figures quoted in the text, such as the 40 to 50 percent of volume and the 200 million to 2 billion year meeting window, are taken from it.
